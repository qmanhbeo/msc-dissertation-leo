\begin{abstract}
This study introduces a measurement protocol that separates research-policy divergence into two separable dimensions, coverage (which SDGs each corpus prioritises) and within-SDG semantic framing (how differently the same SDG is discussed), and stress-tests the instrument against its principal measurement choices, assignment method, policy source family, segment cap, embedding encoder, and register adjustment. Shared SDG labels do not necessarily imply shared framing: existing bibliometric studies map AI research to SDGs using keyword or classification methods \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}, but rarely compare the semantic content of research and policy discourse. This study uses Sentence-BERT embeddings and a supervised logistic regression classifier (test macro-F1 = \MacroFOne) to map semantic distance between \NResearchPapers{} AI-for-sustainability abstracts and \NPolicySegments{} segments from \NPolicyDocs{} institutional SDG policy sources. The analysis separates coverage gaps from within-SDG semantic gaps. Because the corpora differ in register (\citeauthor{biberConrad2009register}, \citeyear{biberConrad2009register}), these gaps are observed textual-semantic divergence, not substantive disagreement.

The central finding is a cancellation: the raw coverage-vs-semantic-gap correlation is near-zero ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, $p = 0.765$) not because coverage and framing are independent, but because two opposing signals cancel — topic divergence rises with coverage divergence ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic = 0.48$, $p = 0.054$) while register divergence falls with it ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister = -0.49$, $p = 0.045$), and the two sum to zero. A register decomposition using Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP) separates the raw centroid distance into a topic component (adjusted gap, mean \MeanAdjustedGap{}) and a register component (mean \MeanRegisterComponent{}). After register removal, the adjusted topic gap is the canonical comparison: it shows a strong positive association with coverage divergence (Spearman $\rho = \RhoCovTopic$, $p = 0.054$), while the raw gap is near-zero ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, $p = 0.765$) because the register component masks the topic signal ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister$, $p = 0.045$). The primary hypothesis (H1a) tests whether the raw coverage gap predicts raw semantic divergence: the association is near-zero in the canonical specification ($r = \HPrimaryCovgapPearsonR$, 95\% CI $[$\HPrimaryCovgapCiLower, \HPrimaryCovgapCiUpper$]$, $p = \HPrimaryCovgapPearsonP$). With only 17 SDG-level observations the design is powered to detect only very large correlations, so this is evidence against a strong monotonic relationship, not proof of independence. Research coverage alone likewise does not predict semantic divergence (H1c: $r = \HPrimaryPearsonR$, 95\% CI $[$\HPrimaryCiLower, \HPrimaryCiUpper$]$, $p = \HPrimaryPearsonP$; $n = \HPrimaryN$). No hypothesis survives robustness checks across alternative assignment methods, policy source families, or segment caps. Together, these null results show that coverage and framing should be measured as separate axes of divergence. The study reports three supporting patterns. First, policy discourse concentrates on SDGs~16 (Peace and Strong Institutions, \PolicyPctSdgSixteen\%), 13 (Climate Action, \PolicyPctSdgThirteen\%), and 17 (Partnerships, \PolicyPctSdgSeventeen\%), together \PolicyTopThreePct\% of policy coverage. Second, research concentrates on SDGs~3 (Good Health, \ResearchPctSdgThree\%), 9 (Industry and Infrastructure, \ResearchPctSdgNine\%), and 4 (Quality Education, artefact-affected, \ResearchPctSdgFour\%). Third, the largest within-SDG semantic gaps occur in SDGs~13 (\SemanticGapSdgThirteen), 3 (\SemanticGapSdgThree), 11 (\SemanticGapSdgEleven), 1 (\SemanticGapSdgOne), and 7 (\SemanticGapSdgSeven).

These findings do not prove substantive research-policy misalignment. They show that shared SDG labels are insufficient evidence of shared semantic framing, and that embedding-based distance is a transparent diagnostic map of proximity and divergence. The register decomposition reveals that the raw gap masks a register--topic cancellation: after INLP register removal, the adjusted topic gap shows a strong positive association with coverage divergence, while the register component cancels this signal in the raw estimate. The results need caution because of policy source-family heterogeneity, OpenAlex retrieval boundaries, and an SDG 4 assignment that is artefact-affected rather than a clean education finding.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Research-policy alignment is often inferred from whether scientific and policy texts address the same topics, yet shared topical coverage does not establish comparable framing: a paper and a policy document can both invoke SDG 13 (Climate Action) or SDG 3 (Good Health) while emphasising different problems, actors, and interventions. High-level thematic agreement can therefore mask deeper divergence in how each discourse community approaches the same goal.

Existing methods for mapping research to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) operate mainly at the level of lexical or bibliometric correspondence: keyword dictionaries, Boolean queries, or citation and mention counts \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. They measure whether a text is \emph{about} a given SDG, not how it frames it. That interchangeability is what this paper questions.

This paper proposes that research-policy alignment has at least two separable dimensions: coverage divergence (which SDGs each corpus prioritises) and semantic framing divergence (how differently they discuss the same SDG). Its contribution is a measurement framework. I advance AI--SDG mapping from Level~1 (lexical correspondence) to Level~2 (observed textual-semantic proximity in embedding space; Section~\ref{sec:alignment-framework}), treating semantic proximity as a register-sensitive proxy for framing, not evidence of substantive alignment. I operationalise both dimensions across all 17 SDGs with frozen Sentence-BERT embeddings \parencite{Reimers2019} and a supervised logistic-regression classifier, and stress-test the instrument against its principal measurement choices, reporting every gap ranking under a cross-sensitivity grid of assignment method, policy source family, and segment cap, with sample-stability and register-adjustment checks.

The central finding is a cancellation: coverage and framing are not independent — topic divergence rises with coverage divergence while register divergence falls with it, and the two sum to a near-zero raw correlation. This matters because SDG tagging is now institutionalised: OpenAlex labels works, funders count SDG outputs, and journals print SDG badges; if shared labels mask a register--topic cancellation, that threatens how the SDG-monitoring ecosystem reads its own signals. The divergence that survives robustness checks concentrates in a small set of goals: SDG 13 is the most divergent SDG under the two general-purpose encoders (MPNet, MiniLM), whereas the domain encoder SciBERT shifts the lead to SDG 3; SDG 15 is the most stably low-divergence SDG (SDG 17 has the narrowest raw gap but is the most method-sensitive); and SDG 17 is the most sensitive to how divergence is measured. I report this pattern with its scope conditions.

The central research question is: \emph{To what extent do academic AI-for-sustainability research and international institutional SDG policy discourse differ in SDG coverage and semantic framing across the 17 SDGs, and how are these two dimensions related?} Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews the literature and introduces the three-level framework, Section~\ref{sec:method} the corpora and measurement procedures, and Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:discussion} the findings and their interpretation.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{AI and the SDGs: What Bibliometrics Shows}

Existing assessments of AI and the SDGs rest on expert views or publication counts rather than on the texts themselves \parencite{Vinuesa2020}. Broader SDG scientometrics and the AI-specific inventories converge on one robust finding: SDG~3 (Good Health) and SDG~7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) are the most addressed goals in the AI-specific studies, followed by SDG~4, 13, 11, and 16. The evidence base spans a citation-based corpus of roughly 25,000 publications on the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals (M\&SDGs) \parencite{BautistaPuig2021}, 20,511 AI publications (2001--2020) \parencite{Singh2024}, 108 AI-for-social-good projects \parencite{Cowls2021}, and 1,288 big-data and AI publications \parencite{Nedungadi2024}, together with a recent review of 792 AI applications in SDG-related research \parencite{Gohr2025AiSdgs}. The least-covered goals differ between the AI-specific inventories and the broader bibliometric picture, and none of these studies measures within-SDG semantic framing.

SDG publication mapping is methodologically contingent. \textcite{Armitage2020} shows that what counts as SDG-relevant depends on interpretive choices about queries, labels, and databases; \textcite{Kashnitsky2024} reach the same caution from a broader comparison of Boolean, machine-learning, and hybrid mapping systems, finding no single best approach and no single ``golden'' validation set. The dependence persists in AI-based mapping: keyword baselines still omit publications that never state an SDG keyword explicitly, so the reported corpus boundary remains query-dependent \parencite{Yin2025SdgMapping}. The embedding-based approach used here reduces some vocabulary-level brittleness of keyword dictionaries, but its results remain conditional on corpus construction, centroid provenance, and model choice.

The SDGs themselves are interdependent by design, yet no operational framework specifies how \parencite{Nilsson2016}; single-SDG assignment is therefore a simplifying assumption. One goal breaks the pattern. \textcite{LeBlanc2015}, in a network analysis of the proposed SDGs, treats SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals) as the dedicated goal for cross-cutting means of implementation: finance, trade, technology transfer, and capacity building, excluding it from cross-goal mapping because its targets are implementation infrastructure rather than substantive domains. The \textcite{ICSU2017} guide likewise describes SDG 17 as the main enabler of the whole framework. Unlike every other SDG, whose discourse centres on a shared substantive topic, SDG 17 policy discourse concerns coordinating and resourcing implementation itself, a conceptual space that technical AI research abstracts are not built to occupy.

\subsection{A Three-Level Framework of Research-Policy Alignment}\label{sec:alignment-framework}

This paper proposes that research-policy alignment be understood as a three-level construct rather than a binary property of shared labels:

\textbf{Level 1: lexical or bibliometric correspondence.} The dominant mode of existing SDG-mapping work: assigning texts to goals through keyword dictionaries, Boolean queries, or citation and mention counts \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. Necessary but weak: shared vocabulary does not guarantee shared meaning \parencite{bornmann2016policy}, and keyword methods are brittle to vocabulary and database choices \parencite{Armitage2020}.

\textbf{Level 2: observed textual-semantic proximity in embedding space.} Sentence embeddings locate texts in a shared semantic space where proximity reflects distributional patterns of co-occurrence \parencite{Harris1954, Reimers2019}, and encoder-based SDG association has been benchmarked as a strong baseline \parencite{Gjorgjevikj2025}. This level detects divergence that keyword methods miss, while remaining a proxy rather than a definitive conclusion: embedding distance mixes modality, abstraction, institutional stance, and theme \parencite{biber1988variation}, and the research-policy contrast is primarily a register contrast \parencite{biberConrad2009register}. The present study is positioned at this level.

\textbf{Level 3: substantive comparison of implied problems, actors, and interventions.} Whether the two discourses address the same problems, involve the same actors, and propose the same interventions \parencite{Cash2003, Guston2001, Haas1992}. Because this requires interpreting causal beliefs, legitimacy, and institutional mechanism \parencite{Caplan1979, Haas1992}, it lies beyond unsupervised text analysis and is left to qualitative follow-up.

Advancing from Level 1 to Level 2 is the study's concrete methodological contribution; Level 3 names the boundary it deliberately does not cross. Figure~\ref{fig:conceptual-framework} illustrates this decomposition.

\subsection{Research-Policy Alignment in AI and Sustainability}

A growing literature documents structural tensions between what AI research produces and what sustainability policy requires: corporate AI research concentrates on pre-deployment technical problems, creating a gap between research priorities and the deployment impacts governance must address \parencite{Strauss2025}; global AI metrics emphasise technical and economic performance while policy discourse stresses ethical and social dimensions aligned with SDG concerns \parencite{SioumalasChristodoulou2025}; and research and policy communities can share trustworthy-AI terminology while differing in term salience and focal concerns \parencite{ToneyWails2024}. The pattern extends beyond AI: in conservation science, 59\% of policy-prioritised research topics appear in fewer than 15 papers \parencite{McCarthy2026}.

Adjacent scientometric work measures research-policy connection through policy-document mentions rather than semantic framing: only 1.2\% of 191,276 climate-change publications have at least one policy mention \parencite{bornmann2016policy}. Mentions are a boundary marker of one narrow form of policy uptake; they do not show how research is discussed or framed inside policy discourse.

Theory explains why such divergence is the default rather than a failure. Knowledge systems support action when they produce information that is simultaneously salient, credible, and legitimate \parencite{Cash2003}. The epistemic-communities framework explains why shared framing matters for policy coordination under uncertainty: expert communities influence policy by articulating cause--effect relationships and framing issues for collective debate \parencite{Haas1992}. Haas's concept is broader than semantic proximity, involving shared normative and causal beliefs, validity criteria, and a common policy enterprise; the present study therefore does not operationalise an epistemic community directly, but measures only the weaker condition of observed semantic proximity. Even this weaker condition is only a possible precondition for knowledge transfer: semantic proximity cannot show the existence, strength, or influence of an expert community \parencite{Cross2013}. The two-communities theory holds that researchers and policy makers live in separate worlds with different values, reward systems, and languages, so divergence is the default state of research-policy interaction \parencite{Caplan1979}.

The benchmark itself is not neutral. The SDG framework is a politically negotiated instrument with documented limitations: goals can create incentives to over-focus on target achievement \parencite{FukudaParr2016}, summit documents remain state-centric and avoid causal responsibility \parencite{BexellJonsson2017}, and Goal 8's 3\% annual growth target is empirically incompatible with the resource-use and emissions goals \parencite{Hickel2019}. Reported SDG engagement is frequently superficial, so reported alignment should not be conflated with substantive commitment \parencite{Heilinger2023, HerasSaizarbitoria2022}. To the extent that research follows Mode 1 knowledge production (problems set within academic disciplines) rather than Mode 2 (problems arising in contexts of application) \parencite{Gibbons1994}, divergence is not uniformly problematic: where research explores possibilities that policy discourse has not yet absorbed, distance may reflect productive exploration rather than failure of alignment.



\subsection{Semantic Methods: Rationale and Cautions}\label{sec:semantic-methods}

The distributional hypothesis, that meaning can be described through patterns of linguistic co-occurrence \parencite{Harris1954}, underlies the use of Sentence-BERT \parencite{Reimers2019} and related transformer encoders for large-scale semantic comparison. Applying them to compare two distinct discourse corpora, research abstracts and policy documents, is a novel usage rather than a directly benchmarked one, and the interpretation of distance remains descriptive rather than causal. \textcite{Gjorgjevikj2025} benchmarked sentence encoders specifically for SDG association tasks, finding that general-purpose models from the sentence-transformers family perform among the strongest as baselines, and that domain-specific fine-tuning further improves predictive performance while reducing sensitivity to description length. \textcite{Rodriguez2023}'s conText framework provides a computational-social-science precedent for comparing meaning across contexts, treating embedding-based ``meaning'' as a thin distributional description rather than a causal model of human interpretation; this study follows that same descriptive premise. \textcite{McCarthy2026} supply a closer empirical precedent, showing with a multi-target SciBERT classifier that research--policy coverage gaps are measurable with transformer encoders outside the AI--SDG setting; this study extends that logic from a single conservation domain to the full SDG landscape, and from coverage to within-SDG semantic framing.

An older corpus-linguistic tradition adds a further caution: Biber's multidimensional analysis of spoken and written English showed that register differences are not simple binary contrasts but continuous patterns of co-occurring linguistic features across genres \parencite{biber1988variation}. For the present study this matters because research abstracts and policy documents may differ not only in substantive topic, but also in abstraction, modality, institutional stance, and rhetorical function; embedding distance is therefore treated as observed textual-semantic proximity, not a clean measure of topic alone.

\subsection{Research Gap}

To the best of my knowledge, the literature reviewed above has not produced: (i) a systematic comparison of how academic AI-for-sustainability research and institutional SDG policy discourse differ in SDG coverage and within-SDG semantic framing; prior work measures research coverage, citation patterns, or policy-document uptake, but does not compare the two corpora directly on these dimensions; or (ii) an examination of whether coverage gaps and semantic gaps are positively related, weakly related, or inversely related. These are the contributions of the present study, and they answer the central research question set out in the Introduction. Together, they provide a systematic framework for distinguishing research-policy coverage from research-policy semantic proximity within a shared SDG representation. This framework is deliberately diagnostic: it maps where observed divergence is visible, without classifying whether any given gap reflects productive or problematic disconnection; the latter would require qualitative follow-up beyond the scope of embedding analysis.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Research Corpus}\label{sec:research-corpus}

This study uses only publicly available text data and code. It does not involve human participants or personal data. No ethical approval was required.

The research corpus consists of \NResearchPapers{} English-language research abstracts retrieved from the OpenAlex API \parencite{Priem2022}, covering publications between 2018 and 2025. Retrieval combined OpenAlex's native SDG work filter with four high-precision AI search terms (\texttt{artificial intelligence}, \texttt{machine learning}, \texttt{deep learning}, \texttt{neural network}), giving 68 queries (17 SDGs × 4 terms). The term set follows established practice for delimiting AI corpora \parencite{Elsevier2019, OECDAiMethod}; because the aim is macro-level semantic alignment rather than exhaustive publication counting, precision is prioritised at the retrieval stage to keep non-AI noise out of the embedding space. Complete query parameters are documented in the Supplementary Methodology (Appendix~\ref{app:supp-methodology}).

A direct test of this scope condition re-ran retrieval using field-of-study membership for the same two AI fields \parencite{OECDAiMethod} and re-scored the resulting 50,000-paper corpus through the identical pipeline. The concept-retrieved coverage and semantic-gap rankings correlate strongly with the keyword baseline (SDG-share Spearman $\rho = \ConceptCoverageSpearman$, coverage-gap rank $\rho = \ConceptCoverageGapRho$, semantic-gap rank $\rho = \ConceptSemanticGapRho$; Appendix~\ref{app:concept-retrieval-sensitivity}), so the reported divergence patterns are not an artefact of the four-term query choice.

An important asymmetry follows: the research corpus is a tight intersection of AI and SDG content ($\text{AI} \cap \text{SDG}$), while the policy corpus (Section~\ref{sec:policy-corpus}) is a broader union covering institutional SDG discourse and AI governance. Between-SDG rankings are conditional on this asymmetric construction; following \textcite{Armitage2020}, the retrieval universe is a method-dependent corpus boundary, not a neutral population of all SDG-relevant AI research. After deduplication and removal of records lacking usable abstracts, \NResearchPapers{} abstracts (each treated as one segment) were retained. No balancing by SDG or citation count is applied, because trimming would distort the coverage profile the study aims to measure.

\subsection{Policy Corpus}\label{sec:policy-corpus}

The policy corpus contains \NPolicySegments{} text segments from three collections, representing \NPolicyDocs{} unique source documents. It should be read as mixed \emph{international institutional SDG policy discourse} rather than the full universe of AI sustainability policy:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Curated AI and SDG policy documents} (13,123 segments, 94 documents): UN, OECD, EU, UK, and AU AI governance frameworks; IPCC assessment reports; SDSN sustainable development reports; and national AI strategies. These are AI-governance instruments by construction, forming the innovation-facing slice of the corpus.

  \item \textbf{SDGi Voluntary National Review and Voluntary Local Review corpus} \parencite{SDGi2024} (21,346 segments, 4,225 documents): National and local self-assessments submitted to the UN High-Level Political Forum. The creators describe it as the most comprehensive multilingual collection of SDG-labelled texts to date, spanning all 17 goals and centred on SDG implementation rather than any single sector.

  \item \textbf{UN General Debate Corpus} \parencite{UNGDC2024} (6,128 segments, 2,048 documents): SDG-relevant passages from General Debate speeches, sessions 70--80 (2015--2024). Its creators analyse SDG coverage directly and report climate action (SDG~13), peace and governance (SDG~16), and partnerships (SDG~17) among the most prominent themes, an orientation the analysis below also observes.
\end{enumerate}

After cross-source deduplication by exact text match (SDGi retained first), \NPolicySegments{} unique segments remained. The three collections differ in genre and scope: the curated subset is AI-governance-focused, while SDGi and UNGDC reflect broader institutional SDG discourse, so the full-corpus distribution should be read accordingly; the curated sub-corpus (94 documents) is the more relevant reference for AI-governance-specific interpretation. Appendix~\ref{app:policy-source-data} reports a source-family sensitivity check. Segmentation is token-aware and detailed in the Supplementary Methodology (Appendix~\ref{app:supp-methodology}).

\subsection{Embedding Model and Normalisation}\label{sec:embedding-choice}

All texts were encoded with \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} \parencite{all-mpnet-base-v2, Reimers2019} from the sentence-transformers library, producing 768-dimensional dense vectors. This pre-trained model requires no fine-tuning on the data under analysis, so the embedding space is a stable, pre-existing coordinate system; Section~\ref{sec:encoder-sensitivity} tests sensitivity to this choice against a domain-specialised scientific encoder of the same dimensionality. Embeddings were L2-normalised to unit vectors so that cosine similarity equals their dot product. A truncation issue discovered during development was corrected by enforcing the token budget at segmentation time (Appendix~\ref{app:supp-methodology}).

\subsection{Supervised Reference Classifier}\label{sec:supervised-classifier}

The canonical instrument is a supervised multinomial logistic regression (LR) classifier trained on pooled single-label reference data from five independent annotated corpora:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OSDG Community Dataset} \parencite{Pukelis2022} (30,532 texts, SDGs 1--16): crowdsourced excerpts from reports, policy documents, and abstracts, each validated by multiple volunteers.
  \item \textbf{SDG Classification Benchmark} \parencite{SDGBenchmark} (281 expert-verified texts, SDGs 1--17): short text snippets annotated by multiple domain experts with consensus resolution.
  \item \textbf{IISD SDG Knowledge Hub} \parencite{Wulff2023SDGKnowledgeHub} (6,122 texts, SDGs 1--17): IISD journalism and policy commentary on 2030 Agenda implementation.
  \item \textbf{SDGi Corpus} \parencite{SDGi2024} (20,267 texts, SDGs 1--17): government Voluntary National and Local Reviews submitted to the UN.
  \item \textbf{Aurora dataset} \parencite{Vanderfeesten2020Aurora} (4,971 expert-validated texts, SDGs 1--17): academic abstracts validated by domain researchers.
\end{itemize}

All five corpora supply human- or expert-validated labels rather than machine-generated ones, and they differ in provenance: OSDG is a crowdsourced volunteer-confirmed collection, SDGi compiles government reviews, the Knowledge Hub is journalism, and Aurora is academic expert-validated text. The mix broadens the label base, but OSDG alone contributes about 49\% of labelled texts and draws on UN-adjacent documents, so the reference data remain weighted toward policy vocabulary. No high-quality graded relevance sets exist for SDG classification, making this combined, multi-source collection the best available resource \parencite{Kashnitsky2024}.

The pooled reference data (n=\NReferencePool{} after single-label filtering) were split into training and held-out test sets (n=\NTrainPool{} train, n=\NTestPool{} test) by stratified document-grouped splitting, keeping all texts from the same source document in one fold to enforce strict separation between training and evaluation. The champion LR ($C=10.0$, L2 penalty, \texttt{class\_weight=None}, solver \texttt{lbfgs}) was selected by 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation on the training pool and reaches macro-$F_1$ = \MacroFOne{} on the held-out test set (Section~\ref{sec:validation}). The grid-search protocol, hyperparameter rationale, and the MLP robustness comparison are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:model-selection}. The validation task does not evaluate a rejection option for non-SDG texts; this is acceptable because the research corpus is first restricted to an OpenAlex AI-and-SDG universe, so the classifier answers a relative question among the 17 SDGs.

\subsection{Scoring and Assignment}\label{sec:scoring}

A text is assigned to the SDG with the highest predicted probability from the trained LR classifier (argmax over 17 classes). Every assignment is traceable to 768 signed logistic-regression coefficients per SDG class, a transparent decision boundary with no hidden layers, no dropout, and no random-seed sensitivity. Macro-$F_1$, the unweighted mean of per-SDG scores, is the primary validation metric (Section~\ref{sec:validation}); LR also provides calibrated per-class probabilities used as a soft-assignment comparison against the canonical hard argmax.

\subsection{Classifier Calibration Across Discourse Types}\label{sec:assumptions}

Because the classifier is trained on pooled reference data that are themselves policy-adjacent (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}), the scoring instrument behaves differently on the two corpora. Policy segments receive systematically higher LR predicted probabilities than research abstracts (mean \AffFifteenPolicyScore{} vs \AffFifteenPaperScore{}, a \AffFifteenDiff{} gap). The gap has two causes the data cannot separate: a genre artefact (the reference data skew toward policy vocabulary) or a substantive difference (policy texts address SDG implementation more directly). Either way the asymmetry does not threaten the findings: hard argmax assignment uses only within-corpus ranking, not absolute probability levels, so the directional gap does not reorder goals. It is not an SDGi circularity effect: the training set and scored policy corpus are disjoint by construction.

\subsection{Coverage Gap Analysis}

For each corpus item, the predicted SDG is the LR argmax (hard assignment, Section~\ref{sec:scoring}); the coverage profile is the proportion of items assigned to each SDG.

The policy corpus is not a flat set of documents: its \NPolicySegments{} segments are grouped into \NPolicyDocs{} source documents of very different lengths (Section~\ref{sec:policy-corpus}). A segment-weighted profile would let a handful of long reports dominate the count, so the analysis instead computes a \emph{document-weighted} profile in which every source document counts equally regardless of length. Concretely: (1) the segment score vectors of each document are averaged into one document-level vector; (2) that vector is hard-assigned to its top SDG; (3) the coverage profile is the share of documents, not segments, assigned to each SDG. The research corpus uses the same logic at the paper level, where each of the \NResearchPapers{} abstract texts is already a single unit. Document-weighted profiles are the canonical representation; segment-weighted profiles are retained as a diagnostic comparison.

The coverage gap for SDG $j$ is defined as:
\[
  \text{CoverageGap}_j = \left|\text{ResearchCoverage}_j - \text{PolicyCoverage}^{\text{docwt}}_j\right|
\]

The signed version of the same quantity is:
\[
  \text{SignedCoverageGap}_j = \text{ResearchCoverage}_j - \text{PolicyCoverage}^{\text{docwt}}_j
\]
A positive value means research dominates SDG $j$; a negative value means policy dominates. These two definitions, together with research coverage and policy coverage, are the four predictors reported in Table~\ref{tab:interaction}.

\subsection{Semantic Gap Analysis}\label{sec:semantic-gap-method}

The semantic gap measures within-SDG divergence: given that both corpora have texts assigned to SDG $j$, how differently do they discuss it? For each SDG $j$, the analysis collects all assigned research papers and all assigned policy segments (capped at 50 per source document to prevent single-document dominance; SDSN reports alone contribute up to 3,179 segments otherwise), computes the L2-normalised mean embedding of each cluster ($\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j$, $\mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j$), and defines:

\[
\text{SemanticGap}_j = 1 - (\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j \cdot \mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j)
\]

A gap of 0 indicates perfectly overlapping semantic directions; a gap approaching~1 indicates orthogonal framing.

The \emph{per-document segment cap} of $K=50$ (approximately 7,500 words) uses seeded random sampling, hence reproducible, and sits well above the median of roughly 14 segments per document. Repeating the computation at a cap of 20 and with no cap leaves the SDG gap rankings near-identical (rank correlation $\rho = \CapStabilityRho$; Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}, Section~\ref{sec:cross-sensitivity}), so the findings are robust to the cap.

The semantic gap is not a clean measure of substantive disagreement. Following Biber and Conrad's distinction between register, genre, and style \parencite{biberConrad2009register}, I treat the research--policy contrast primarily as a register contrast: the two corpora differ in situation of use, audience, and communicative purpose, not merely in topic. Embedding distance therefore mixes genuine within-SDG substantive divergence with register divergence in abstraction, modality, and institutional stance \parencite{biber1988variation}. To separate these components, I decompose the raw centroid distance into a register-adjusted (topic) component and a register component using Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP; \textcite{Ravfogel2020INLP}). The adjusted topic gap is the canonical measure: it isolates within-SDG divergence after removing the register channel. The raw centroid distance is retained as a reference measure because it directly corresponds to the observed framing difference. After register removal, the mean adjusted gap is \MeanAdjustedGap{} (40\% compression from the raw mean of \MeanRawGap{}), and the residual register component averages \MeanRegisterComponent{} (Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}).

\paragraph{INLP register-removal procedure.}
The procedure follows the Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP) framework of \textcite{Ravfogel2020INLP}, which trains linear classifiers to predict a target property and repeatedly projects the representations onto the nullspace of the learned directions until no further linear separation is detectable. The present adaptation introduces SDG stratification: at each iteration, exactly \RegisterIterNPerSdg{} research and \RegisterIterNPerSdg{} policy embeddings are drawn from each SDG, preventing the classifier from learning topic membership as a proxy for register. The stratification key is $2 \times \text{sdg\_labels} + y$ (34 classes), ensuring balanced corpus--topic combinations. Each iteration orthogonalises the new direction against all previous ones, then held-out accuracy is measured on a stratified 15\% test split of the projected sample. Training uses L2-regularised logistic regression (solver: lbfgs, max\_iter: 1000). The procedure stops when held-out accuracy reaches the majority-class baseline ($\leq 0.5$), the new direction is numerically degenerate (cosine similarity $> 0.99$ with any prior direction), or the maximum number of iterations ($K = 200$) is reached.

The identification argument rests on the stratification design. Because every iteration trains on equal numbers of research and policy embeddings from each SDG, the classifier cannot learn which SDG a document belongs to: every SDG is balanced across classes. The only remaining signal available for discrimination is the corpus-level register difference: the way research papers sound versus the way policy documents sound, within the same substantive topic. What gets removed at each iteration is therefore precisely the register component, modality differences (academic prose versus policy prose), audience conventions (peer reviewers versus policymakers), institutional stance (hedged claims versus prescriptive language), and structural patterns (methods sections versus action items). What survives is the semantic content difference between AI research and SDG policy for each specific SDG.

Figure~\ref{fig:pca-register-before-after} shows the geometric effect of INLP register removal: the raw embedding space (left) separates research and policy clouds, while the adjusted space (right) merges them, confirming that the separation is driven by register rather than topic. The effect is encoder-dependent --- SciBERT already fuses the clouds without INLP --- so this figure is MPNet-specific.

\subsection{Coverage--Semantic Interaction}\label{sec:coverage-semantic-interaction}

The first hypothesis (H1) decomposes into four sub-hypotheses, each correlating a distinct coverage predictor with the within-SDG semantic gap across the 17 SDGs: H1a, coverage gap $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap; H1b, research--policy dominance $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap; H1c, research coverage $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap; and H1d, policy coverage $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap. H1a is the primary, motivating hypothesis. I compute Pearson $r$ and Spearman $\rho$ between each predictor and semantic-gap scores, with sensitivity analyses excluding SDG~4 (Section~\ref{sec:sdg4artefact}). Given $n = 17$, the tests have limited power to detect anything but a large linear effect; this is a scope condition.

\subsection{Key Assumptions and Mitigations}\label{sec:assumptions-stub}

Two design-level choices shape the comparison before any measurement takes place; both are methodological decisions rather than empirical findings.

\textbf{Text-unit handling is a design choice, not a result.} Research papers are single abstract-length texts (mean $\sim$200 words); policy items are 150--300-word segments from documents of highly variable length. Document-weighting and per-document segment caps are adopted up front to keep the two corpora comparable; they are fixed procedures, not findings derived from the data.

\textbf{Labelled-data limitation on SDG 4.}
\label{sec:sdg4artefact}
The research coverage assigns \ResearchPctSdgFour\% of papers to SDG 4 (Quality Education, see later Section~\ref{sec:coverage-gap}), flagged as artefact-affected because ML papers share core vocabulary (``learning'', ``training'', ``model'') with the OSDG education corpus, a case of lexical brittleness in keyword-based semantic mapping \parencite{Armitage2020}. Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit} confirms that SDG~4-assigned texts contain education-specific terms more often than matched non-SDG4 samples, but co-occur with ML-learning vocabulary. The result is retained as a learning-related SDG~4 assignment rather than a clean education estimate. Sensitivity analyses excluding SDG~4 are provided for all correlation results.

\textbf{Policy corpus.} Three SDGs dominate the document-weighted profile of the full institutional policy corpus: SDG 16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions, \PolicyPctSdgSixteen\%), SDG 13 (Climate Action, \PolicyPctSdgThirteen\%), and SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals, \PolicyPctSdgSeventeen\%), accounting for \PolicyTopThreePct\% of policy documents. Second, the SDG~3 dominance reproduced here is consistent with four prior bibliometric studies that independently find SDG~3 leading AI-for-sustainability research (Section~\ref{sec:litreview}), even though those studies used keyword and citation methods rather than embeddings. Third, this is all while the classifier is trained on reference corpora that are themselves policy-adjacent (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}); that the ranking nonetheless converges with independent, methodologically unrelated literature is the strongest available evidence that the classifier anchors a meaningful semantic coordinate system, not a policy-specific artefact.

\subsection{Domain-Encoder Sensitivity (Same-Dimension Scientific Encoder)}
\label{sec:encoder-sensitivity}

The cross-sensitivity check above compares \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} (768-d) against the lighter \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} (384-d); because the two differ in both architecture and dimensionality, that comparison bounds encoder sensitivity only modulo the dimensionality drop. This section adds a third encoder, \texttt{allenai/scibert\_scivocab\_uncased} \parencite{Beltagy2019}, a scientific-domain BERT that is also 768-d and the architecture used by \textcite{McCarthy2026} in the closest published precedent for the present design. MPNet and SciBERT therefore differ in architecture and training domain but share dimensionality, so any remaining disagreement between them is a pure architecture/domain effect. Both encoders embed the identical canonical MPNet-segmented texts, and SciBERT is scored on the same 50,000-paper representative research sample used by the sample-stability ladder, so inputs are held fixed.

The more revealing result is the gap magnitude. SciBERT gives a mean semantic gap of \SciBERTMeanGap{} (cosine \SciBERTMeanCos{}) against MPNet's \MPNetMeanGap{} (\MPNetMeanCos{}): the research--policy distance collapses to near uniformity across all 17 SDGs. The embeddings are not degenerate (SciBERT's cohesion values \SciBERTResCohesion{}, \SciBERTPolCohesion{} exceed MPNet's \MPNetResCohesion{}, \MPNetPolCohesion{}), but its scientific pretraining fuses the two genres: SciBERT's weights are tuned to 1.14M scientific papers, so policy text, which mirrors scientific vocabulary and formal syntax, lands in the same region as research abstracts; MPNet, trained on general web text, keeps the technical-method versus institutional-mandate contrast that SciBERT erases. A general-purpose encoder is therefore the right ruler: a domain-specialised one that fuses the genres cannot measure the register gap this study tracks.

Tables~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-semantic} and~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-coverage} report the within-SDG gap rankings under MPNet, MiniLM, and SciBERT for each assignment method. Despite the compressed magnitudes, the semantic-gap ranking is moderately preserved across the three encoders (MPNet--LR vs.\ SciBERT--LR Spearman $\rho = \SciBERTSemanticRho$; MPNet--LR vs.\ MiniLM--LR $\rho \approx \MiniLMSemanticRho$), and the coverage-gap ranking is highly stable (MPNet--LR vs.\ SciBERT--LR $\rho = \SciBERTCoverageRho$). SDG~13 leads and SDG~17 trails under both general-purpose encoders; the scientific-domain SciBERT compresses the magnitudes and, at its LR assignment, shifts the lead to SDG~3 and the trail to SDG~8. The divergence findings are therefore not an artefact of the MPNet geometry or its 768-d dimensionality: the magnitudes are encoder-dependent, but the divergence conclusion and the coverage-structure invariance hold across general-purpose and domain-specialised encoders alike.

\subsection{Robustness of the Semantic-Gap Ranking to the Distance Functional}
\label{sec:distributional-robustness}

The centroid gap (\S\ref{sec:semantic-gap}) is the cosine distance between per-SDG mean embeddings. A reviewer may object that a single mean discards the shape of the two point clouds: two SDGs could share a mean direction yet differ in spread or modality \parencite{Villani2008}. This section tests that objection directly by recomputing the per-SDG research--policy gap under a battery of distribution-aware metrics on the identical point clouds, holding the supervised SDG assignment and embeddings fixed; only the distance functional changes.

The canonical gap is reproduced exactly as a validation gate: the full-corpus centroid gap equals the published value to $10^{-4}$, and the streamed research means reproduce the committed centroids exactly. Full-corpus metrics (sliced Wasserstein \parencite{Bonneel2015}, Chamfer \parencite{Borgefors1988}, Gaussian-2-Wasserstein \parencite{Dowson1982}, Grassmann \parencite{Edelman1998}, linear MMD \parencite{Gretton2012}) carry no sampling variance; sampled metrics (exact EMD \parencite{Rubner2000}, Sinkhorn \parencite{Cuturi2013}, energy \parencite{Szekely2013}, RBF-MMD \parencite{Gretton2012}, C2ST \parencite{LopezPaz2017}) use a 50,000-paper draw per SDG, with SDGs~1 and~17 using their full population. Table~\ref{tab:distributional-gap} reports the full per-SDG values (Appendix~\ref{app:distributional-table}).

\textbf{Shape-sensitive metrics preserve the ranking.} Gaussian-2-Wasserstein ($\rho=\DistGapSpearmanFrechet$), RBF maximum mean discrepancy ($\rho=\DistGapSpearmanMmd$), exact optimal-transport EMD ($\rho=\DistGapSpearmanEmd$), sliced Wasserstein ($\rho=\DistGapSpearmanSwd$), and energy distance ($\rho=\DistGapSpearmanEnergy$; \parencite{Seijdinovic2013}) all preserve the canonical ordering at $p<0.001$ \parencite{Spearman1904}. SDG~13 is the most divergent SDG under every metric; its primacy is unconditional.

\textbf{What centroids discard is quantified, not hidden.} The Gaussian-2-Wasserstein distance decomposes into a mean term and a shape (covariance) term \parencite{Dowson1982,Heusel2017}. Across SDGs the shape term accounts for a mean share of $\approx\DistGapShapeShareMean\%$ of the distance. Centroids therefore drop real distributional signal, but that signal does not reorder the SDGs, which is why the centroid ranking is robust.

\textbf{Margin-level nuance.} The second and third canonical positions (SDGs~3 and~11) are less stable: several distribution-aware metrics elevate SDGs~1, 7, 12, and~14 instead. This is a genuine margin-level reshuffle among SDGs with similar gap magnitudes, not an error; the overall monotonic agreement (positive $\rho$ for every shape-sensitive metric) holds.

\textbf{Separation, not ranking, from C2ST.} The classifier two-sample test \parencite{LopezPaz2017} returns AUC $\approx1$ for all 17 SDGs, so it confirms that research and policy are separable in every SDG but carries no ranking signal ($\rho\approx0$). Its near-constant value is expected, not a failure.

\textbf{Sampling stability.} The sampled metrics use a 50,000-paper draw; a replicate seed changes any sampled gap by at most $\DistGapMaxSeedDelta$, confirming the estimate is stable and the default seed is representative. With the cross-sensitivity checks (\S\ref{sec:cross-sensitivity}), the centroid gap ranking is therefore robust to both the assignment method and the distance functional.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The headline result is a cancellation: the raw coverage-vs-semantic-gap correlation is near-zero ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, $p = 0.765$) because two opposing signals cancel — topic divergence rises with coverage divergence ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic = 0.48$, $p = 0.054$) while register divergence falls with it ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister = -0.49$, $p = 0.045$), and the two sum to zero. The primary test (H1a) finds no detectable raw association in the canonical specification (Section~\ref{sec:results}; Table~\ref{tab:interaction}): with only $n = \HPrimaryN$ SDG-level observations the design is powered only to detect very large correlations ($|\HPrimaryMinDetectableR|$ at 80\% power), so the wide interval is consistent with either no effect or a moderate one and is evidence against a strong monotonic relationship, not proof of exact independence. All four hypotheses (H1a--H1d) are null on the raw gap, and the null holds across every sensitivity check excluding SDG~4 or SDG~17. After register removal the adjusted topic gap is positively associated with coverage divergence, so coverage and framing are linked through topic, not independent.

This cancellation is not a design confound. The supervised LR classifier (\S\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}) is a labelling instrument with a transparent 768-coefficient boundary; the frozen Sentence-BERT space (\S\ref{sec:embedding-choice}) is a separate measurement surface. Because classification and measurement use different procedures, the cancellation describes the corpora's discourse structure. The encoder-sensitivity check confirms it: coverage ranks are nearly invariant across general-purpose and domain-specialised encoders ($\rho = \SciBERTCoverageRho$), while semantic-gap magnitude tracks whether the encoder separates research from policy registers.

The paper makes two contributions of unequal weight: a measurement framework separating classification from measurement and coverage from framing, which holds across changes of assignment method, policy source family, and segment cap (Section~\ref{sec:cross-sensitivity}, appendices); and a weaker set of AI--SDG findings, in which the coverage--semantic association reverses sign under some configurations (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}) and SDG~17's position depends heavily on assignment method (Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-semantic}). The framework is the main contribution; the alignment findings are a first application to be checked against a larger, more diverse policy corpus, not settled results.

The coverage and gap profiles are conditional on corpus composition. The policy source-family split shifts the full profile: the curated AI/SDG sub-corpus is innovation-heavy (SDG~9 leads), while SDGi and UNGDC are climate-, partnerships-, and governance-oriented (SDGs~13,~17,~16) (Table~\ref{tab:policy-source-family}, Appendix~\ref{app:policy-source-data}). The cross-sensitivity table (Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}) is therefore the primary interpretive device, with encoder and assignment-method axes covered by Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-semantic} (Section~\ref{sec:encoder-sensitivity}); claims are limited to findings that survive this filtering.

\subsection{Two Distinct Dimensions of Observed Divergence}

The cancellation is best read as a structural difference between knowledge systems, not a communication failure. The Mode~1/2 distinction \parencite{Gibbons1994} sharpens this: research concentrates on technically tractable applications in health, energy, and infrastructure (SDGs~3,~7,~9) where ML tasks fit well-defined problem-solving, while policy dominance of SDGs~13,~16,~17 reflects Mode~2 goal-setting from contexts of application demanding coordination. Epistemic-community theory bounds the claim \parencite{Haas1992}: large within-SDG semantic gaps mark where shared problem-framing is less plausible, yet semantic proximity is only a possible precondition for knowledge transfer \parencite{Cross2013} and cannot show whether expert communities or policy uptake exist. The two-communities theory \parencite{Caplan1979} reinforces the reading: uncorrelated coverage and framing are expected when the two corpora are distinct knowledge cultures with different reward systems.

\subsection{Robust Patterns of Divergence}

The cross-sensitivity comparison narrows the gap findings stable enough for individual interpretation. SDG~15 is the robustly lowest-gap SDG: rank~16 (LR) and rank~17 (MLP), rising to rank~11 under zero-shot. SDG~17 is the most method-sensitive: rank~17 (smallest gap) under LR, rank~13 under MLP, rank~7 under zero-shot nearest-centroid, and register treatment flips its position entirely --- from the smallest raw gap to the largest adjusted gap after INLP register removal (Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}). This is consistent with SDG~17's character as the means-of-implementation goal \parencite{LeBlanc2015}; the centroid method struggles to anchor this boundary, while supervised LR isolates a tighter region where research and policy converge. The register decomposition confirms that the adjusted topic gap is the meaningful comparison: after register removal, the adjusted gap for SDG~17 is \AdjGapSdgSeventeen{} (raw: \RawGapSdgSeventeen{}), the largest adjusted gap in the corpus, while the register component is \RegCompSdgSeventeen{} --- the only negative value, indicating that register actually \emph{increases} the semantic similarity for this policy-dominant SDG.

SDG~13, SDG~11, and SDG~3 are the most robustly high-gap SDGs, holding the top six ranks across all three assignment methods and across cap and policy-source configurations. The climate gap (SDG~13) is the most consistent high-gap finding (rank~1 LR, 4 MLP, 5 zero-shot; top six under every policy sub-corpus and segment cap); SDG~3 is high-gap canonically and under every cap but drops to rank~9 under the curated AI/SDG family, which frames health through an innovation lens. SDG~9, the closest gap under the centroid approach, now sits mid-to-lower (gap \SemanticGapSdgNine{}) -- still relatively close, no longer narrowest.

The research corpus concentrates on technically tractable SDGs (3,~9,~4,~16,~11); SDG~4 is strongly represented but its share is partly inflated by ML learning-vocabulary (Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}). SDGs~7 and~12 sit consistently in the upper half of gap ranks; SDG~11 is the most consistent high-gap goal outside the top three (LR~3, zero-shot~2, MLP~1). SDGs~1,~2,~5,~6,~8,~9,~10,~14, and~16 move four or more ranks between configurations (e.g.\ SDG~8 shifts rank~6 under LR to rank~15 under zero-shot; SDG~9 shifts rank~12 under LR to rank~1 under the SDGi-only reference). No individual claim is made about their precise positions.

Two examples make the gap concrete at the term level\footnote{Distinctive terms are TF-IDF-weighted unigrams from deterministic samples assigned to each SDG by the LR classifier; full cases in Appendix~\ref{app:semantic-gap-text-interpretability}.}. SDG~13 (gap \SemanticGapSdgThirteen{}), the widest gap, pairs research terms (\textit{model}, \textit{prediction}, \textit{neural}, \textit{analysis}) with policy terms (\textit{climate}, \textit{change}, \textit{emissions}, \textit{countries}, \textit{action}): one measures, the other commits. SDG~17 (gap \SemanticGapSdgSeventeen{}), the narrowest under LR, pairs technical (\textit{model}, \textit{method}, \textit{machine}) with institutional (\textit{countries}, \textit{nations}, \textit{global}, \textit{agenda}) terms yet converges on a shared partnership core. The gap thus captures a real difference in how the two discourses frame shared SDG assignments, not a uniform property across goals.

\subsection{Implications}

Two implications argue against reading topical mention as alignment. \textbf{Agenda-setting}: the map does not prescribe funding redirection from embedding distances; it shows where research--policy translation and brokerage are most needed, and high-gap SDGs are candidates for boundary-organising practices that produce usable boundary objects accountable to both producers and users \parencite{Guston2001}. \textbf{Research communication}: more output on neglected SDGs is insufficient without communication norms; funder-required policy-facing summaries, journal-level SDG justification fields, or policy synthesis briefs may raise semantic proximity more than topic-level mandates \parencite{Cash2003}. For SDG monitoring bodies, funders, and journals alike, topical mention is not alignment: monitoring should report coverage and framing separately, allocation alone will not close the gap without policy-relevant communication, and SDG tags are not evidence of policy-aligned research.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Each scope condition below states the limit and the check that bounds it.

\textbf{Semantic proximity, not substantive alignment or influence.} The study measures embedding proximity, not whether research and policy are substantively aligned or whether research is used by policy. This is a scoping decision, not an unaddressed gap: the framework is positioned at Level~2 of a three-level construct and explicitly leaves Level~3 (substantive agreement) to qualitative follow-up.

\textbf{Distance metric.} Centroids discard distributional shape, but the SDG ranking is preserved across all eight distribution-aware distances (Spearman $\rho = 0.44$--$0.91$; Gaussian-2-Wasserstein $\rho=\DistGapSpearmanFrechet$, exact EMD $\rho=\DistGapSpearmanEmd$) and SDG~13 is the most divergent under every metric; the Wasserstein shape term accounts for $\approx\DistGapShapeShareMean\%$ but does not reorder the SDGs. The only non-ranker is the classifier two-sample test (C2ST, AUC $\approx 1$), a separation test (Appendix~\ref{sec:distributional-robustness}). Sampling stability is verified with a replicate seed (maximum gap change $\DistGapMaxSeedDelta$).

\textbf{Estimate stability under sampling.} A 100-draw subsampling ladder (1k--2M papers) shows headline metrics stabilise well before the full corpus, so results are not sampling artefacts (Appendix~\ref{app:sample-stability}).

\textbf{Corpus scope and retrieval.} The policy corpus is international-institutional SDG discourse in English; the research query prioritises precision over recall (Section~\ref{sec:research-corpus}), minimising non-AI noise at the cost of specialised subfields. The source-family split makes composition sensitivity explicit (Appendix~\ref{app:policy-source-data}). The comparison is inherently asymmetric: AI-for-sustainability research is a well-defined, if niche, academic category, whereas ``AI and the SDGs'' is not yet a mainstream organising category in policy discourse, so a policy corpus exactly comparable to the research intersection does not exist. I addressed this as best I could by re-testing the central interaction on the curated AI/SDG policy family, a symmetric AI/SDG-vs-AI/SDG construction (Section~\ref{sec:interaction}, Appendix~\ref{app:policy-source-data}); broadening the research side to all-SDG research (dropping the AI-term intersection) would complete the symmetry and is left to future work. Between-SDG rankings are conditional on this asymmetric construction.

\textbf{OpenAlex upstream classification.} SDG tags inherit an upstream classifier's bias; un-tagged records are absent. This is a documented method-dependent boundary \parencite{Armitage2020}, and coverage proportions are read conditional on it.

\textbf{Assignment-method and encoder sensitivity.} Gaps are conditional on assignment: the encoder- and assignment-method sensitivity tables show the supervised LR ranking is preserved across the two general-purpose encoders (MPNet vs.\ MiniLM LR $\rho \approx \MiniLMSemanticRho$), while the domain encoder SciBERT compresses gap magnitude to \SciBERTMeanGap{} yet preserves coverage structure ($\rho = \SciBERTCoverageRho$). The raw LR gaps are retained because they best match what the study measures and are most stable across source family and segment cap; MLP and zero-shot are the uncertainty bounds. The zero-shot method is scoped to one supervised-versus-nearest-centroid comparison under the canonical encoder (Appendix~\ref{app:assignment-method-comparison}); its moderate correlation with the LR baseline ($\rho = \ZeroShotSemanticRho$) is a method-axis effect (nearest-centroid assignment without a trained decision boundary), not an encoder one.

\textbf{Register effects.} The gap may capture register, not topic. A register decomposition using Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP) separates the raw centroid distance into a topic component (adjusted gap, mean \MeanAdjustedGap{}) and a register component (mean \MeanRegisterComponent{}; Appendix~\ref{app:register-robustness}). After register removal, the adjusted topic gap is the canonical comparison: it shows a strong positive association with coverage divergence (Spearman $\rho = \RhoCovTopic$, $p = 0.054$), while the raw gap is near-zero ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, $p = 0.765$) because the register component ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister$, $p = 0.045$) cancels the topic signal. For all SDGs except SDG~17, the register component is positive (range: \RegCompSdgFifteen{} to \RegCompSdgThirteen{}), confirming that register uniformly inflates the raw gap. The raw gap is retained as a reference measure because it directly corresponds to the observed framing difference, while the adjusted topic gap is the canonical estimate.

\textbf{Cross-sectional design.} The study observes one period (2018--2025) against a similarly timed policy corpus; it cannot assess whether alignment is improving or deteriorating, or whether specific events (e.g., the EU AI Act 2024, IPCC AR6 2022) shifted research framing.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduces a measurement protocol that separates research-policy divergence into coverage and semantic framing, and demonstrates on the AI--SDG interface that the two are linked but separable: the raw coverage-vs-semantic-gap correlation is near-zero not because coverage and framing are independent, but because a positive topic signal is cancelled by a negative register signal (after INLP register removal the adjusted topic gap is positively associated with coverage divergence, so topical attention does predict framing proximity once register is removed). Across three assignment methods, four policy source families, and two segment-cap configurations, the null raw result is robust and the register--topic decomposition replicates.

The protocol is portable to any research-policy interface that shares a category system and embeds both sides in a common semantic space, such as conservation science, health governance, or technology assessment. Its cross-sensitivity structure (Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}) is a reusable template for bounding uncertainty: instead of reporting a single gap ranking, it makes the stability of every position transparent across the measurement choices analysts control.

The framework does not solve the alignment problem. It advances measurement from Level~1 (lexical correspondence) to Level~2 (observed semantic proximity) of a three-level construct (Section~\ref{sec:alignment-framework}); semantic distance cannot show whether policy actors use research, whether epistemic communities exist, or whether divergence is productive or problematic. Level~3 remains the domain of qualitative, institutional, and deliberative methods. The AI-for-sustainability findings are a first application, not settled results.

\subsection{Retrieval and Query Chain}
\label{app:supp-retrieval}

The research corpus was retrieved from the OpenAlex API \parencite{Priem2022} using 68 queries formed by the cross-product of OpenAlex's native SDG work filter and four AI search terms (\texttt{artificial intelligence}, \texttt{machine learning}, \texttt{deep learning}, \texttt{neural network}), restricted to \texttt{publication\_year > 2017}. The 2018 start date reflects the post-AlexNet \parencite{NIPS2012_c399862d} emergence of deep learning as the dominant paradigm and a research-uptake lag after the SDGs' adoption in 2015. OpenAlex SDG metadata defines the candidate universe; the SDG assignments used in the analysis are produced by the independently validated supervised classifier (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}). Complete query construction and credential handling are implemented in \path{1_code/0_fetch/fetch_openalex.py}.

The retrieval boundary is a deliberate scope condition. Elsevier's data-driven characterisation of AI research draws on over 700 field-specific keywords across some 600,000 documents \parencite{Elsevier2019}, favouring recall. The OECD.AI Observatory methodology instead defines a paper as AI when it carries the Artificial Intelligence or Machine Learning field of study in the Microsoft Academic Graph (MAG), whose Fields of Study taxonomy OpenAlex later inherited and extended by mapping it to Wikidata identifiers \parencite{OECDAiMethod, Priem2022, Wang2020}. The present design prioritises precision: a four-term query keeps non-AI noise out of the embedding space at the cost of recall in specialised AI subfields. \textcite{CSET2023} reach the same conclusion for AI-research identification, namely that results are sensitive to the retrieval method and that supervised classification is preferred.

A direct test of this scope condition re-ran retrieval using field-of-study membership for the same two AI fields, mirroring the OECD.AI definition \parencite{OECDAiMethod}, and re-scored the resulting corpus through the identical pipeline. Concept retrieval used OpenAlex concept tags for Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning, each capped at \texttt{MAX\_PAPERS\_PER\_CONCEPT} = 25,000 papers, yielding the roughly 50,000-paper concept corpus. This scale sits on the empirical variance plateau established by the sample-stability ladder (Appendix~\ref{app:sample-stability}): at 50,000 papers the coverage-profile standard deviation is \SampleMacroVarianceFiftyK{} and the mean within-SDG semantic gap is \SampleMeanSemanticGapFiftyK{}, within 0.002 of the full-corpus estimate of \SampleMeanSemanticGapTwoM{} (full corpus N = \SampleStabilityFullCorpusN), while policy-text calibration bias has also converged (\SamplePolicyBiasFiftyK{}). The scale also exceeds every corpus size used in prior SDG classification and mapping benchmarks, namely the OSDG community dataset (30,534 texts; \textcite{Pukelis2022}), the SDG benchmark encoder evaluation corpus (35,001 papers; \textcite{Gjorgjevikj2025}), the SDGi corpus (20,267 segments; \textcite{SDGi2024}), the Aurora expert-validated set (4,971 texts; \textcite{Vanderfeesten2020Aurora}), and the 6,000-paper Kashnitsky et al.\ benchmark (\textcite{Kashnitsky2024}), while remaining far smaller than the full 3.1-million-paper corpus and thus a computationally tractable substrate for OpenAlex concept tagging.

\subsection{Segmentation Mechanics}
\label{app:supp-segmentation}

Each source document was segmented using the embedding model's own tokenizer: NLTK sentence-boundary detection (\texttt{sent\_tokenize}) is followed by greedy accumulation of sentences up to a token budget of \texttt{max\_seq\_length - 10} (margin 10, empirically verified to reduce truncation to near zero). Segments falling below 20 words (\texttt{MIN\_SEGMENT\_WORDS}) are discarded; a single sentence exceeding the token budget is kept whole because mid-sentence splitting is not permissible. For the UN General Debate Corpus, paragraph-level keyword filtering precedes segmentation, so only SDG-relevant speech passages enter the pipeline; this pre-filtering is why UNGDC's segment count (6,128) is much smaller than its source-speech count. OSDG and the SDG Classification Benchmark bypass segmentation because they already consist of short single-label texts. The reference corpora (Knowledge Hub, SDGi-labels, Aurora) and the policy sources are segmented with the same procedure. Segment identifiers encode source document identity for document-level weighting; cross-source deduplication by exact text match (SDGi retained first) yields \NPolicySegments{} unique policy segments.

\subsection{Truncation Fix}
\label{app:supp-truncation}

During pipeline development, a truncation issue was discovered and corrected: texts exceeding the encoder's maximum sequence length were being silently truncated before embedding, discarding tail content. The fix uses the model's own tokenizer during segmentation (Appendix~\ref{app:supp-segmentation}) to enforce a token budget of \texttt{max\_seq\_length - 10}, verified by the \texttt{verify\_truncation\_rate} utility to produce near-zero post-segmentation truncation. The full corpus was re-embedded after the fix; all reported results use post-fix embeddings.

\subsection{Reference-Corpus Provenance}
\label{app:supp-provenance}

The five reference corpora enter the pipeline at different sizes before preprocessing: OSDG 30,534 raw texts; SDG Classification Benchmark 616; IISD SDG Knowledge Hub 9,172; Aurora 4,022; SDGi 4,225 documents. After deduplication and English filtering, the pooled reference set feeds the single-label filter (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}), which retains only records with exactly one SDG label, yielding the per-source pool counts reported there (OSDG 30,532; SDGi 20,267; Knowledge Hub 6,122; Aurora 4,971; Benchmark 281) and the \NReferencePool{} total. OSDG and the Benchmark bypass segmentation, so their pool counts equal their deduplicated text counts; the other three corpora are segmented before pooling, which is why their pool counts are segment counts.

\subsection{Implications for Interpreting the Main Estimates}

These diagnostics are methodological stress tests that clarify the limits of the LR classifier approach, rather than failed repairs. The PCA diagnostics show that real-world SDG discourse is fuzzy rather than cleanly clustered, especially in the research corpus. The SDG 4 audit shows that the concern is real, but more nuanced than a pure machine-learning false positive. The policy source-family split shows that the full-corpus policy profile is a broader institutional SDG discourse rather than a universal AI policy profile. The canonical hard-threshold classifier assignment (argmax) is retained because it is transparent, reproducible, and directly interpretable.

These analyses also show that the LR classifier should be interpreted as a transparent semantic anchoring procedure rather than a definitive operational SDG system. Attempts to address multi-label overlap and corpus-register bias improve some aspects of the design but introduce new modelling choices. In this paper, the hard-threshold classifier assignment is therefore retained as the canonical specification, while the alternatives are reported as robustness and boundary-condition checks.

\section{Model Selection: Grid Search Protocol and Rationale}
\label{app:model-selection}

\subsection{Models Tested}

Three model families were evaluated under 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation (document-level grouping, not random splits) on the pooled training data (n=\NTrainPool{}). The test set (n=\NTestPool{}) was never touched during any grid search.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Logistic Regression (LR)}: Interpretable per-dimension coefficients per SDG; fast; standard multiclass baseline.
  \item \textbf{MLP (PyTorch)}: Can capture non-linear interactions in the embedding space.
  \item \textbf{RF / XGB}: Considered but excluded because the embedding space is already a learned representation  --  bagged trees add ensemble complexity without a clear mechanism for improvement over LR/MLP, and would not produce interpretable coefficients per dimension.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{LR Grid Search}

The LR grid searched C $\in \{100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01\}$ with L2 penalty. The best configuration was C=10.0, penalty=l2, class\_weight=None, solver=lbfgs (macro-F1 = \LrCvMacroFone{} $\pm$ \LrCvMacroFoneStd{} CV). An extended grid adding L1 penalty (l1\_ratio=1) and balanced class\_weight found no improvement: L1 was 6$\times$ slower and scored 0.8066, while balanced class\_weight scored 0.8070. The embedding space is dense  --  L1 sparsity offers no benefit  --  and the dual correction of weighted loss plus macro-averaged scoring degrades majority-class performance without compensating minority classes. C=10.0 and C=1.0 are essentially tied (difference $\sim$0.001), but C=10.0 was selected for conservatism.

\subsection{MLP Grid Search}

The MLP swept n\_layers $\in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$ $\times$ hidden\_size $\in \{256, 384\}$ (20 configurations), followed by a learning-rate sweep (lr $\in \{1\mathrm{e}{-4}, 3\mathrm{e}{-4}, 1\mathrm{e}{-3}, 3\mathrm{e}{-3}\}$) on the best architectures. The best MLP (4 layers, 384 hidden, lr=3e-4) achieved macro-F1 = \MlpCvMacroFone{} $\pm$ \MlpCvMacroFoneStd{}. Learning rate had negligible impact  --  all combinations within $\sim$0.003 of the best.

\noindent\textbf{Champion: LR (C=10.0, L2).} The gap between LR and MLP is \LrMlpGap{} macro-F1 ($\sim$1.5 pooled $\sigma$), a modest difference insufficient to justify the complexity trade-off. MLP requires 4 hidden layers, AdamW, learning rate tuning, early stopping, and dropout selection (36 configurations swept). LR requires one free parameter (C), no hidden layers, no dropout, no random seed sensitivity. For this study's purpose  --  building an interpretable supervised reference to compare corpora, not a classifier built to label single documents  --  LR is the right choice. LR provides 768 signed coefficients per SDG class, each directly attributable to a specific semantic dimension; MLP's hidden layers diffuse this information across non-linear transformations. The macro-F1 numbers are reported honestly: LR 0.811 $\pm$ 0.007, MLP 0.824 $\pm$ 0.006, gap $\sim$1.5$\sigma$. LR is chosen for transparency, not superior accuracy.

\subsection{Held-Out Test Evaluation of the MLP Robustness Check}

The MLP test macro-F1 (\MlpMacroFOne{}) cited in Section~\ref{sec:validation} is a separate evaluation from the CV grid-search results above. After the CV sweep, the champion MLP architecture (4 layers, 384 hidden, lr=3e-4) was retrained on the full training pool (n=\NTrainPool{}) and evaluated once against the held-out test set (n=\NTestPool{}), the same sanctioned single-use test evaluation applied to the LR champion. The resulting test macro-F1 of \MlpMacroFOne{} is not directly comparable to the CV mean of \MlpCvMacroFone{}~$\pm$~\MlpCvMacroFoneStd{} from the grid search: the CV number is an average across five document-grouped folds on the training pool alone, while the test number is a single evaluation on a disjoint held-out sample after retraining on the full pool. Both numbers confirm that the LR--MLP gap is modest and that architecture choice is not the binding constraint on classification quality.

\subsection{Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP) Register Removal: Convergence}
\label{app:register-inlp}

The full INLP procedure and identification argument are given in Methodology (Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap-method}, p.~\pageref{sec:semantic-gap-method}). This appendix reports only the convergence behaviour. The adjusted results are the canonical decomposition, not sensitivity diagnostics.

\paragraph{Convergence results.}
Held-out accuracy dropped from \RegisterFirstAcc{} (iteration~1) to \RegisterFinalAcc{} by iteration~\RegisterIterFinalK{}, at which point no further linear register separation was detectable. The Spearman rank correlation between the iteration~1 and iteration~\RegisterIterFinalK{} gap vectors was \RegisterIterSpearmanRho{}: the additional removed directions shifted the relative SDG ordering substantially. The mean gap compresses from \MeanRawGap{} to \MeanAdjustedGap{}, a reduction of \RegisterIterReductionPct{}\%, before plateauing. The Spearman $\rho$ decays rapidly in the first 15 iterations (1.000 to \RegisterIterRhoAtFifteen{}) and then stabilises around \RegisterIterPlateauRho{}, indicating that the first 15 orthogonal directions capture the bulk of the register signal.

The decomposition reveals a striking pattern for SDG~17 (Partnerships for the Goals). Under the raw centroid distance, SDG~17 shows the smallest gap (\RawGapSdgSeventeen{}, rank 17 of 17), suggesting near-identical framing. After register removal, the adjusted gap is \AdjGapSdgSeventeen{} --- the largest adjusted gap in the corpus (rank 1). The register component is \RegCompSdgSeventeen{}, the only negative value, indicating that register actually \emph{increases} the semantic similarity for this policy-dominant SDG: the policy discourse on partnerships uses register conventions that make it appear closer to research than the underlying topic content warrants. For all other SDGs, the register component is positive (range: \RegCompSdgFifteen{} to \RegCompSdgThirteen{}), confirming that register uniformly inflates the raw gap. The headline correlation pattern confirms the decomposition: the raw coverage-vs-gap correlation is near-zero ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, $p = 0.765$) because the register component ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister$, $p = 0.045$) cancels the topic signal ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic$, $p = 0.054$; Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}).
