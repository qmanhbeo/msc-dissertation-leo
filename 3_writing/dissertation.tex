\begin{abstract}
 Shared SDG labels do not mean research and policy \emph{frame} a goal similarly. This paper contributes a measurement framework that separates research-policy divergence into two dimensions: coverage (which SDGs each corpus prioritises) and within-SDG semantic framing (how differently the same SDG is discussed). Framing is composed of topic (substantive content) and register (communicative conventions).

 The paper embeds \NReferencePool{} labelled reference texts, \NResearchSegments{} research segments, and \NPolicySegments{} policy segments with a frozen Sentence-BERT encoder. Labelled segments are used to train a single-label logistic regression classifier to score research and policy text segments. The study then trains an SDG-stratified research-policy LR classifier to subtract the linear directions that separate research and policy from the original semantic space using Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP, \textcite{Ravfogel2020INLP}). What remains is called the adjusted semantic space, interpreted as containing the substantive topic (Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}). Texts are re-scored in this adjusted space. The difference between the raw and adjusted embeddings is called the register component.

 For each SDG in each corpus, coverage is its share within the corpus and semantic centroid is the mean value of the embeddings labelled that SDG. For each SDG, coverage gap is the coverage difference between the two corpora and raw semantic gap is the cosine distance between research and policy centroids. Adjusted semantic gaps are computed analogously on the projected embeddings, and the register component is the difference between the raw and adjusted gaps. Correlation tests between H1a) absolute coverage gap, H1b) signed dominance, H1c) research coverage, H1d) policy coverage and within-SDG semantic gaps (raw, adjusted, register) are calculated with two-sided Monte Carlo permutation p-values (100,000 resamples).

 The SDG classifier achieves macro-F1 = \MacroFOne{} on a held-out test set and reproduces the high research coverage of SDGs 3, 4, and 16 reported in prior inventories; SDG 9's second place is plausibly an artefact of the AI-focused retrieval. The policy-side ranking (top SDGs: 16, 17, and 13) is reported here. The raw coverage-semantic correlation is null (\(\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw\), p = \SpearmanCovRawP{}). However, the adjusted semantic gap is positively associated with coverage (\(\rho = \RhoCovTopic\), p = \RhoCovTopicP), whereas the register component has a negative, non-significant association (\(\rho = \RhoCovRegister\), p = \RhoCovRegisterP). Policy coverage is the strongest directional predictor of the adjusted semantic gap (\(\rho = \HOneDPolicyCovAdjRho\), p = \HOneDPolicyCovAdjRhoP{}), while research coverage shows no consistent signal (\(\rho = \HOneDResearchCovAdjRho\), p = \HOneDResearchCovAdjRhoP{}).

 These results are stable across encoders, classifier family, retrieval strategy, segment cap, and policy-source family (source families agree on the raw-gap pattern; Section~\ref{sec:interaction}), apart from one sign flip in the concept-retrieved track (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). To conclude, coverage and framing should be measured and reported as separate axes of divergence. Shared labels are insufficient evidence of shared framing.
\end{abstract}

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} research-policy alignment; Sustainable Development Goals; semantic similarity; transformer embeddings; measurement protocol; bibliometrics.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Research-policy alignment is often inferred from whether scientific and policy texts address the same topics \parencite{bornmann2016policy}, but shared coverage does not mean comparable framing. Research and policy rarely even co-occur on a topic to begin with: only 1.2\% of 191,276 climate-change publications carry at least one policy-document mention \parencite{bornmann2016policy}, and even where research and policy communities share terminology, they diverge in term salience and focal concerns \parencite{ToneyWails2024}. A paper and a policy document can both be labelled the same SDG while emphasising different problems, actors, and interventions. Legislative agendas, ESG disclosures, and funding decisions increasingly treat SDG tags as evidence of alignment \parencite{Heilinger2023, HerasSaizarbitoria2022} while this assumption is rarely tested directly.

Existing methods for mapping research to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) operate mainly at the level of lexical or bibliometric correspondence: keyword dictionaries, Boolean queries, or citation and mention counts \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. Broader AI-SDG assessments compound this, resting on expert opinion or publication counts rather than on the texts themselves \parencite{Vinuesa2020}. They measure whether a text is about a SDG instead of how it frames it.

Coverage gaps like this are not AI-specific: in conservation science, 59\% of policy-prioritised topics appear in fewer than 15 papers \parencite{McCarthy2026}. Framing gaps are not AI-specific either: corporate AI research concentrates on pre-deployment problems, leaving the deployment-impact space policy must address \parencite{Strauss2025}, and global AI metrics emphasise technical/economic performance where policy stresses ethical/social dimensions \parencite{SioumalasChristodoulou2025}.

This paper proposes that research-policy alignment has at least two separable dimensions: coverage divergence (which SDGs each corpus prioritises) and semantic framing divergence (how differently they discuss the same SDG). Its contribution is a measurement framework. Applied to the AI-SDG scientific and policy literature, this framework advances such mapping from Level~1 (lexical correspondence) to Level~2 (observed textual-semantic proximity in embedding space; Section~\ref{sec:alignment-framework}). A novel SDG-stratified INLP adaptation separates register from topic.

The central research question is: To what extent do academic AI-for-sustainability research and policy discourse differ in SDG coverage and semantic framing across the 17 SDGs, and how are these two dimensions related?

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{Previous AI-SDG Bibliometrics}\label{sec:prev-biblio}

Existing assessments of AI and the SDGs rest on expert views or publication counts rather than on the texts themselves \parencite{Vinuesa2020}. Broader SDG scientometrics and the AI-specific inventories converge on one robust finding: SDG~3 (Good Health) and SDG~7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) are the most addressed goals in the AI-specific studies, followed by SDG~4 (Quality Education), SDG~13 (Climate Action), SDG~11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), and SDG~16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions). The evidence base spans several inventories, including a citation-based corpus of roughly 25,000 publications on the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals \parencite{BautistaPuig2021} and 792 AI-and-SDG research articles \parencite{Gohr2025AiSdgs}. Still, none of these studies measures within-SDG semantic framing.

SDG publication mapping depends on the methods used. \textcite{Armitage2020} shows that what counts as SDG-relevant depends on interpretive choices about queries, labels, and databases. \textcite{Kashnitsky2024} reach the same caution from a broader comparison of Boolean, machine-learning, and hybrid mapping systems, finding no single best approach and no single ``golden'' validation set. The dependence persists in AI-based mapping: keyword baselines still omit publications that never state an SDG keyword explicitly, so the reported corpus boundary remains query-dependent \parencite{Yin2025SdgMapping}. The embedding-based approach used in this paper reduces some vocabulary-level brittleness of keyword dictionaries, but its results are still conditional on corpus construction, which centroids are used, and model choice.

The SDGs themselves are interdependent by design, yet no operational framework specifies how to manage these interdependencies \parencite{Nilsson2016}; SDG~17 is the partial exception. \textcite{LeBlanc2015}, in a network analysis of the proposed SDGs, treats SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals) as the dedicated goal for cross-cutting means of implementation (finance, trade, technology transfer, capacity building) and excludes these from cross-goal mapping to focus on links between thematic areas. Unlike every other SDG, whose discourse more likely centres on a shared substantive topic, SDG 17 policy discourse concerns coordinating and resourcing implementation itself, a conceptual space that AI research rarely addresses. Single-SDG assignment used in this paper is therefore a simplifying assumption.

\subsection{Research-Policy Alignment in AI and Sustainability}\label{sec:rp-alignment}

A growing literature documents structural tensions between what AI research produces and what sustainability policy requires. Corporate AI research concentrates on pre-deployment technical problems, creating a gap between research priorities and the deployment impacts governance must address \parencite{Strauss2025}. Global AI metrics emphasise technical and economic performance when policy discourse stresses ethical and social dimensions aligned with SDG concerns \parencite{SioumalasChristodoulou2025}. Research and policy communities can share trustworthy-AI terminology while differing in term salience and focal concerns \parencite{ToneyWails2024}. The pattern extends beyond AI: in conservation science, 59\% of policy-prioritised research topics appear in fewer than 15 papers \parencite{McCarthy2026}.

Adjacent scientometric work measures research-policy connection through policy-document mentions rather than semantic framing and still finds only 1.2\% of 191,276 climate-change publications have at least one policy mention \parencite{bornmann2016policy}.

Theory explains why such divergence is the default rather than a failure. Knowledge systems support action when they produce information that is simultaneously salient, credible, and legitimate \parencite{Cash2003}. Because these are properties of how information is framed and packaged rather than of which topics it covers, they motivate measuring framing alongside coverage instead of only topical presence. The epistemic-communities framework explains why shared framing matters for policy coordination under uncertainty: expert communities influence policy by articulating cause effect relationships and framing issues for collective debate \parencite{Haas1992}. Haas's concept is broader than semantic proximity, involving shared normative and causal beliefs, validity criteria, and a common policy enterprise; the present study therefore measures only the weaker condition of observed semantic proximity, not an operationalised epistemic community.

The benchmark itself is not neutral. The SDG framework is a politically negotiated instrument with documented limitations: goals can create incentives to over-focus on target achievement \parencite{FukudaParr2016}, summit documents remain state-centric and avoid causal responsibility \parencite{BexellJonsson2017}. Reported SDG engagement is frequently superficial, so reported alignment should not be conflated with substantive commitment \parencite{Heilinger2023, HerasSaizarbitoria2022}. To the extent that research follows Mode 1 knowledge production (problems set within academic disciplines) rather than Mode 2 (problems arising in contexts of application) \parencite{Gibbons1994}, divergence is not uniformly problematic: where research explores possibilities that policy discourse has not yet absorbed, distance may reflect productive exploration rather than failure of alignment.

\subsection{A Three-Level Framework of Research-Policy Alignment}\label{sec:alignment-framework}

This paper proposes that research-policy alignment be understood as a three-level construct rather than a binary property of shared labels:

\textbf{Level 1: lexical or bibliometric correspondence.} The dominant mode of existing SDG-mapping work: assigning texts to goals through keyword dictionaries, Boolean queries, or citation and mention counts \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. This level is criticised: shared vocabulary does not guarantee shared meaning \parencite{bornmann2016policy}, and keyword methods are brittle to vocabulary and database choices \parencite{Armitage2020}.

\textbf{Level 2: observed textual-semantic proximity in embedding space.} Sentence embeddings locate texts in a shared semantic space where proximity reflects distributional patterns of co-occurrence \parencite{Harris1954, Reimers2019}, and encoder-based SDG association has been benchmarked as a strong baseline \parencite{Gjorgjevikj2025}. This level detects divergence that keyword methods miss. It also makes divergence structurally visible: observed proximity decomposes into separable components (coverage versus framing, and topic versus register within framing), and because the decomposition operates on the texts themselves, its findings can be reproduced and tested rather than merely judged (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}). The present study is positioned at this level, and the limits of that measurement are developed in Section~\ref{sec:semantic-methods} below.

\textbf{Level 3: substantive comparison of implied problems, actors, and interventions.} Whether the two discourses address the same problems, involve the same actors, and propose the same interventions \parencite{Cash2003, Guston2001, Haas1992}. Because this requires interpreting causal beliefs, legitimacy, and institutional mechanism \parencite{Caplan1979, Haas1992}, it lies beyond unsupervised text analysis and is left to qualitative follow-up.

Advancing from Level 1 to Level 2 is the study's main methodological contribution; Level 3 is not addressed. Section~\ref{sec:level0} discusses a reference-relative precondition below these levels. Figure~\ref{fig:conceptual-framework} illustrates this decomposition.

\subsection{Semantic Methods: Rationale and Cautions}\label{sec:semantic-methods}

 The distributional hypothesis, that meaning can be described through patterns of linguistic co-occurrence \parencite{Harris1954}, motivates the use of Sentence-BERT \parencite{Reimers2019} and related transformer encoders for large-scale semantic comparison. However, using them to compare research abstracts and policy documents is new and not yet directly benchmarked. The distance is descriptive rather than causal.

 \textcite{Gjorgjevikj2025} benchmarked sentence encoders specifically for SDG association tasks, finding that general-purpose models from the sentence-transformers family perform among the strongest as baselines, and that domain-specific fine-tuning further improves predictive performance while reducing sensitivity to description length. \textcite{Rodriguez2023}'s conText framework provides a computational-social-science precedent for comparing meaning across contexts, adopting a narrow, structuralist view of meaning as distributional co-occurrence and making no causal claims about human understanding. This study follows that same descriptive premise: semantic proximity is a distributional measurement rather than evidence of substantive framing alignment. The implication is that embedding distance mixes topic, register, and contextual convention into a single number: distributional representations are not topic-only, since language models inherently encode varieties of language including register \parencite{Grieve2025}, and stylistic variation is demonstrably present in distributional vector spaces \parencite{NiuCarpuat2017}. This motivates the decomposition into these components (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}).

 \textcite{McCarthy2026} supply a closer empirical precedent, showing with a multi-target SciBERT classifier that research-policy coverage gaps are measurable with transformer encoders outside the AI-SDG setting. This study extends that logic from a single conservation domain to all 17 SDGs, and from coverage to within-SDG semantic framing.

 An older corpus-linguistic tradition adds a further caution. Biber's multidimensional (MD) analysis identifies six co-varying dimensions of linguistic variation across 23 spoken and written genres, derived from factor analysis of 67 linguistic features \parencite{biber1988variation}. Each dimension captures a continuum of co-occurring linguistic features rather than a binary speech/writing split \parencite{biber1988variation, biberConrad2009register}. Register is therefore a multi-layered property: two texts can differ on some dimensions and converge on others. INLP removes a linear slice of the embedding space. What it isolates is the largest single direction of writing-style difference. It is not claimed to capture every aspect of register, which is multi-dimensional. For the present study this matters because research abstracts and policy documents may differ not only in substantive topic, but also in abstraction, modality, institutional stance, and rhetorical function. Embedding distance is therefore treated as observed textual-semantic proximity rather than a clean measure of topic alone.

\subsection{Research Gap and Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}

The literature reviewed above has not produced (i) a systematic comparison of how academic AI-for-sustainability research and policy discourse differ in SDG coverage and within-SDG semantic framing --- existing work measures research coverage, citation patterns, or policy-document uptake, but does not compare the two corpora directly on these dimensions --- or (ii) an examination of how coverage gaps and semantic gaps are related. This framework is diagnostic. It maps where observed divergence is, without classifying whether any given gap reflects productive or problematic disconnection. The latter would require qualitative follow-up beyond the scope of embedding analysis.

The research question is operationalised by four sub-hypotheses, each correlating a distinct coverage predictor with the within-SDG semantic gap across the 17 SDGs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1a} (motivating hypothesis): the coverage gap is positively associated with the semantic gap;
  \item \textbf{H1b}: signed dominance (research share $-$ policy share) $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap;
  \item \textbf{H1c}: research coverage $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap;
  \item \textbf{H1d}: policy coverage $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap.
\end{itemize}
H1b--H1d are signed tests of directional components: with dominance defined as research share minus policy share, the sign of each association locates which community's framing norms drive the linkage, per the epistemic-communities direction-of-dominance argument. The operationalisation of these tests, Pearson $r$ and Spearman $\rho$ between each predictor and semantic-gap scores including the $n = 17$ scope conditions, is described in Section~\ref{sec:coverage-semantic-interaction}.

These contributions and hypotheses are motivated by the theoretical frameworks reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:rp-alignment}. The two-communities theory holds that researchers and policymakers occupy separate worlds with different values, reward systems, and languages \parencite{Caplan1979}. If these communities also develop different topical priorities, as the empirical record of research-policy divergence suggests \parencite{Strauss2025, McCarthy2026}, then coverage divergence and semantic divergence should co-occur: SDGs where one community dominates attention should also show divergent framing, because the community shaping the discussion brings its own communicative norms. The epistemic-communities framework further distinguishes the direction of dominance: where research prioritises a given SDG, the framing should reflect academic norms of knowledge production; where policy prioritises it, the framing should reflect institutional norms of coordination \parencite{Haas1992}. H1a tests for general coverage-semantic relationships. H1b--H1d decompose the coverage-semantic relationship into these directional components.
\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This study uses publicly available text data and requires no ethical approval.

\subsection{Token-Aware Segmentation}\label{sec:segmentation}

 All source documents are segmented at sentence boundaries using the embedding model's (Section~\ref{sec:embedding-choice}) own tokenizer to enforce a hard token budget. The procedure applies identically to research abstracts, policy documents, and reference corpora: NLTK sentence-boundary detection is followed by greedy accumulation of sentences up to a token budget of \texttt{max\_seq\_length} $-$ 10 (for the canonical MPNet encoder: 384 $-$ 10 = 374 tokens). Segments falling below 20 words are discarded.

 For the research--policy comparison this design has two consequences. First, research abstracts (mean $\sim$200 words) mostly fit within the 374-token budget, but \NResearchMultiSegPct{}\% exceed it and split into multiple segments; the research-side text unit is therefore the abstract, whose segments are aggregated to a single document vector before assignment, not the individual segment. Second, policy documents of variable length are split into multiple segments of comparable size to research abstracts, making the two corpora directly comparable in embedding space.

\subsection{Embedding Model and Normalisation}\label{sec:embedding-choice}

All texts were encoded with \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} \parencite{all-mpnet-base-v2, Reimers2019} from the sentence-transformers library, producing 768-dimensional dense vectors. Embeddings were L2-normalised to unit vectors so that cosine similarity equals their dot product.

No fine-tuning is used. The embeddings are frozen. A linear classifier on frozen embeddings, well-known as linear probing, is the standard way to test what a representation already captures \parencite{alain2018understandingintermediatelayersusing, conneau2018cramsinglevectorprobing}. Both the SDG classifier (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}) and the INLP step (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}) are linear operations on this fixed space. Freezing also keeps the two corpora in one shared space. Retraining on the SDG data (which skews toward policy words) would distort that space.

Encoder choice is a primary robustness axis: gap rankings are re-computed under two alternative encoders, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \parencite{all-minilm-l6-v2} (384-dimensional) and SciBERT \parencite{Beltagy2019} (768-dimensional). Encoder-robustness checks use the same reference (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}) and policy corpora (Section~\ref{sec:policy-corpus}), but only 100,000 research abstracts drawn once with a fixed seed from the full research-abstract corpus (Section~\ref{sec:research-corpus}) to save computation. This is motivated by the finding that the semantic gap (Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap-method}) becomes stable compared to the full-corpus value within 100 random re-samples at around the size of 50,000 abstracts (Appendix~\ref{app:sample-stability}). This sample is larger than the corpora used in prior SDG-classification studies (Section~\ref{sec:prev-biblio}).

\subsection{Research Corpus}\label{sec:research-corpus}

 The research corpus consists of \NResearchAbstracts{} research abstracts retrieved from the OpenAlex API \parencite{Priem2022}, covering publications between 2018 and 2025.

 Retrieval combined OpenAlex's native SDG work filter with four high-precision AI search terms (\texttt{artificial intelligence}, \texttt{machine learning}, \texttt{deep learning}, \texttt{neural network}), giving 68 queries (17 SDGs $\times$ 4 terms). The term set draws on established practice for delimiting AI corpora \parencite{Elsevier2019, OECDAiMethod}, capturing AI-related work without trying to catch every AI term. Because this study measures semantic alignment between research and policy rather than counting publications, the search favours precision over recall: each term must clearly mark AI work, accepting that some papers are missed, to keep non-AI papers out of the embedding space. \textcite{CSET2023} reach the same precision-over-recall conclusion for AI-research identification, finding results sensitive to retrieval method and favouring supervised classification. The `learning' terms plausibly inflate the SDG 4 share. This is examined in Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}.

 A direct test re-ran retrieval using field-of-study membership for the same two AI fields/concepts of ``AI'' and ``ML'' \parencite{OECDAiMethod} and re-scored the resulting 100,000-paper corpus through its own segmentation (Section~\ref{sec:segmentation}) and classifier (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}), but reuses the register-adjusted space from the main research corpus (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}), assuming research-policy register directions are unchanged in the same semantic space, regardless of how the corpus was retrieved. Coverage (Section~\ref{sec:coverage-gap-method}) and semantic-gap (Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap-method}) rankings are stable under this alternative strategy. Results are reported mainly in Section~\ref{sec:interaction}. For diagnostics of detailed differences between the two retrieval strategies, see Appendix~\ref{app:concept-retrieval-sensitivity}.

 Importantly, the robustness check applies no SDG filter at retrieval time and is therefore a pure AI/ML corpus with SDG labels assigned post hoc by the classifier, not a corpus independently verified as SDG-relevant (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

  After deduplication and removal of records lacking usable abstracts and the 20-word minimum, the research corpus survives as \NResearchAbstracts{} abstracts, tokenised into segments for embedding (record and segment counts in Table~\ref{tab:corpus-provenance}). The corpus is predominantly English (99.5\%) but no hard language filter is applied. The main results reproduce under subsampling from 50,000 papers (Appendix~\ref{app:sample-stability}), two orders of magnitude larger than the non-English share. No balancing by SDG or citation count is applied.

\subsection{Policy Corpus}\label{sec:policy-corpus}

The policy corpus contains text segments from three collections, representing distinct source documents (segment and document counts per collection in Table~\ref{tab:corpus-provenance}). It should be read as mixed international institutional SDG policy discourse rather than every AI-sustainability policy that exists:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Curated AI and SDG policy documents}: UN, OECD, EU, UK, and AU AI governance frameworks; IPCC assessment reports; SDSN sustainable development reports; and national AI strategies.
  \item \textbf{SDGi Voluntary National Review and Voluntary Local Review corpus} \parencite{SDGi2024}: National and local self-assessments submitted to the UN High-Level Political Forum.
  \item \textbf{UN General Debate Corpus} \parencite{JankinEtAl2024}: SDG-relevant passages from General Debate speeches, sessions 70--80 (2015--2024); paragraph-level keyword filtering precedes segmentation, so only SDG-relevant passages enter the pipeline, which is why its segment count is far fewer than the full corpus of speeches.
\end{enumerate}
The SDGi VNR/VLR collection is also one of the five supervised reference corpora (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}), so its policy segments carry gold SDG labels.
An asymmetry follows: the research corpus is an intersection of AI and SDG content while the policy corpus is a broader union covering institutional SDG discourse and AI governance. The curated sub-corpus is the more relevant reference for AI-governance-specific interpretation; Appendix~\ref{app:policy-source-data} reports a source-family sensitivity check.

\subsection{Supervised Reference Classifier}\label{sec:supervised-classifier}

The canonical instrument is a supervised classifier trained on pooled single-label reference data from five independent annotated corpora. Corpus sizes at each pipeline stage---raw, preprocessed + de-duplicated, segmented, and single-label---are reported in Table~\ref{tab:corpus-provenance}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OSDG Community Dataset} (OSDG) \parencite{Pukelis2022} (no SDG 17): crowdsourced excerpts validated by multiple volunteers.
  \item \textbf{SDG Classification Benchmark} (SDGCB) \parencite{SDGBenchmark} (expert-verified): expert-annotated snippets with consensus resolution.
  \item \textbf{IISD SDG Knowledge Hub} (SDGKH) \parencite{Wulff2023SDGKnowledgeHub}: multi-label journalism and policy commentary on 2030 Agenda implementation.
  \item \textbf{SDGi Corpus} (SDGi) \parencite{SDGi2024}: government Voluntary National and Local Reviews (VNRs/LRs).
  \item \textbf{Aurora dataset} (Aurora) \parencite{Vanderfeesten2020Aurora} (expert-validated): academic abstracts validated by domain researchers.
\end{itemize}

OSDG alone contributes about 49\% of labelled texts and draws on UN-adjacent documents, so the reference data remain weighted toward policy vocabulary. OSDG and SDGCB bypass segmentation due to having short texts. SDGKH, SDGi, and Aurora are segmented before pooling.

The pooled reference data were split into training and held-out test sets by stratified document-grouped splitting (pool size and split are reported in Table~\ref{tab:corpus-provenance}), keeping all texts from the same source document in one fold to enforce strict separation between training and evaluation. The champion LR ($C = 3.0$, L2 penalty, \texttt{class\_weight=None}, solver \texttt{lbfgs}) was selected by 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation \parencite{Hastie2009} on the training pool; the grid-search protocol and hyperparameter rationale are given in Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}, and the MLP robustness comparison in Appendix~\ref{app:model-selection}.

 The validation task does not evaluate a rejection option for non-SDG texts. This is acceptable for the primary corpus, which is restricted to an OpenAlex AI-and-SDG universe so the classifier answers a relative question among the 17 SDGs; the concept-retrieved robustness corpus lifts that restriction (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

Each text is assigned to its highest-probability SDG (argmax), traceable to 768 signed coefficients per class with no hidden layers, no dropout, and no random-seed sensitivity. Macro-F1, the unweighted mean of per-SDG scores, is the primary validation metric (See results in Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

Because the classifier is trained on pooled reference data that are themselves policy-adjacent, the scoring instrument behaves differently on the two corpora. Policy segments receive systematically higher LR predicted probabilities than research abstracts (mean \AffFifteenPolicyScore{} vs \AffFifteenPaperScore{}). The gap has two causes the data cannot separate: a genre artefact (the reference data skew toward policy vocabulary) or a substantive difference (policy texts address SDG implementation more directly). Either way, hard argmax assignment depends only on each text's within-corpus probability ranking across the 17 SDGs, not on absolute probability levels. As a result, the asymmetry threatens the findings only if it shifts SDGs unevenly within a corpus rather than uniformly.

Two supervised model families were compared: logistic regression, which exposes interpretable per-dimension coefficients, and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). Random forests and gradient boosting were excluded because the embedding space is already a learned representation, so bagged trees add ensemble complexity without a mechanism for improvement or per-dimension interpretability.

The LR grid searched $C \in \{0.1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 30, 100\}$ with an L2 penalty and \texttt{class\_weight} $\in$ \{\texttt{None}, \texttt{balanced}\}. The MLP grid swept $n_{\text{layers}} \in \{2, 3, 4, 6\} \times \text{hidden\_size} \in \{256, 384\} \times \text{lr} \in \{3\mathrm{e}{-4}, 1\mathrm{e}{-3}\}$ with \texttt{weight\_decay}=0, \texttt{dropout}=0.3, AdamW, and early stopping.

For robustness, a zero-shot nearest centroid assignment method (hereafter ZS) was also considered. This method builds the reference SDG centroids by aggregating the labelled reference texts, and unlabelled texts are assigned to the nearest SDG centroid in the embedding space.

Finally, the model of choice is LR because the MLP's performance gain is negligible relative to the interpretability it loses. Grid search and test results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:validation}.

\subsection{Coverage Gap Analysis}\label{sec:coverage-gap-method}

For each corpus item, the predicted SDG is the LR argmax (hard assignment, Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}); the coverage profile is the proportion of analysis units assigned to each SDG (an abstract for research, a source document for policy).

The policy corpus comprises \NPolicyDocs{} source documents of very different lengths (\NPolicySegments{} segments in total; Section~\ref{sec:policy-corpus}). A segment-weighted profile would let a handful of long reports dominate the count, so the analysis instead computes a document-weighted profile in which every source document counts equally regardless of length, following the standard centroid formulation in which each document contributes one length-normalised unit vector \parencite{Manning2008}. In practice: segment vectors are averaged per document, hard-assigned to the top SDG, and the coverage profile counts documents (not segments) per SDG. The research corpus uses the same logic at the paper level: each of the \NResearchAbstracts{} abstracts is represented by the mean of its segments, hard-assigned as a single document unit, so the research-side text unit is the abstract and the two corpora are weighted identically.

The coverage gap for SDG $j$ is defined as:
\[
  \text{CoverageGap}_j = \left|\text{ResearchCoverage}_j - \text{PolicyCoverage}_j\right|
\]

The signed version of the same quantity is:
\[
  \text{SignedCoverageGap}_j = \text{ResearchCoverage}_j - \text{PolicyCoverage}_j
\]
A positive value means research dominates SDG $j$. A negative value means policy dominates.

\subsection{Semantic Gap Analysis}\label{sec:semantic-gap-method}

The semantic gap measures within-SDG divergence: given that both corpora have texts assigned to SDG $j$, how differently do they discuss it?

For each SDG $j$ the analysis collects all assigned research papers and all assigned policy segments (capped at 50 per source document to bound single-document influence). The two corpora are aggregated at different text units here by design, unlike the coverage profile (Section~\ref{sec:coverage-gap-method}), which weights both corpora at the document level. Research abstracts are single-topic by genre convention, so each of the \NResearchAbstracts{} abstracts contributes exactly one unit vector, the L2-renormalised mean of its segment embeddings, and a 33-segment abstract and a single-segment abstract carry equal weight: the research centroid $\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j$ is the L2-normalised mean of these paper-level unit vectors assigned to SDG~$j$. Policy sources, by contrast, are long, deliberately excerpted institutional texts (UNGDC speeches, SDGi VNRs) whose passages are topically distinct and may span several SDGs. Weighting each source document equally would collapse that within-document variation into a single point. The policy centroid $\mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j$ is therefore the L2-normalised mean of the (capped) segment embeddings assigned to SDG~$j$, sampling the topical content of each passage rather than treating each report as one undifferentiated unit.

The semantic gap for each SDG is then defined as:
\[
  \text{SemanticGap}_j = 1 - (\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j \cdot \mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j)
\]

A gap of 0 indicates perfectly overlapping semantic directions. A gap approaching 1 indicates orthogonal framing. Cosine similarity between centroids is the standard method for document-level semantic comparison in embedding space \parencite{vorderbruck2019text, Babic2020}. Centroid cosine distance collapses each embedding cloud to its mean direction.

Appendix~\ref{app:distributional-table} shows the adjusted-gap ranking correlates moderately with most full-distribution alternatives ($\rho = 0.444$--$0.686$), while Gaussian-2-Wasserstein, squared MMD, Sinkhorn, C2ST, and Grassmann show weak or negative agreement ($\rho \leq 0.260$), confirming the centroid estimate captures a dominant signal while distribution shape also carries signal. An alternative reading is that weak agreement from five independent distribution-aware measures reflects a real limitation of the mean-direction centroid rather than a merely complementary signal; the present study cannot distinguish between these readings and reports both distribution-aware and centroid results (Appendix~\ref{app:distributional-table}) so readers can judge for themselves. However, further analysis with those distance measures is left for future work.

The per-document segment cap of $K = 50$ (seeded, reproducible) bounds a single document's contribution to at most 50 segments, mitigating the influence of very long reports on the policy centroid; sensitivity at $K = 20$ and no cap is reported in Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}, Appendix~\ref{app:cross-method-section}.

\subsection{Register Adjustment via Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP)}\label{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}

The semantic gap is not a clean measure of substantive disagreement. Research and policy texts differ in both topic (what they discuss) and register (how they are written -- audience, purpose, style). Embedding distance mixes the two. INLP \parencite{Ravfogel2020INLP} is used in this paper to roughly split the gap into an adjusted semantic gap (topic) and a register component (See results in Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap}).

\paragraph{Objective.}
INLP learns directions that, when subtracted from the embeddings, erase the research--policy difference. After $K$ iterations, held-out corpus-classification accuracy is at chance on the stratified training distribution; a residual corpus signal remains detectable out of sample (Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}). Each iteration removes the direction carrying the most remaining corpus signal.

\paragraph{SDG-stratified procedure.}
Vanilla INLP removes a binary attribute (e.g.\ gender). The present adaptation must remove register, a corpus-level property confounded with SDG topic in the raw embeddings. The modification is SDG stratification: at each iteration, equal numbers of research and policy embeddings are drawn from each of the 17 SDGs, so the classifier cannot use topic as a stand-in for register. The procedure proceeds as follows at each iteration $k = 1,\ldots,K$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stratified sample.} Independently redraw $n_{\text{target}}$ texts per SDG per corpus (without replacement from the full pool; global-minimum rule: $n_{\text{target}} = \min_j \min(|X_j^{\text{res}}|, |X_j^{\text{pol}}|)$; under the frozen snapshot, $n_{\text{target}} = \RegisterIterNPerSdg{}$ for MPNet). Form the 34-class stratification key $z_{\text{strat}} = 2 \times \text{sdg\_labels} + y$, ensuring balanced corpus $\times$ topic combinations.
  \item \textbf{Project existing directions.} Subtract from each sample embedding the components along all previously learned directions: $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \sum_{j<k} (\mathbf{g}_j \cdot \mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{g}_j$.
  \item \textbf{Train classifier.} Fit L2-regularised logistic regression to predict corpus identity $y$ from the projected embeddings $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$. The hyperparameters are fixed defaults, not validated, because the objective is to recover a decodable direction, not to maximise classification accuracy. This means the quality of any single iteration's direction is not individually verified; only the aggregate stopping criterion (held-out accuracy $\leq 0.5$ across all $K$ iterations) guards against a degenerate or noisy direction being retained. A per-iteration validation pass is not performed and is left for future refinement. Split 15\% held-out, stratified by $z_{\text{strat}}$.
  \item \textbf{Extract direction.} Normalise the weight vector: $\mathbf{g}_k = \mathbf{w}_k / \|\mathbf{w}_k\|$. Orthogonalise against all prior directions: $\mathbf{g}_k \leftarrow \mathbf{g}_k - \sum_{j<k} (\mathbf{g}_k \cdot \mathbf{g}_j)\,\mathbf{g}_j$. If $\|\mathbf{g}_k\| < 10^{-8}$ (direction collapse), stop.
  \item \textbf{Stopping criterion.} Stop if held-out accuracy $\leq 0.5$ (the majority-class baseline for balanced binary classification). Otherwise append $\mathbf{g}_k$ and continue.
\end{enumerate}
All three encoder tracks converge within \RegisterCrossMinilmIters{}--\RegisterCrossScibertIters{} iterations (Appendix~\ref{app:register-robustness}). The final adjusted embedding is $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} - \sum_{k=1}^{K} (\mathbf{g}_k \cdot \mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{g}_k$, and the adjusted gap uses centroids computed on these projected embeddings.

 Identification argument. The stratification design is the structural precondition: because every iteration trains on equal numbers of research and policy embeddings from each SDG, no linear direction can simultaneously encode SDG topic and corpus identity. What remains linearly decodable is the corpus-level register difference: modality, audience conventions, institutional stance, and structural patterns. The removed subspace is interpreted as register in the sense of \textcite{biberConrad2009register}: a variety associated with a particular situation of use, including communicative purpose, audience, and production circumstances. Stratification controls for SDG topic, but not for other differences such as publication period or author background. The removed component is therefore best read as register-like, not as pure register. This interpretation is evaluated against independent linguistic markers in Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}.

 With the projector learned, the semantic-gap analysis of Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap-method} is recomputed on the adjusted embeddings, giving the within-SDG adjusted gap.

\subsection{Coverage--Semantic Interaction}\label{sec:coverage-semantic-interaction}

The test works as follows. Pearson $r$ and Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ are computed between each coverage predictor (coverage gap, signed dominance, research coverage, policy coverage) and the within-SDG semantic-gap scores across the 17 SDGs; SDG~4 is treated as artefact-affected (Section~\ref{sec:research-corpus}) and examined separately (Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}).

A possible criticism is that $n = 17$ limits statistical power. However, the 17 SDGs are the whole framework, not a sample. Finer-grained units (the framework defines 169 targets) would raise the observation count, but no labelled target-level reference data exist. That is left for future work when data is available.

The only randomness comes from measurement (classifier, corpus build, embeddings). All $p$-values are two-sided Monte Carlo permutation tests (100,000 resamples, fixed seed). They test the null of no association given the measured values, without relying on asymptotic approximations \parencite{BerkWesternWeiss1995, AbadieAtheyImbensWooldridge2020}.

With only 17 units, a single goal can sway the result, so the associations are checked with a leave-one-out analysis (Section~\ref{sec:interaction}) and across configurations (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).

Raw, adjusted, and register-component correlations come from one decomposition (register = raw $-$ adjusted). They move together, so the claim is about their combined pattern, not three separate tests. This reasoning covers the raw/adjusted/register triad specifically. It does not extend to the four H1a--H1d hypotheses or the $\sim$24 robustness configurations reported elsewhere, which are interpreted collectively for consistency of sign and magnitude rather than treated as a formally corrected multiple-testing family. All three are reported for every configuration. Because the decomposition is fixed in advance and the three numbers inform each other, no multiple-comparison correction is applied.

A supplementary pooled-regression analysis combines all 24 configurations into a single panel (Appendix~\ref{app:regression}).

\subsection{Robustness of the Gap Rankings}\label{sec:robustness}

Register removal is validated against independent linguistic markers (Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}); the adjusted gap is the primary estimate.

\textbf{Cross-sensitivity (policy source, segment cap, retrieval).} Under MPNet--LR, the semantic-gap ranking is stable across segment cap and retrieval in the adjusted panel ($\rho \geq 0.65$; Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}) and across policy source, segment cap, and retrieval in the raw panel ($\rho \geq 0.65$; Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness-raw}). The same holds for coverage (Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-coverage}).

\textbf{Encoder sensitivity.} Coverage-gap ranks are stable across encoders (MPNet--LR vs.\ SciBERT--LR $\rho = \SciBERTCoverageRho$; vs.\ MiniLM--LR $\rho = \MiniLMCoverageRho$; Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-coverage}). Semantic-gap magnitude is encoder-dependent (SciBERT: \SciBERTMeanGap{} vs.\ MPNet: \MPNetMeanGap{}) because scientific pretraining fuses the genres \parencite{Beltagy2019}, but the divergence conclusion holds (Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-semantic}). Under the raw gap, SDG~3 leads for MPNet--LR and SciBERT--LR while SDG~13 leads for MiniLM--LR; SDG~17 trails in all three (rank 16 or 17).

\textbf{Classifier sensitivity.} LR and MLP show varied agreement (MPNet LR vs.\ MLP $\rho = \MlpSemanticRho$; Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-semantic}). Under the raw gap, SDG~17 is the most classifier-sensitive goal, sitting at rank~17 under LR but rank~8 under zero-shot.

\textbf{Distance-functional robustness.} Most distribution-aware metrics correlate only moderately with the adjusted semantic gap (sliced Wasserstein $\rho=\DistGapSpearmanSwd$, Chamfer $\rho=\DistGapSpearmanChamfer$), while Gaussian-2-Wasserstein and squared MMD are weaker ($\rho \approx 0.24$--$0.26$) and C2ST and Grassmann show negative agreement ($\rho = \DistGapSpearmanCtst{}$ and $\DistGapSpearmanGrassmann{}$; Appendix~\ref{app:distributional-table}). The adjusted gap is primarily a mean-direction finding; shape carries a related but distinct signal.

\textbf{Synthesis.} Two conclusions survive: SDG~15 is robustly least-divergent (rank~17 across configs); SDG~17 is most classifier-sensitive and most divergent under the adjusted gap, while SDG~3 leads under the raw gap for MPNet--LR. The raw-null pattern replicates across all configs and policy-source families. Pooled OLS confirms the adjusted association (Appendix~\ref{app:regression}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The raw coverage-gap correlation is near zero ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, $p = \SpearmanCovRawP$). The adjusted gap is positively associated with coverage ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic$, $p = \RhoCovTopicP$), whereas the register component has a negative association ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister$, $p = \RhoCovRegisterP$; Table~\ref{tab:interaction}).

\subsection{Semantic cancellation effect}
\label{sec:discussion-cancellation}

 \textbf{Independence of classification and measurement.} Classification uses softmax probabilities; semantic measurement uses cosine distance between L2-normalised centroids. The main confounder risk is INLP register-adjustment adjusting less for low-coverage SDGs than high-coverage ones, manufacturing rather than revealing the adjusted-gap correlation. Stratification rules this out (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}): at every iteration INLP draws an equal, globally fixed number of research and policy embeddings per SDG (n\_target = \RegisterIterNPerSdg{} for MPNet), regardless of actual coverage share. Coverage-as-label-share cannot therefore leak into the learned or removed direction.

\textbf{Cross-configuration replication.} This pattern replicates across most configs (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}): signs are stable for all encoder--classifier combinations under the primary keyword-retrieved corpus; excluding the concept-retrieved track (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), it does not appear to be a quirk of a specific encoder space, classifier, or methodological choice (segmentation, segment-capping for long documents).

\textbf{SciBERT compression supports the register interpretation.} SciBERT compresses the raw gap from \MPNetMeanGap{} to \SciBERTMeanGap{} (88\% reduction) while preserving coverage ($\rho = \SciBERTCoverageRho$; Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-coverage}). Scientific pretraining \parencite{Beltagy2019} fuses the registers because both use scientific conventions, which the decomposition expects.

\subsection{Two communities, two dimensions}

If the cancellation is real, it calls for a theoretical explanation. The two-communities theory \parencite{Caplan1979} provides one. Caplan characterises social scientists and policy makers as living in ``separate worlds with different and often conflicting values, different reward systems, and different languages'' \parencite[459]{Caplan1979}. The gap, he argues, ``necessarily involves value and ideological dimensions as well as technical ones'' \parencite[461]{Caplan1979}. If the two communities are genuinely distinct knowledge cultures, then a near-zero raw correlation is expected: the two corpora develop different topical priorities (producing coverage divergence) and different communicative norms (producing register divergence), and the two effects operate in opposite directions on the semantic gap. Caplan himself warned that ``it does not follow\ldots\ that an alliance of social scientists and policy makers is the panacea'' \parencite[461]{Caplan1979}. Under this account, a near-zero raw correlation is unsurprising.

 The adjusted association also shows that coverage and framing are separable dimensions, not a single ``alignment'' property. A single alignment score would conflate these two genuinely different patterns. This separability is the paper's core empirical contribution to the SDG-mapping literature, which has treated coverage as the primary output \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. The framework demonstrates that coverage and framing should be reported as independent axes.

 The positive adjusted correlation ($\rho = +0.544$, read as pattern-consistent rather than confirmatory evidence --- Section~\ref{sec:discussion-cancellation}) is consistent with the two dimensions being linked through topic: where one community dominates a given SDG's coverage, the framing tends to diverge. The epistemic-communities framework \parencite{Haas1992} makes this reading clearer. Haas argues that ``before states can agree on whether and how to deal collectively with a specific problem, they must reach some consensus about the nature and scope of the problem\ldots'' \parencite[29]{Haas1992}. As an illustration, where research dominates coverage (SDGs~3,~9), the framing reflects academic norms of knowledge production: ``problems are set and solved in a context governed by the, largely academic, interests of a specific community'' \parencite[3]{Gibbons1994}. On this reading, the coverage--semantic association reflects two communities framing the same goals differently wherever their attention diverges.

However, Haas also cautions that ``given the many examples of different states reacting in different ways to the same consensual evidence provided by specialists, it is unclear how effective consensual knowledge is, as an independent variable, at explaining or predicting state behavior'' \parencite[30]{Haas1992}. The adjusted association shows that coverage and framing are linked through topic, but it does not show that this linkage produces policy uptake.

The directional decomposition (H1b--H1d) shows an asymmetric structure, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:interaction}. H1d (policy coverage) is the strongest consistent predictor, positive in \HOneDPolicyAdjPositiveCount/\HOneDPolicyAdjPositiveCountTotal{} configurations. Framing divergence is wider where policy heavily covers an SDG, consistent with Haas's observation that epistemic communities occupy institutional niches \parencite[30]{Haas1992} and with the Mode~1/Mode~2 distinction \parencite{Gibbons1994}.

This asymmetry is consistent with Cash et al.'s \parencite{Cash2003} observation that salience, credibility, and legitimacy are ``tightly coupled'' such that efforts to enhance one ``normally incur a cost to the others.'' The SDGs where policy attention concentrates (13, 16, 17) are also those whose framings sit furthest from research norms of credibility.

\subsection{The register decomposition: what the raw gap actually measures}

For 16 of 17 SDGs, the register component is positive (\RegCompSdgTen{} to \RegCompSdgThirteen{}; Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}), meaning register uniformly inflates the apparent divergence. Studies comparing corpora that differ in register overstate semantic divergence when they use raw embedding distances. The raw gap is a composite of topic, register, and convention. Only the adjusted gap approximates topic.

The exception is SDG~17, where the register component is negative (\RegCompSdgSeventeen{}): register makes the two communities appear more similar than their topics warrant. Under the raw gap SDG~17 ranks near the bottom (\SemanticGapSdgSeventeen{}) but after register removal it jumps to the top (\AdjGapSdgSeventeen{}). Policy partnership discourse uses institutional language (\textit{countries}, \textit{nations}, \textit{global}) that superficially resembles research vocabulary, but the substance differs: research concerns technical terms (\textit{model}, \textit{method}), while policy concerns institutional coordination.

SDG~17 shows the least visible framing difference: partnership vocabulary resembles both communities' language, while the coordination logic belongs to policy alone. \textcite{LeBlanc2015} treats SDG~17 as dedicated to cross-cutting ``means of implementation'' rather than thematic areas. The register inversion supports this reading.

 SDG stratification works because the 17 goals span many topics, so a direction separating research from policy cannot also encode any one SDG topic. The method assumes topic and register can be pulled apart; where they cannot (e.g., clinical notes vs biomedical literature), some topic would leak into the removed component.

\subsection{Level 0: Institutional Commensurability}
\label{sec:level0}

 The three-level framework assumes research and policy discourse are already commensurable categories; constructing the corpora exposes a prior condition it does not capture. The research corpus is an intersection of AI and SDG content (\NResearchAbstracts{} abstracts), while the curated AI-and-SDG policy family is \PolicyCuratedDocs{} documents --- and policy documents overwhelmingly discuss AI governance and SDG implementation as separate agendas. This is not a sampling limitation to be corrected with more time (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}): ``AI and the SDGs'' is not yet an established organising category in institutional policy discourse the way it is in academic research. ``Level'' here is reference-relative, not absolute: the observation applies the framework's logic one category outward, asking whether AI coverage is established within general SDG discourse on the policy side. It is not, in the formal sense of this paper's Level~1 analysis, a measurement (no general-SDG coverage statistic was collected); it is a plausible extension motivating future work, not a finding in its own right.

 This asymmetry maps onto the Mode~1/Mode~2 distinction \parencite{Gibbons1994} one level up: Mode~1 lets a research community define a novel cross-cutting category internally, while institutional discourse adopts new categories only when demanded by a concrete problem. The near-total absence of a comparably sized ``AI-and-SDG'' policy corpus is therefore the gap, observed before Level~1 coverage or Level~2 framing can be measured --- not a gap this study failed to fill. As \textcite{LeBlanc2015, ICSU2017} note for SDG~17, an analogous reading suggests ``AI and the SDGs'' may occupy pre-domain status in policy discourse, so the gaps measured here describe divergence within a category that barely exists on the policy side. A future Level~0 measurement --- tracking co-occurrence of AI and SDG terms in institutional agendas over time --- would convert this inference into a Level-1-style measurement; the present study only registers the asymmetry it produced.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Semantic proximity, not substantive alignment.} The study is positioned at Level~2 of a three-level construct (Section~\ref{sec:alignment-framework}). Level~3 requires qualitative, institutional, and deliberative methods and is left to future work.

\textbf{Distance metric discards distributional shape.} Centroids are mean-direction measures. Under raw embeddings, distribution-aware metrics (sliced Wasserstein, Chamfer) correlated strongly with the semantic gap ($\rho = 0.44$--$0.91$), but this agreement was largely a register artifact: the distribution-aware metrics track the register component of the gap, not the adjusted semantic gap (Appendix~\ref{app:distributional-table}). After register removal, the adjusted semantic gap is a mean-direction measure, and distribution-aware metrics correlate with it only weakly to moderately (e.g.\ Gaussian-2-Wasserstein $\rho=\DistGapSpearmanFrechet$, sliced Wasserstein $\rho=\DistGapSpearmanSwd$). They remain an informative full-distribution complement, not a re-ranking. The covariance-shape term accounts for $\approx\DistGapShapeShareMean\%$ of the squared Gaussian-2-Wasserstein distance, and a replicate run under the adjacent seed changes the largest per-SDG gap by at most \DistGapMaxSeedDelta{} (Appendix~\ref{app:distributional-table}).

\textbf{Unaudited upstream SDG filter.} The research corpus depends on OpenAlex's native SDG-work classification to define the candidate universe before the four AI terms are applied (Section~\ref{sec:research-corpus}). This filter's precision and recall against ground-truth SDG relevance have not been independently audited in this study, and the \NResearchAbstracts{} abstracts retrieved reflect OpenAlex's operational definition of SDG-relevant work rather than a validated one. The corpus size is large enough that composition estimates are stable, but it does not, on its own, show that the filter is accurate. Auditing it against a manually labelled sample is left for future work.

\textbf{Retrieval-method dependency.} The keyword-retrieved corpus trades recall for precision. Appendix~\ref{app:concept-retrieval-sensitivity} shows that concept-retrieved papers (field-of-study membership) produce closely aligned gap rankings, with a lower SDG~4 share ($-$\ConceptCoverageMaxAbsDelta{}pp) as the largest deviation between the two profiles. Re-running the full pipeline with concept-retrieved papers as the primary corpus is the most direct path to eliminating this artefact and is left for future work. Additionally, the concept-retrieved robustness check (Appendix~\ref{app:concept-retrieval-sensitivity}) reuses the register-adjusted space fitted on the keyword-retrieved corpus rather than re-fitting INLP on the concept-retrieved embeddings, assuming the research-policy register direction is stable across retrieval strategies. This assumption is untested. Re-fitting INLP natively on the concept-retrieved corpus is recommended before treating that corpus as a fully independent robustness check.

\textbf{Concept-retrieved corpus is unfiltered for SDG relevance.} Unlike the primary keyword-retrieved corpus, which applies OpenAlex's SDG work filter before the AI terms, the concept-retrieved robustness corpus (Appendix~\ref{app:concept-retrieval-sensitivity}) restricts only to AI/ML field-of-study membership. No SDG filter is applied at retrieval. SDG labels for this corpus are assigned entirely by the trained classifier post hoc, so its agreement with the keyword-retrieved corpus (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}) demonstrates stability of coverage-share ranking under a different retrieval strategy, not independent confirmation that the concept corpus itself is SDG-relevant. This concern is consistent with a pattern in Table~\ref{tab:interaction}: on H1a's raw semantic-gap column, the concept-retrieved configuration is the only one whose sign flips against the rest of the encoder--classifier grid (Concept LR: +0.108, vs.\ negative values in the other eight configurations), while the MiniLM and SciBERT tracks --- also different encoders --- show no comparable sign flip. (H1c's raw column is mixed across all configs, so no single track uniquely flips there.) This does not prove the unfiltered-retrieval concern causes the instability, but it is the pattern that concern would predict, and MiniLM/SciBERT show no comparable instability despite also being different encoders. Time constraints prevented applying a comparable SDG filter to this alternative corpus. Doing so is recommended before treating it as a fully independent validation. Follow-up works should either apply OpenAlex's SDG filter for consistency and audit it independently for validation, or not apply OpenAlex's SDG filter and allow for baseline non-SDG classification (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}).

\textbf{Register adjustment is partial.} INLP removes a linear subspace that captures the dominant corpus-level register difference, but register is multi-layered \parencite{biber1988variation}. The register interpretation rests on a structural argument (SDG stratification ensures topic cannot leak into the removed direction; Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}) plus partial empirical validation against independent linguistic markers (Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}). The validation finds a reduction of the corpus-level signal but not its complete elimination.

\textbf{Per-SDG assignment is approximate.} The hard argmax treats SDG discourse as cleanly separable, but the PCA space (Figure~\ref{fig:pca-register-before-after}) shows real-world SDG discourse is fuzzy. SDG~4 is artefact-affected by the machine-learning/education ``learning'' overlap; its rank is read conditional on Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}. Leave-one-out of SDG~4 preserves the adjusted association ($\rho = \HExclFourCovgapAdjSpearmanRho$, $p = \HExclFourCovgapAdjSpearmanP$). Future work with multi-class SDG classification is recommended.

\textbf{Cross-sectional design.} The study observes one period (2018--2025) against a similarly timed policy corpus. It cannot assess whether alignment is improving or deteriorating, or whether specific events (e.g., the EU AI Act 2024, IPCC AR6 2022) \parencite{EUAI2024, IPCC2022} shifted research framing.

\subsection{Contribution and Implications}
\label{sec:framework-contribution}

The primary contribution of this paper is the measurement framework, not the specific AI--SDG findings. The framework advances AI--SDG mapping from Level~1 (lexical correspondence) to Level~2 (observed semantic proximity), with explicit marking of Level~3 boundaries (Section~\ref{sec:alignment-framework}). The cross-sensitivity transparency (Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}) is a reusable template: instead of reporting a single gap ranking, it shows how stable every ranking position is across the measurement choices. The SDG-stratified INLP adaptation --- training the corpus classifier on balanced topic--corpus strata so that no linear direction can encode both topic and register --- is a methodological contribution applicable to any cross-corpus comparison where topic and register are confounded. The protocol is portable to any research-policy interface that shares a category system and embeds both sides in a common semantic space.

The AI--SDG findings are a first application to be checked against a larger, more diverse policy corpus, not settled results. The findings reported here, the cancellation, the SDG~17 inversion, and the positive adjusted association, are properties of this specific pair of corpora at this moment in time.

Beyond the measurement advance, the results carry a direct implication for how SDG engagement is read. The paper's premise, that shared coverage is not shared framing, is intuitive, and the results confirm it: coverage and framing are separable, with only a correlation of $\rho = \RhoCovTopic{}$ between them. Coverage, the Level~1 metric existing studies report, captures only a weak form of salience; it does not address credibility or legitimacy \parencite{Cash2003}. The adjusted gap is a Level~2 metric that begins to measure framing compatibility. The high-gap SDGs (17, 3, 16, 9, 13) are candidates for boundary-organising practices \parencite{Guston2001}: policy synthesis briefs, joint workshops, or funder-required summaries. Cash et al.\ \parencite{Cash2003} caution that translation alone cannot resolve the tradeoffs among salience, credibility, and legitimacy.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

 The Introduction asked how academic AI-for-sustainability research and institutional policy discourse differ in SDG coverage and semantic framing, and how these two dimensions are related. This paper demonstrated that coverage divergence (which SDGs each community prioritises) and within-SDG semantic framing divergence (how differently they discuss the same goal) are separable dimensions that can diverge independently. The linkage is asymmetric: policy coverage is the strongest directional predictor of framing divergence (positive in \HOneDPolicyAdjPositiveCount/\HOneDPolicyAdjPositiveCountTotal{} configurations), while research coverage shows no consistent signal. Where policy attention concentrates, the discourse resembles Mode~2 knowledge production \parencite{Gibbons1994}, and its framings sit further from research norms. Both regularities fit the account of two distinct knowledge cultures addressing the same goals \parencite{Caplan1979, Cash2003}.

The takeaway for practice is that an SDG label is not evidence of alignment: a tag records that a text is about a goal, not that it frames the goal the way another community does. The register decomposition shows embedding distance, like keyword matching, is a composite signal inflated by genre for 16 of 17 SDGs, with SDG~17 the stark exception where institutional vocabulary masks the deepest topic divergence. Because funders and policymakers read SDG counts as proof of engagement \parencite{Heilinger2023, HerasSaizarbitoria2022}, the two-dimensional reading developed here --- coverage alongside framing, with the register component made explicit --- is needed to interpret that engagement correctly.

 The asymmetric corpus construction is itself suggestive: the difficulty of building a symmetric AI-SDG policy corpus may reflect that ``AI and the SDGs'' is not yet an established organising category in policy discourse the way it already is in research --- an inferred, not measured, institutional gap (Section~\ref{sec:level0}). Level~3, whether the divergences documented here reflect productive exploration, institutional failure, or different knowledge cultures pursuing different mandates, requires qualitative, institutional, and deliberative methods that lie beyond the scope of embedding analysis.

\subsection{SDG 4 Lexical Artefact Audit: The ML `Learning' Overlap}
\label{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}

SDG 4 is the only goal audited because it is the only one carrying an a priori lexical-overlap concern: ``learning'' is shared ML and education vocabulary, and prior AI--SDG inventories place SDG 4 outside the leading tier of SDG 3 and SDG 7 \parencite{Singh2024, Cowls2021, Nedungadi2024} (in \textcite{Singh2024}'s 20,511-document corpus SDG 4 ranks third of 17 with 2,312 publications versus 6,829 for SDG 3 and 6,683 for SDG 7), so its third-place research share here is suspect on independent grounds. The audit does not relabel records. It tests whether the concern is empirically plausible.

The keyword groups are defined as follows. The ML group comprises 14 terms: \emph{machine learning, deep learning, reinforcement learning, supervised learning, unsupervised learning, neural network, model, models, train, trained, training, algorithm, classification, prediction}. The education group comprises 17 terms: \emph{education, educational, school, schools, student, students, teacher, teachers, classroom, curriculum, learning outcome, pedagogy, university, universities, course, courses, teaching}. Note that bare ``learning'' is not a matched term on either side. It appears only within compound phrases (\emph{machine learning} on the ML side, \emph{learning outcome} on the education side), reconciling the subsection title with the actual matching behaviour. Matching is case-insensitive, whole-word (\verb|\b...\b| regex), and unstemmed, applied to the segmented title+abstract research text. A record is classified as ``both'' if it contains at least one term from each group; otherwise it is ML-only, education-only, or neither. The machine-readable keyword lists and aggregate counts are stored in \texttt{4\_outputs/appendix/mpnet/a3\_sdg4\_audit/data/sdg4\_lexical\_audit\_summary.json}.

Two methodological caveats apply. First, the audit operates at the segment level (3,105,144 rows, one per text segment), not the paper level. Roughly 34\% of multi-segment papers carry differing labels across their segments, so segment-level percentages are segment-weighted and may overrepresent long abstracts. The matched non-SDG 4 sample is a seed-42 reservoir draw from non-SDG-4 segment rows. Second, the ML list intentionally includes generic method vocabulary (\emph{model, training, algorithm}) that is near-universal in an AI corpus. The diagnostic signal is therefore not the ML-presence rate itself but the education-versus-ML asymmetry across subsets (44.4\% education-only in SDG 4 versus 4.0\% and 2.5\% in the comparison sets).

Table~\ref{tab:sdg4-lexical-audit} compares the lexical composition of SDG 4-assigned research with a matched non-SDG 4 sample and with SDG 9, using these two keyword groups. The result is a negative test of the false-positive hypothesis: education-specific vocabulary alone covers 44.4\% of SDG 4-assigned records and overlaps ML vocabulary in a further 23.8\%, while only 11.8\% are ML-only. The comparison sets are inverted, with ML-only vocabulary dominating the non-SDG 4 sample and the SDG 9 set (50.3\% and 61.5\%). The SDG 4 signal is therefore not the same technical language that drives SDG 9. But the 23.8\% ``both'' share (versus 2.6\% and 2.9\% elsewhere) shows the two vocabularies do overlap materially within the SDG 4-assigned set.

The main analysis therefore retains the canonical LR argmax assignment but continues to treat SDG 4 as artefact-affected. The most accurate reading is a learning-related assignment whose interpretation is blurred by lexical overlap: neither ``clean education'' nor ``pure ML artefact.''

\section{Distance-Functional Robustness Table}
\label{app:distributional-table}

Table~\ref{tab:distributional-gap} reports the per-SDG research--policy gap under the full battery of distribution-aware metrics, together with the primary semantic gap. Part~1 covers the full-corpus exact metrics (sliced Wasserstein, Chamfer, Hausdorff, Gaussian-2-Wasserstein, Grassmann, linear MMD); part~2 covers the sampled metrics (exact EMD, Sinkhorn, energy, squared RBF MMD, C2ST AUC) plus the sampled semantic gap. The final row of each part reports Spearman $\rho$ between each metric's ranking and the primary (adjusted) ranking.

\textbf{Why the raw robustness result does not carry over.} Under raw embeddings the distribution-aware metrics appeared to preserve the semantic gap ranking ($\rho = 0.44$--$0.91$), an agreement driven by the shared \emph{register} component rather than by topic. Decomposing the raw gap into adjusted semantic gap $+$ register component, the register component has \emph{larger} variance than the raw gap itself (variance ratio $\DistRawVarRatio$) and correlates $\rho = \DistRegVsRawRho$ with the raw gap while correlating $\rho = \DistRegVsAdjRho$ with the adjusted semantic gap. The distribution-aware metrics therefore tracked register, agreeing with the raw semantic gap but diverging from the adjusted one. After INLP removes register, the metrics instead track topic-space \emph{shape}: most correlate moderately with the adjusted semantic gap ($\rho = 0.44$--$0.69$; sliced Wasserstein $\rho = \DistGapSpearmanSwd$, exact EMD $\rho = \DistGapSpearmanEmd$), Gaussian-2-Wasserstein and squared MMD are weaker ($\rho \approx 0.24$--$0.26$), and Sinkhorn, C2ST, and Grassmann show near-zero or negative agreement ($\rho = \DistGapSpearmanSinkhorn{}$, $\DistGapSpearmanCtst{}$, $\DistGapSpearmanGrassmann{}$), and they elevate SDGs~12 and~1 at the margin. The adjusted semantic gap is a mean-direction estimate, and the distribution-aware distances are a complementary full-distribution view. The covariance-shape term of the Gaussian-2-Wasserstein distance accounts for $\approx\DistGapShapeShareMean\%$ of the squared distance on average, and a replicate run under the adjacent seed changes the largest per-SDG gap by at most $\DistGapMaxSeedDelta$.

\section{Register Removal: Convergence and Validation}
\label{app:register-removal}

\subsection{Convergence}\label{app:register-robustness}

The full INLP procedure and identification argument are given in Methodology (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}). This appendix reports the convergence behaviour of the procedure and the construct validation of the component it removes. The adjusted results are the primary decomposition, not sensitivity diagnostics.

\paragraph{Sampling design.}
Two details of the sampling in Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp} bear on how the adjusted gap should be read. First, $n_{\text{target}}$ is one global constant -- the smallest available count across all SDG--corpus pools, \RegisterIterNPerSdg{} segments for MPNet -- so every SDG contributes the same number of research and policy texts at every iteration regardless of how large its actual pool is; how much text an SDG has cannot change how much register adjustment it receives. Second, the stratified sample is redrawn independently at each iteration (seeded per iteration, without replacement within each draw), so the learned directions do not hinge on any single subsample of the pool. One residue is unavoidable: for the pool that binds the global minimum, all available texts are used at every iteration, so that one stratum sees identical samples throughout; this affects only the smallest pool, treats both corpora identically, and cannot differentiate well-covered from sparsely-covered goals.

\paragraph{Convergence results.}\label{app:register-inlp}
INLP removal converges fast. Held-out accuracy falls from \RegisterFirstAcc{} at iteration~1 to \RegisterFinalAcc{} by iteration~\RegisterIterFinalK{}, and the mean gap shrinks by \RegisterIterReductionPct{}\% before flattening. Almost all of that happens in the first 15 directions: the gap's correlation with the raw (un-removed) gap drops from \RegisterIterRhoAtOne{} to about \RegisterIterPlateauRho{} and stays there. Table~\ref{tab:iterative-register-check} shows the per-iteration path for all three encoders.

The decomposition identifies the SDG~17 inversion discussed in Results (Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap}) and Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}: the register component is positive for all SDGs except SDG~17 (range: \RegCompSdgTen{} to \RegCompSdgThirteen{}, smallest SDG~10, largest SDG~13), the only negative value, where register \emph{increases} the apparent research--policy similarity of the partnership goal.

\paragraph{Cross-config replication.}
The same iterative procedure was run on the MiniLM and SciBERT encoder tracks (the latter two on the S1 subset, by design). All three reach the $\leq 0.5$ stopping threshold. Held-out register-classification accuracy falls from \RegisterCrossFirstAccMin{}--\RegisterCrossFirstAccMax{} at iteration~1 to \RegisterCrossFinalAccMin{}--\RegisterCrossFinalAccMax{} at convergence, and all three compress sharply in the first 15 iterations (to \RegisterCrossAccAtFifteenMin{}--\RegisterCrossAccAtFifteenMax{}) before plateauing at the majority-class baseline. The iteration count required to reach the threshold differs across encoders, but the qualitative convergence --- a rapid early drop followed by a plateau --- is identical, confirming that the convergence behaviour is a property of the INLP procedure rather than of the MPNet embedding space.

\subsection{Construct Validation}\label{app:register-validation}

\subsubsection{Purpose and Validation Question}
\label{sec:regval-purpose}

Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp} shows \emph{what} INLP removes, a corpus-separable linear component, but not what that component is. This subsection evaluates the register interpretation empirically, using an independent surface-linguistic register score computed from the segment texts, following Biber-style register features \parencite{biber1988variation}.

The validation can show three things: whether the adjustment removes a large corpus-separable component; whether the removal is accompanied by evidence that SDG (topic) structure is being destroyed; and whether apparent residual register signals survive controlled samples. It cannot prove that the removed subspace is identical to the measured linguistic register features, and the register score used here is a small set of surface indicators. The validation is single-encoder and sample-limited.

\subsubsection{Register Operationalisation and Sample Construction}
\label{sec:regval-operationalisation}

\textbf{What the test checks.} The main analysis removes a writing-style (register) component with INLP and reads the remaining gap as topic. The appendix checks that reading with independent, surface-level style measurements. Two questions are asked: (1) does removal actually weaken the research--policy distinction? (2) does removal destroy topic? The short answer is yes to (1) and no to (2); whether the removed part is truly \emph{register} is not proven (see the conclusion).

\textbf{Sample construction.} All results use the canonical MPNet track (\RegValSampleN{} segments per sample: 12 per SDG per corpus, seed 42), and the same frozen projection matrix $G$ as the main analysis. A small set of long flagship reports (the SDSN Sustainable Development Reports 2024 and 2025, the UNDP Human Development Report 2021/22, the WHO report on ethics and governance of AI for health, the EU AI Act, and two UN SDG progress reports) contribute \RegValMultiParentUnits{} segments across \RegValMegaSdgs{} of the 17 SDGs, often as near-duplicates of the same document. These segments are style outliers (mean sentence length \RegValMegaMeanSentLen{} words vs.\ \RegValNonMegaMeanSentLen{} for the remaining units; nominalisation rate \RegValMegaNominal{} vs.\ \RegValNonMegaNominal{}) and, because segments of one document sit close in embedding space, can create fake pooled correlations. The main test therefore uses a one-segment-per-document design (global deduplication by source document; \RegValOppDistinctParents{} distinct parents, no multi-parent units), which caps each flagship document at one segment and replaces excluded segments with other policy segments from the same SDG, drawn mostly from shorter national and thematic monitoring reports. The two designs differ in makeup, which the Limitations return to. Table~\ref{tab:register-sample-design} summarises both.

\subsubsection{Corpus Discrimination Before and After INLP}
\label{sec:regval-discrimination}

A 5-fold logistic-regression classifier separates research from policy segments almost perfectly on raw embeddings (accuracy \RegValAccRawOrig{} on the original sample; \RegValAccRawOpp{} on the one-per-parent sample; Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-accuracy}). After register removal the accuracy falls to \RegValAccAdjOrig{} on the original sample and \RegValAccAdjOpp{} on the one-per-parent sample -- a large share of the corpus-linear signal is gone, though not all -- and the canonical one-per-parent value stays above chance ($\RegValAccAdjOpp{}$). Confidence intervals and $p$-values are in the table.

A per-SDG-deduplicated variant of the sample (dominated by the \RegValMultiParentUnits{} flagship policy segments) yields an adjusted accuracy of \RegValAccAdjOrig{}, indistinguishable from chance. Excluding those segments raises it to \RegValAccMegaExcl{} (difference \RegValAccRise{}, 95\% CI \RegValAccRiseCi{}), confirming that flagship clustering drives the original-sample result. The canonical one-per-parent estimate (\RegValAccAdjOpp{}) is the primary result. Residual above-chance separation after removal is not a method failure, since INLP's stopping rule converges on its own training distribution while the validation classifier is re-estimated from scratch.

The register-only rows of Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-accuracy} bound the interpretation. A classifier trained on the six surface features alone reaches only \RegValAccRegOrig{} on the original sample and \RegValAccRegOpp{} on the one-per-parent sample, barely above chance in the latter case. The linguistic markers therefore capture only a part of the corpus distinction. This is consistent with the register reading (the corpora do differ in register-like surface ways), but it also delimits it: the removed embedding component is far broader than these six surface features, so the validation cannot claim that the removed subspace is exhausted by, or identical to, the measured features.

\subsubsection{Topic Preservation}
\label{sec:regval-topic}

If the adjustment removed topic, 17-way SDG selectivity, how well the SDG of a segment can be predicted from its embedding, would fall. It does not (Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-selectivity}): on the original per-SDG-dedup sample (draw~1), LR cross-validated accuracy is \RegValSelRawLr{} on raw and \RegValSelAdjLr{} on adjusted embeddings, and kNN \RegValSelRawKnn{} and \RegValSelAdjKnn{} (chance \RegValSelChance{}). The change is small and of mixed sign, so there is no evidence that topic structure is systematically removed; the analysis does not claim perfect preservation, and it was run on the original sample only, not re-estimated on the one-per-parent rebuild. Topic remains linearly decodable after register removal, as the identification argument anticipates.

\subsubsection{Residual Register Diagnostics and Robustness Investigation}
\label{sec:regval-residual}

The register score used here is the independent surface-linguistic score defined in G.2.2. Does any register survive removal? Two faint traces appear on the messy sample but both vanish or reverse on the cleaner one-per-parent sample, so they reflect flagship-document clustering, not real residual register. Specifically, on the messy sample the manual score correlates weakly with the amount removed (\RegValTwoBOrigPooled{}, within-research \RegValTwoBOrigWithinRes{}, within-policy \RegValTwoBOrigWithinPol{}), and correlates more strongly with SDG-centroid distance in adjusted than raw space (\RegValTwoCOrigRaw{} $\to$ \RegValTwoCOrigAdj{}). On the clean sample both correlations disappear or go negative (\RegValTwoBOppPooled{}; centroid \RegValTwoCOppRaw{} $\to$ \RegValTwoCOppAdj{}). A within-SDG trace (\RegValOtherDistRho{}) and the draw-stability check (seeds 43--45, sign-flipping, only \RegValPerSdgSig{}/17 SDGs significant) confirm it is draw-level noise, not structure.

\subsubsection{Validation Conclusion}
\label{sec:regval-conclusion}

The validation supports the following claims.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The adjustment removes a corpus-level component: corpus-discrimination accuracy falls from near-perfect on raw embeddings to moderately above chance after removal, on both the original and one-per-parent samples (Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-accuracy}).
  \item The removed component is not strongly attributable to SDG topic loss: 17-way SDG selectivity is essentially unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-selectivity}).
  \item Apparent residual register signals were explained by sampling and document-clustering effects rather than by stable residual structure: the initial removed-magnitude and centroid-distance associations vanished or reversed on the controlled sample, and the one surviving within-SDG trace was draw-unstable.
  \item The remaining evidence provides partial empirical support for the register reading: the corpora differ in register-like surface features in stable directions, and the adjustment removes corpus-separable signal while leaving topic structure intact, but a measured correspondence between the removed component and the six features was not demonstrated, and residual corpus-linear signal remains.
\end{itemize}

The register interpretation is therefore consistent with the validation evidence rather than confirmed by it. The main text describes it as resting on partial empirical support.

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:regval-limitations}

The validation is bounded in ways that should be read alongside the main text. First, the register score is one choice among several: an alternative a-priori ``institutional'' combination gave null or reversed results, and the score's sign is a convention, not a substantive prior. Second, each sample contains only \RegValSampleN{} segments (12 per SDG per corpus), and the per-SDG tests within the draw-stability stage have $n = 12$ policy units each, so the \RegValPerSdgSig{}-of-17 significant per-SDG result is not evidence of absence at the per-SDG level. Third, the draw-stability check was applied to the within-SDG trace only; the corpus-level accuracies in Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-accuracy} were not re-estimated on fresh draws. Fourth, the one-per-parent sample is a rebuild, not a subset of the original sample, and the excluded flagship documents are replaced by shorter national and thematic monitoring reports, so the two-sample comparison is partly compositional. Fifth, the validation is MPNet-canonical only. Sixth, the residual corpus-linear signal that survives adjustment is not characterised further; its exact linguistic content remains open, part of why the empirical support is only partial.

\subsection{Detailed Policy Profiles by Source Family}
\label{app:policy-source-data}

The full policy corpus combines a curated AI/SDG sub-corpus, SDGi VNR/VLR reports, and UN General Debate speeches. These are all policy-facing texts, but they are not interchangeable genres. The family split reported in Table~\ref{tab:policy-source-family} therefore asks a narrow question: whether the full-corpus policy profile is broadly stable across source families or whether it is strongly shaped by corpus composition.

The answer is mixed. The international-institutional profile is source-family sensitive. The curated AI/SDG family is much more innovation-oriented, with SDG 9 leading its document-weighted profile, while the UNGDC family is more strongly concentrated on climate, partnerships, and governance, consistent with the UNGD topic distributions reported by \textcite{JankinEtAl2024}, and the SDGi family is comparatively even, with SDG 11-style institutional review language at its top. The implication is interpretive rather than corrective. The full policy corpus should be read as international institutional SDG discourse, not as a universal profile of all AI sustainability policy. The scope of what the policy-side distribution represents is accordingly narrower, but the main diagnostic purpose remains valid.
