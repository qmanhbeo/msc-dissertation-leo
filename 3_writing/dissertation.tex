\begin{abstract}
Shared SDG labels do not establish that research and policy \emph{frame} a goal similarly \parencite{Armitage2020}. This paper contributes a measurement framework that separates research-policy divergence into two independent dimensions: coverage (which SDGs each corpus prioritises) and within-SDG semantic framing (how differently the same SDG is discussed); framing is itself a composite signal of topic (substantive content) and register (communicative conventions), and this paper isolates topic from register using Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP). Using frozen Sentence-BERT embeddings and a supervised logistic-regression classifier trained on \NTrainPool{} SDG-labelled reference segments (test macro-$F_1$ = \MacroFOne), this study scores \NResearchSegments{} segments from \NResearchAbstracts{} AI-for-sustainability abstracts and \NPolicySegments{} segments from \NPolicyDocs{} institutional policy sources, and measures both dimensions across all 17 SDGs.

The supervised classifier reproduces the coverage ranking established by prior keyword- and citation-based inventories: SDG~3 dominates research while SDGs~13,~16, and~17 dominate policy.

The raw coverage--semantic-gap correlation is nevertheless null ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, permutation $p = \SpearmanCovRawP$). Decomposing within-SDG distance into topic and register components, however, reveals a systematic cancellation: the adjusted semantic gap is positively associated with coverage ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic$, $p = \RhoCovTopicP$) while the removed register component is negatively associated ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister$, $p = \RhoCovRegisterP$). All $p$-values are two-sided Monte Carlo permutation $p$-values (100{,}000 resamples). Across the full $n = 17$ SDG framework, only the adjusted association reaches significance; the register component is directionally negative but not significant, so the null raw gap reflects a partial, not complete, cancellation.

The result is nonetheless stable: the adjusted semantic-gap ranking survives every sensitivity check (encoders, classifier family, retrieval strategy, segment cap, and policy-source family) and distribution-aware metrics confirm it is a mean-direction effect. Coverage and framing should be measured and reported as separate axes of divergence; shared labels are insufficient evidence of shared framing \parencite{Heilinger2023}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Research-policy alignment is often inferred from whether scientific and policy texts address the same topics \parencite{bornmann2016policy}, yet shared coverage does not establish comparable framing: a paper and a policy document can both invoke SDG 13 (Climate Action) or SDG 3 (Good Health) while emphasising different problems, actors, and interventions. High-level thematic agreement can therefore mask deeper divergence in how each discourse community approaches the same goal.

Existing methods for mapping research to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) operate mainly at the level of lexical or bibliometric correspondence: keyword dictionaries, Boolean queries, or citation and mention counts \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. They measure whether a text is \emph{about} a given SDG, not how it frames it. That interchangeability is what this paper questions. The coverage distribution reproduces the ranking those methods established, SDG~3 dominant in research and SDGs~13,~16, and~17 in policy, anchoring external validity before the paper turns to framing.

This paper proposes that research-policy alignment has at least two separable dimensions: coverage divergence (which SDGs each corpus prioritises) and semantic framing divergence (how differently they discuss the same SDG). Its contribution is a measurement framework. This framework advances AI--SDG mapping from Level~1 (lexical correspondence) to Level~2 (observed textual-semantic proximity in embedding space; Section~\ref{sec:alignment-framework}), treating semantic proximity as a register-sensitive proxy for framing, not evidence of substantive alignment. The framework operationalises both dimensions across all 17 SDGs with three frozen sentence-transformer encoders (MPNet, MiniLM, SciBERT; Section~\ref{sec:embedding-choice}) and a supervised logistic-regression classifier, stress-tested against encoder, assignment method, policy source, and segment-cap choices, with INLP register-adjustment checks.

The near-zero raw coverage-vs-semantic-gap correlation may reflect cancellation: topic divergence rises with coverage divergence while register divergence falls with it, and the two appear to offset (permutation $p = \RhoCovTopicP$ and $p = \RhoCovRegisterP$ respectively, $n = 17$ SDGs). This matters because SDG tagging is now institutionalised: OpenAlex labels works, funders count SDG outputs, and journals print SDG badges \parencite{Heilinger2023, HerasSaizarbitoria2022}; if shared labels mask a register--topic cancellation, that threatens how the SDG-monitoring ecosystem reads its own signals. After register removal, the adjusted semantic gap identifies SDG~17 as the most divergent goal and the one most sensitive to how divergence is measured; under the raw gap (naive baseline), SDG~3 leads instead. SDG~15 is the stably least-divergent goal under both.

The central research question is: \emph{To what extent do academic AI-for-sustainability research and international institutional SDG policy discourse differ in SDG coverage and semantic framing across the 17 SDGs, and how are these two dimensions related?}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{AI and the SDGs: What Bibliometrics Shows}

Existing assessments of AI and the SDGs rest on expert views or publication counts rather than on the texts themselves \parencite{Vinuesa2020}. Broader SDG scientometrics and the AI-specific inventories converge on one robust finding: SDG~3 (Good Health) and SDG~7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) are the most addressed goals in the AI-specific studies, followed by SDG~4 (Quality Education), SDG~13 (Climate Action), SDG~11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), and SDG~16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions). The evidence base spans a citation-based corpus of roughly 25,000 publications on the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals (M\&SDGs) \parencite{BautistaPuig2021}, 20,511 AI publications (2001--2020) \parencite{Singh2024}, 108 AI-for-social-good projects \parencite{Cowls2021}, and 1,288 big-data and AI publications \parencite{Nedungadi2024}, together with a recent review of 792 AI applications in SDG-related research \parencite{Gohr2025AiSdgs}. None of these studies measures within-SDG semantic framing.

SDG publication mapping is methodologically contingent. \textcite{Armitage2020} shows that what counts as SDG-relevant depends on interpretive choices about queries, labels, and databases; \textcite{Kashnitsky2024} reach the same caution from a broader comparison of Boolean, machine-learning, and hybrid mapping systems, finding no single best approach and no single ``golden'' validation set. The dependence persists in AI-based mapping: keyword baselines still omit publications that never state an SDG keyword explicitly, so the reported corpus boundary remains query-dependent \parencite{Yin2025SdgMapping}. The embedding-based approach used here reduces some vocabulary-level brittleness of keyword dictionaries, but its results remain conditional on corpus construction, centroid provenance, and model choice.

The SDGs themselves are interdependent by design, yet no operational framework specifies how \parencite{Nilsson2016}; single-SDG assignment is therefore a simplifying assumption. One goal breaks the pattern. \textcite{LeBlanc2015}, in a network analysis of the proposed SDGs, treats SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals) as the dedicated goal for cross-cutting means of implementation: finance, trade, technology transfer, and capacity building, excluding it from cross-goal mapping because its targets are implementation infrastructure rather than substantive domains. The \textcite{ICSU2017} guide likewise describes SDG 17 as the main enabler of the whole framework. Unlike every other SDG, whose discourse centres on a shared substantive topic, SDG 17 policy discourse concerns coordinating and resourcing implementation itself, a conceptual space that technical AI research abstracts are not built to occupy.

\subsection{A Three-Level Framework of Research-Policy Alignment}\label{sec:alignment-framework}

This paper proposes that research-policy alignment be understood as a three-level construct rather than a binary property of shared labels:

\textbf{Level 1: lexical or bibliometric correspondence.} The dominant mode of existing SDG-mapping work: assigning texts to goals through keyword dictionaries, Boolean queries, or citation and mention counts \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. Necessary but weak: shared vocabulary does not guarantee shared meaning \parencite{bornmann2016policy}, and keyword methods are brittle to vocabulary and database choices \parencite{Armitage2020}.

\textbf{Level 2: observed textual-semantic proximity in embedding space.} Sentence embeddings locate texts in a shared semantic space where proximity reflects distributional patterns of co-occurrence \parencite{Harris1954, Reimers2019}, and encoder-based SDG association has been benchmarked as a strong baseline \parencite{Gjorgjevikj2025}. This level detects divergence that keyword methods miss. The present study is positioned at this level, and the limits of that measurement are developed in Section~\ref{sec:semantic-methods} below.

\textbf{Level 3: substantive comparison of implied problems, actors, and interventions.} Whether the two discourses address the same problems, involve the same actors, and propose the same interventions \parencite{Cash2003, Guston2001, Haas1992}. Because this requires interpreting causal beliefs, legitimacy, and institutional mechanism \parencite{Caplan1979, Haas1992}, it lies beyond unsupervised text analysis and is left to qualitative follow-up.

Advancing from Level 1 to Level 2 is the study's concrete methodological contribution; Level 3 names the boundary it deliberately does not cross. Figure~\ref{fig:conceptual-framework} illustrates this decomposition.

\subsection{Research-Policy Alignment in AI and Sustainability}\label{sec:rp-alignment}

A growing literature documents structural tensions between what AI research produces and what sustainability policy requires: corporate AI research concentrates on pre-deployment technical problems, creating a gap between research priorities and the deployment impacts governance must address \parencite{Strauss2025}; global AI metrics emphasise technical and economic performance while policy discourse stresses ethical and social dimensions aligned with SDG concerns \parencite{SioumalasChristodoulou2025}; and research and policy communities can share trustworthy-AI terminology while differing in term salience and focal concerns \parencite{ToneyWails2024}. The pattern extends beyond AI: in conservation science, 59\% of policy-prioritised research topics appear in fewer than 15 papers \parencite{McCarthy2026}.

Adjacent scientometric work measures research-policy connection through policy-document mentions rather than semantic framing: only 1.2\% of 191,276 climate-change publications have at least one policy mention \parencite{bornmann2016policy}.

Theory explains why such divergence is the default rather than a failure. Knowledge systems support action when they produce information that is simultaneously salient, credible, and legitimate \parencite{Cash2003}. The epistemic-communities framework explains why shared framing matters for policy coordination under uncertainty: expert communities influence policy by articulating cause--effect relationships and framing issues for collective debate \parencite{Haas1992}. Haas's concept is broader than semantic proximity, involving shared normative and causal beliefs, validity criteria, and a common policy enterprise; the present study therefore does not operationalise an epistemic community directly, but measures only the weaker condition of observed semantic proximity. Even this weaker condition is only a possible precondition for knowledge transfer: semantic proximity cannot show the existence, strength, or influence of an expert community \parencite{Cross2013}. The two-communities theory holds that researchers and policy makers live in separate worlds with different values, reward systems, and languages, so divergence is the default state of research-policy interaction \parencite{Caplan1979}.

The benchmark itself is not neutral. The SDG framework is a politically negotiated instrument with documented limitations: goals can create incentives to over-focus on target achievement \parencite{FukudaParr2016}, summit documents remain state-centric and avoid causal responsibility \parencite{BexellJonsson2017}, and Goal 8's 3\% annual growth target is empirically incompatible with the resource-use and emissions goals \parencite{Hickel2019}. Reported SDG engagement is frequently superficial, so reported alignment should not be conflated with substantive commitment \parencite{Heilinger2023, HerasSaizarbitoria2022}. To the extent that research follows Mode 1 knowledge production (problems set within academic disciplines) rather than Mode 2 (problems arising in contexts of application) \parencite{Gibbons1994}, divergence is not uniformly problematic: where research explores possibilities that policy discourse has not yet absorbed, distance may reflect productive exploration rather than failure of alignment.



\subsection{Semantic Methods: Rationale and Cautions}\label{sec:semantic-methods}

The distributional hypothesis, that meaning can be described through patterns of linguistic co-occurrence \parencite{Harris1954}, underlies the use of Sentence-BERT \parencite{Reimers2019} and related transformer encoders for large-scale semantic comparison. Applying them to compare two distinct discourse corpora, research abstracts and policy documents, is a novel usage rather than a directly benchmarked one, and the interpretation of distance remains descriptive rather than causal. \textcite{Gjorgjevikj2025} benchmarked sentence encoders specifically for SDG association tasks, finding that general-purpose models from the sentence-transformers family perform among the strongest as baselines, and that domain-specific fine-tuning further improves predictive performance while reducing sensitivity to description length. \textcite{Rodriguez2023}'s conText framework provides a computational-social-science precedent for comparing meaning across contexts, adopting a narrow, structuralist view of meaning as distributional co-occurrence and making no causal claims about human understanding. This study follows that same descriptive premise: semantic proximity is a distributional measurement, not evidence of substantive framing alignment. The implication is that embedding distance mixes topic, register, and contextual convention into a single number: distributional representations are not topic-only, since language models inherently encode varieties of language including register \parencite{Grieve2025}, and stylistic variation is demonstrably present in distributional vector spaces \parencite{NiuCarpuat2017}. This motivates the decomposition into these components (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}). \textcite{McCarthy2026} supply a closer empirical precedent, showing with a multi-target SciBERT classifier that research--policy coverage gaps are measurable with transformer encoders outside the AI--SDG setting; this study extends that logic from a single conservation domain to the full SDG landscape, and from coverage to within-SDG semantic framing.

An older corpus-linguistic tradition adds a further caution. Biber's multidimensional (MD) analysis identifies six co-varying dimensions of linguistic variation across 23 spoken and written genres, derived from factor analysis of 67 linguistic features \parencite{biber1988variation}. Each dimension captures a continuum of co-occurring linguistic features rather than a binary speech/writing split \parencite{biber1988variation, biberConrad2009register}. Register is therefore a multi-layered property: two texts can differ on some dimensions and converge on others. The INLP procedure in this study removes a \emph{linear} subspace from the embedding, which is a projection onto one or more directions in the 768-dimensional space; this is best understood as capturing the dominant linear component of Biber's continuous register space, not as a claim that register is binary or that a single projection exhausts it. For the present study this matters because research abstracts and policy documents may differ not only in substantive topic, but also in abstraction, modality, institutional stance, and rhetorical function; embedding distance is therefore treated as observed textual-semantic proximity, not a clean measure of topic alone.

\subsection{Research Gap}

To the best of the evidence reviewed, the literature reviewed above has not produced: (i) a systematic comparison of how academic AI-for-sustainability research and institutional SDG policy discourse differ in SDG coverage and within-SDG semantic framing; prior work measures research coverage, citation patterns, or policy-document uptake, but does not compare the two corpora directly on these dimensions; or (ii) an examination of whether coverage gaps and semantic gaps are positively related, weakly related, or inversely related. This framework is deliberately diagnostic: it maps where observed divergence is visible, without classifying whether any given gap reflects productive or problematic disconnection; the latter would require qualitative follow-up beyond the scope of embedding analysis.

These two contributions are motivated by the theoretical frameworks reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:rp-alignment}. The two-communities theory holds that researchers and policymakers occupy separate worlds with different values, reward systems, and languages \parencite{Caplan1979}. If these communities also develop different topical priorities, as the empirical record of research-policy divergence suggests \parencite{Strauss2025, McCarthy2026}, then coverage divergence and semantic divergence should co-occur: SDGs where one community dominates attention should also show divergent framing, because the community shaping the discussion brings its own communicative norms. This motivates H1a. The epistemic-communities framework further distinguishes the \emph{direction} of dominance: where research prioritises a given SDG, the framing should reflect academic norms of knowledge production; where policy prioritises it, the framing should reflect institutional norms of coordination \parencite{Haas1992}. H1b--H1d decompose the coverage-semantic relationship into these directional components. The Mode~1/Mode~2 distinction \parencite{Gibbons1994} provides a theoretical reason why not all divergence is failure: where research follows discipline-driven Mode~1 priorities while policy discourse follows contexts of application, coverage-semantic distance may reflect productive exploration rather than misalignment.

\subsection{Research Questions and Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}

The research question is operationalised by four sub-hypotheses, each correlating a distinct coverage predictor with the within-SDG semantic gap across the 17 SDGs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1a} (motivating hypothesis): coverage gap $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap;
  \item \textbf{H1b}: research--policy dominance $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap;
  \item \textbf{H1c}: research coverage $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap;
  \item \textbf{H1d}: policy coverage $\leftrightarrow$ semantic gap.
\end{itemize}
SDG~4 is treated as artefact-affected and examined separately (Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}). The operationalization of these tests, Pearson $r$ and Spearman $\rho$ between each predictor and semantic-gap scores including the $n = 17$ scope conditions, is described in Section~\ref{sec:coverage-semantic-interaction}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Research Corpus}\label{sec:research-corpus}

This study uses publicly available text data and requires no ethical approval.

The research corpus consists of \NResearchAbstracts{} research abstracts retrieved from the OpenAlex API \parencite{Priem2022}, covering publications between 2018 and 2025. Retrieval combined OpenAlex's native SDG work filter with four high-precision AI search terms (\texttt{artificial intelligence}, \texttt{machine learning}, \texttt{deep learning}, \texttt{neural network}), giving 68 queries (17 SDGs × 4 terms). The term set draws on established practice for delimiting AI corpora \parencite{Elsevier2019, OECDAiMethod}, capturing a focused subset rather than the full universe of AI-related terms. Because this study measures semantic alignment between research and policy rather than counting publications, retrieval favours precision: each search term must clearly identify AI-related work, accepting that some relevant papers may be missed, to keep non-AI papers out of the embedding space. Complete query parameters are documented in the Supplementary Methodology (Appendix~\ref{app:supp-methodology}).

A direct test re-ran retrieval using field-of-study membership for the same two AI fields \parencite{OECDAiMethod} and re-scored the resulting 100,000-paper corpus through the identical pipeline. Coverage and semantic-gap rankings are stable under this alternative strategy (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}; Appendix~\ref{app:concept-retrieval-sensitivity}).

An important asymmetry follows: the research corpus is a tight intersection of AI and SDG content ($\text{AI} \cap \text{SDG}$), while the policy corpus (Section~\ref{sec:policy-corpus}) is a broader union covering institutional SDG discourse and AI governance. Following \textcite{Armitage2020}, the retrieval universe is a method-dependent corpus boundary. After deduplication and removal of records lacking usable abstracts, \NResearchAbstracts{} abstracts were retained and tokenised into \NResearchSegments{} segments (\NResearchSegPerAbstract{} segments per abstract on average; \NResearchMultiSegPct{}\% exceed the 374-token encoder window and split into multiple segments). A small number of records fail the 20-word segment minimum and are excluded: \NResearchPreprocessed{} records survive preprocessing, of which \NResearchDroppedAtSegmentation{} are dropped at segmentation because their combined text is too short (mostly very short abstracts). This is a documented scope boundary, and the dropped share is below 0.3\%. The corpus is predominantly English but no hard language filter is applied: roughly 0.5\% of abstracts are predominantly non-English and are retained. The semantic-gap ranking reproduces under subsampling to 10{,}000 papers (Appendix~\ref{app:balanced-subset-stability}), two orders of magnitude larger than the non-English share. No balancing by SDG or citation count is applied.

\subsection{Policy Corpus}\label{sec:policy-corpus}

The policy corpus contains \NPolicySegments{} text segments from three collections, representing \NPolicyDocs{} unique source documents. It should be read as mixed \emph{international institutional SDG policy discourse} rather than the full universe of AI sustainability policy:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Curated AI and SDG policy documents} (13,123 segments, 94 documents): UN, OECD, EU, UK, and AU AI governance frameworks; IPCC assessment reports; SDSN sustainable development reports; and national AI strategies.

  \item \textbf{SDGi Voluntary National Review and Voluntary Local Review corpus} \parencite{SDGi2024} (21,346 segments, 4,225 documents): National and local self-assessments submitted to the UN High-Level Political Forum.

  \item \textbf{UN General Debate Corpus} \parencite{JankinEtAl2024} (6,128 segments, 2,048 documents): SDG-relevant passages from General Debate speeches, sessions 70--80 (2015--2024); paragraph-level keyword filtering precedes segmentation, so only SDG-relevant passages enter the pipeline, which is why its 6,128 segments are far fewer than the full corpus of speeches.
\end{enumerate}

After cross-source deduplication by exact text match (SDGi retained first), \NPolicySegments{} unique segments remained. The curated sub-corpus (94 documents) is the more relevant reference for AI-governance-specific interpretation; Appendix~\ref{app:policy-source-data} reports a source-family sensitivity check.

\subsection{Embedding Model and Normalisation}\label{sec:embedding-choice}

All texts were encoded with \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} \parencite{all-mpnet-base-v2, Reimers2019} from the sentence-transformers library, producing 768-dimensional dense vectors. No fine-tuning is applied. Encoder choice is a principal robustness axis: gap rankings are re-computed under two alternative encoders, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \parencite{all-minilm-l6-v2} (384-dimensional) and SciBERT \parencite{Beltagy2019} (768-dimensional). Embeddings were L2-normalised to unit vectors so that cosine similarity equals their dot product.

\subsection{Token-Aware Segmentation}\label{sec:segmentation}

All source documents are segmented at sentence boundaries using the embedding model's own tokenizer to enforce a hard token budget, guaranteeing that every segment fits within the encoder's context window. The procedure applies identically to research abstracts, policy documents, and reference corpora: NLTK sentence-boundary detection is followed by greedy accumulation of sentences up to a token budget of \texttt{max\_seq\_length} $- 10$ (for the canonical MPNet encoder, 384 $-$ 10 = 374 tokens). Segments falling below 20 words are discarded; a single sentence exceeding the token budget is kept whole because mid-sentence splitting is not permissible.

For the research--policy comparison this design has two consequences. First, research abstracts (mean $\sim$200 words) mostly fit within the 374-token budget, but \NResearchMultiSegPct{}\% exceed it and split into multiple segments; the research-side text unit is therefore the abstract, whose segments are aggregated to a single document vector before assignment, not the individual segment. Second, policy documents of variable length are split into multiple segments of comparable size to research abstracts, making the two corpora directly comparable in embedding space. The per-document segment cap of $K=50$ applied when computing semantic-gap centroids (Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap-method}) is a separate, additional restraint on document-level dominance within a single SDG cluster; it does not affect the segmentation itself.

\subsection{Supervised Reference Classifier}\label{sec:supervised-classifier}

The canonical instrument is a supervised multinomial logistic regression (LR) classifier trained on pooled single-label reference data from five independent annotated corpora:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OSDG Community Dataset} \parencite{Pukelis2022} (30,532 texts, SDGs 1--16): crowdsourced excerpts validated by multiple volunteers.
  \item \textbf{SDG Classification Benchmark} \parencite{SDGBenchmark} (281 expert-verified texts, SDGs 1--17): expert-annotated snippets with consensus resolution.
  \item \textbf{IISD SDG Knowledge Hub} \parencite{Wulff2023SDGKnowledgeHub} (6,122 texts, SDGs 1--17): journalism and policy commentary on 2030 Agenda implementation.
  \item \textbf{SDGi Corpus} \parencite{SDGi2024} (20,267 texts, SDGs 1--17): government Voluntary National and Local Reviews.
  \item \textbf{Aurora dataset} \parencite{Vanderfeesten2020Aurora} (4,971 expert-validated texts, SDGs 1--17): academic abstracts validated by domain researchers.
\end{itemize}

OSDG alone contributes about 49\% of labelled texts and draws on UN-adjacent documents, so the reference data remain weighted toward policy vocabulary. OSDG and the Benchmark bypass segmentation; the Knowledge Hub, SDGi, and Aurora are segmented before pooling.

The pooled reference data (n=\NReferencePool{} after single-label filtering) were split into training and held-out test sets (n=\NTrainPool{} train, n=\NTestPool{} test) by stratified document-grouped splitting, keeping all texts from the same source document in one fold to enforce strict separation between training and evaluation. The champion LR ($C=3.0$, L2 penalty, \texttt{class\_weight=None}, solver \texttt{lbfgs}) was selected by 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation on the training pool and reaches macro-$F_1$ = \MacroFOne{} on the held-out test set (Section~\ref{sec:validation}). The grid-search protocol, hyperparameter rationale, and the MLP robustness comparison are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:model-selection}. The validation task does not evaluate a rejection option for non-SDG texts; this is acceptable because the research corpus is first restricted to an OpenAlex AI-and-SDG universe, so the classifier answers a relative question among the 17 SDGs.

Each text is assigned to its highest-probability SDG (argmax), traceable to 768 signed coefficients per class with no hidden layers, no dropout, and no random-seed sensitivity. Macro-$F_1$, the unweighted mean of per-SDG scores, is the primary validation metric (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

Because the classifier is trained on pooled reference data that are themselves policy-adjacent, the scoring instrument behaves differently on the two corpora. Policy segments receive systematically higher LR predicted probabilities than research abstracts (mean \AffFifteenPolicyScore{} vs \AffFifteenPaperScore{}, a \AffFifteenDiff{} gap). The gap has two causes the data cannot separate: a genre artefact (the reference data skew toward policy vocabulary) or a substantive difference (policy texts address SDG implementation more directly). Either way the asymmetry does not threaten the findings: hard argmax assignment uses only within-corpus ranking, not absolute probability levels, so the directional gap does not reorder goals. It is not an SDGi circularity effect: the training set and scored policy corpus are disjoint by construction.

\subsection{Coverage Gap Analysis}

For each corpus item, the predicted SDG is the LR argmax (hard assignment, Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}); the coverage profile is the proportion of items assigned to each SDG.

The policy corpus is not a flat set of documents: its \NPolicySegments{} segments are grouped into \NPolicyDocs{} source documents of very different lengths (Section~\ref{sec:policy-corpus}). A segment-weighted profile would let a handful of long reports dominate the count, so the analysis instead computes a \emph{document-weighted} profile in which every source document counts equally regardless of length. Concretely: segment vectors are averaged per document, hard-assigned to the top SDG, and the coverage profile counts documents (not segments) per SDG. The research corpus uses the same logic at the paper level: each of the \NResearchAbstracts{} abstracts is represented by the mean of its segments, hard-assigned as a single document unit, so the research-side text unit is the abstract and the two corpora are weighted identically.

The coverage gap for SDG $j$ is defined as:
\[
  \text{CoverageGap}_j = \left|\text{ResearchCoverage}_j - \text{PolicyCoverage}^{\text{docwt}}_j\right|
\]

The signed version of the same quantity is:
\[
  \text{SignedCoverageGap}_j = \text{ResearchCoverage}_j - \text{PolicyCoverage}^{\text{docwt}}_j
\]
A positive value means research dominates SDG $j$; a negative value means policy dominates.

\subsection{Semantic Gap Analysis}\label{sec:semantic-gap-method}

The semantic gap measures within-SDG divergence: given that both corpora have texts assigned to SDG $j$, how differently do they discuss it? For each SDG $j$, the analysis collects all assigned research papers and all assigned policy segments (capped at 50 per source document to prevent single-document dominance; SDSN reports alone contribute up to 3,179 segments otherwise), computes the L2-normalised mean embedding of each cluster ($\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j$, $\mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j$), and defines:

\[
\text{SemanticGap}_j = 1 - (\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j \cdot \mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j)
\]

A gap of 0 indicates perfectly overlapping semantic directions; a gap approaching~1 indicates orthogonal framing.

The \emph{per-document segment cap} of $K=50$ (seeded, reproducible) prevents single-document dominance; sensitivity at $K=20$ and no cap is reported in Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}.

\subsection{Register Adjustment via Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP)}\label{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}

The semantic gap is not a clean measure of substantive disagreement. Following Biber and Conrad's distinction between register, genre, and style \parencite{biberConrad2009register}, this study treats the research--policy contrast as a composite of register and topic: the two corpora differ in situation of use, audience, and communicative purpose (register), and also in substantive content (topic). Embedding distance therefore mixes genuine within-SDG substantive divergence with register divergence in abstraction, modality, and institutional stance \parencite{biber1988variation}. To separate these components, the semantic gap is decomposed into an adjusted semantic gap and a register component using Iterative Nullspace Projection \parencite[INLP]{Ravfogel2020INLP}. After register removal, the adjusted gap is the primary estimate of within-SDG topic divergence, while the residual register component captures the removed register signal (Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}).

\paragraph{Objective.}
Following \textcite{Ravfogel2020INLP}, the goal is to learn a linear projector that removes corpus-identity information from embeddings while preserving distances as much as possible. After $K$ iterations the composite projector $P$ removes all linearly decodable corpus signal; the orthogonal nullspace projection is the least harmful linear operation for this purpose.

\paragraph{SDG-stratified procedure.}
Vanilla INLP removes a binary attribute (e.g.\ gender). The present adaptation must remove \emph{register}, a corpus-level property that is confounded with SDG topic in the raw embeddings. The key modification is SDG stratification: at each iteration, exactly \RegisterIterNPerSdg{} research and \RegisterIterNPerSdg{} policy embeddings are drawn from each of the 17 SDGs, preventing the classifier from learning topic membership as a proxy for register. The procedure proceeds as follows at each iteration $k = 1, \ldots, K$:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Stratified sample.} Draw $n_{\text{target}} = \RegisterIterNPerSdg{}$ texts per SDG per corpus (global-minimum rule: $n_{\text{target}} = \min_j \min(|X_j^{\text{res}}|, |X_j^{\text{pol}}|)$; under the frozen snapshot, $n_{\text{target}} = 1207$ for MPNet). Form the 34-class stratification key $z_{\text{strat}} = 2 \times \text{sdg\_labels} + y$, ensuring balanced corpus $\times$ topic combinations.
\item \textbf{Project existing directions.} Subtract from each sample embedding the components along all previously learned directions: $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \sum_{j<k}(\mathbf{g}_j \cdot \mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{g}_j$.
\item \textbf{Train classifier.} Fit L2-regularised logistic regression ($C = 1.0$, solver: lbfgs, max\_iter: 1000) to predict corpus identity $y$ from the projected embeddings $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$. Split 15\% held-out, stratified by $z_{\text{strat}}$.
\item \textbf{Extract direction.} Normalise the weight vector: $\mathbf{g}_k = \mathbf{w}_k / \|\mathbf{w}_k\|$. Orthogonalise against all prior directions: $\mathbf{g}_k \leftarrow \mathbf{g}_k - \sum_{j<k}(\mathbf{g}_k \cdot \mathbf{g}_j)\,\mathbf{g}_j$. If $\|\mathbf{g}_k\| < 10^{-8}$ (direction collapse), stop.
\item \textbf{Stopping criterion.} Stop if held-out accuracy $\leq 0.5$ (the majority-class baseline for balanced binary classification). Otherwise append $\mathbf{g}_k$ and continue.
\end{enumerate}

The INLP projection iterates until held-out accuracy falls to $\leq 0.5$ (majority-class baseline). The implementation imposes a safety cap that is never reached (all three encoder tracks converge within \RegisterCrossMinilmIters{}--\RegisterCrossScibertIters{} iterations; Appendix~\ref{app:register-robustness}). The final adjusted embedding is $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} - \sum_{k=1}^{K}(\mathbf{g}_k \cdot \mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{g}_k$, and the adjusted gap uses centroids computed on these projected embeddings. Convergence behaviour is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:register-robustness} (Table~\ref{tab:iterative-register-check}).

\paragraph{Identification argument.}
The stratification design is the critical structural precondition: because every iteration trains on equal numbers of research and policy embeddings from each SDG, no linear direction can simultaneously encode SDG topic and corpus identity. What remains linearly decodable is the corpus-level register difference: modality, audience conventions, institutional stance, and structural patterns. The removed subspace is interpreted as register in the sense of \textcite{biberConrad2009register}: a variety associated with a particular situation of use, including communicative purpose, audience, and production circumstances. Because stratification balances SDG topic but not other variety axes, the removed component may combine register with period effects and author-background signals, so the register reading is a supported interpretation rather than an exhaustive characterisation \parencite{Grieve2025}. This interpretation is evaluated against independent linguistic markers in Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}; the validation finds substantial reduction of the corpus-level signal but not its complete elimination.

With the projector learned, the semantic-gap analysis of Section~\ref{sec:semantic-gap-method} is recomputed twice: once on the adjusted embeddings, yielding the within-SDG \emph{adjusted gap} (the primary estimate of topic divergence), and once on the removed component ($\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'$), yielding the \emph{register component} (the amount of the semantic gap attributable to register).

\subsection{Coverage--Semantic Interaction}\label{sec:coverage-semantic-interaction}

Their operationalization is as follows. Pearson $r$ and Spearman $\rho$ are computed between each coverage predictor (coverage gap, research--policy dominance, research coverage, policy coverage) and the within-SDG semantic-gap scores across the 17 SDGs; SDG~4 is treated as artefact-affected and examined separately (Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}).

The 17 SDGs are the complete framework, not a sample; the observed associations are properties of the framework, and the only stochastic element is the measurement process (classifier assignment, corpus construction, embeddings). All reported $p$-values are two-sided Monte Carlo permutation $p$-values (100{,}000 resamples, fixed seed): they condition on the measured values and test the null of no association between them, so no sampling-based power reasoning is involved \parencite{BerkWesternWeiss1995, AbadieAtheyImbensWooldridge2020}.

The operative small-$n$ concern is robustness rather than power: with only 17 framework units, individual goals can be influential, so the associations are read together with the leave-one-out check (Section~\ref{sec:interaction}) and the cross-configuration checks (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).

The raw, adjusted, and register-component correlations are a single decomposition (register = raw $-$ adjusted), mechanically linked; the substantive claim concerns their joint cancellation pattern, not three separate significance decisions. All three are reported unconditionally for every configuration, so the family is fully disclosed. Because the decomposition is pre-specified (Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}) and the statistics are jointly informative, no familywise correction is applied.

Finer-grained units (the framework defines 169 targets) would raise the observation count, but no labelled target-level reference data exist. A supplementary pooled-regression analysis combines all 24 configurations into a single panel (Appendix~\ref{app:regression}).

Register removal is validated against independent linguistic markers (Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}); the adjusted gap is the primary estimate.

Under the raw gap, SDG~3 leads for MPNet--LR while SDG~13 leads for MiniLM--LR and SciBERT--LR; SDG~17 trails under all three.

\textbf{Assignment-method sensitivity.} LR and MLP show varied agreement (MPNet LR vs.\ MLP $\rho = \MlpSemanticRho$; Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-semantic}). SDG~17 is most method-sensitive (rank~17 under LR, rank~8 under zero-shot).

\textbf{Distance-functional robustness.} Distribution-aware metrics (sliced Wasserstein, Chamfer, Gaussian-2-Wasserstein, MMD, energy, C2ST) correlate only moderately with the adjusted semantic gap ($\rho=\DistGapSpearmanSwd$ for sliced Wasserstein; Appendix~\ref{sec:distributional-robustness}). The adjusted gap is primarily a mean-direction finding; shape carries a related but distinct signal.

\textbf{Synthesis.} Two conclusions survive: SDG~15 is robustly least-divergent (rank~17 across configs); SDG~17 is most method-sensitive and most divergent under the adjusted gap, while SDG~3 leads under the raw gap for MPNet--LR. The cancellation replicates across all configs and policy-source families. Pooled OLS confirms the adjusted association (Appendix~\ref{app:regression}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The headline result is a cancellation: the raw correlation is null because register and topic offset ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic$ vs.\ $\rho = \RhoCovRegister$; Table~\ref{tab:interaction}).

\subsection{The cancellation is real, not artefactual}

Three potential confounds must be ruled out before the cancellation can be interpreted substantively.

\textbf{Independence of classification and measurement.} The LR classifier and the embedding space are separate instruments: classification uses softmax probabilities; measurement uses cosine distance between L2-normalised centroids.

\textbf{Cross-configuration replication.} The cancellation replicates across 8 of 9 configs (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}), including concept retrieval and MLP; the register component is negative in 7 of 9; the raw gap is null in all.

\textbf{SciBERT compression validates the register interpretation.} SciBERT compresses the raw gap from \MPNetMeanGap{} to \SciBERTMeanGap{} (88\% reduction) while preserving coverage ($\rho = \SciBERTCoverageRho$; Table~\ref{tab:encoder-sensitivity-coverage}). Scientific pretraining \parencite{Beltagy2019} fuses the registers because both use scientific conventions, exactly what the decomposition predicts.

\subsection{Two communities, two dimensions}

If the cancellation is real, it demands a theoretical explanation. The two-communities theory \parencite{Caplan1979} provides one. Caplan characterises social scientists and policy makers as living in ``separate worlds with different and often conflicting values, different reward systems, and different languages'' \parencite[459]{Caplan1979}. The gap, he argues, ``necessarily involves value and ideological dimensions as well as technical ones'' \parencite[461]{Caplan1979}. If the two communities are genuinely distinct knowledge cultures, then a near-zero raw correlation is \emph{expected}, not puzzling: the two corpora develop different topical priorities (producing coverage divergence) and different communicative norms (producing register divergence), and the two effects operate in opposite directions on the semantic gap. This is not a communication failure. Caplan himself warned that ``it does not follow\ldots\ that an alliance of social scientists and policy makers is the panacea'' \parencite[461]{Caplan1979}. The raw null is the expected signature of two distinct knowledge cultures coexisting without deep integration.

The adjusted association reveals something further: coverage and framing are \emph{separable dimensions}, not a single ``alignment'' property. As shown in Results (Section~\ref{sec:coverage-gap}), SDGs~9 and~17 illustrate this: SDG~9 has the second-largest coverage gap but a moderate semantic gap, while SDG~17 has moderate coverage but the widest adjusted gap. A single alignment score would conflate these two genuinely different patterns. This separability is the paper's core empirical contribution to the SDG-mapping literature, which has treated coverage as the primary output \parencite{Armitage2020, Kashnitsky2024}. The framework demonstrates that coverage and framing should be reported as independent axes.

The positive adjusted correlation ($\rho = +0.544$) further confirms that the two dimensions are linked through topic: where one community dominates a given SDG's coverage, the framing tends to diverge. The epistemic-communities framework \parencite{Haas1992} sharpens this reading. Haas argues that ``before states can agree on whether and how to deal collectively with a specific problem, they must reach some consensus about the nature and scope of the problem'' \parencite[30]{Haas1992}. Where research dominates coverage (SDGs~3,~9), the framing reflects academic norms of knowledge production: ``problems set and solved in a context governed by the, largely academic, interests of a specific community'' \parencite[404]{Gibbons1994}. Where policy dominates (SDGs~13,~16,~17), the framing reflects institutional norms of coordination: ``knowledge carried out in a context of application'' \parencite[404]{Gibbons1994}. The coverage-semantic linkage is the empirical signature of two communities producing different framings of the same goals.

However, Haas also cautions that ``given the many examples of different states reacting in different ways to the same consensual evidence provided by specialists, it is unclear how effective consensual knowledge is, as an independent variable, at explaining or predicting state behavior'' \parencite[31]{Haas1992}. The adjusted association shows that coverage and framing are linked through topic; it does not show that this linkage produces policy uptake.

The directional decomposition (H1b--H1d) further refines the picture, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:interaction}. H1d (policy coverage) is the strongest consistent predictor: positive in 9 of 9 configurations, significant at $p < 0.05$ for MPNet LR ($\rho = +0.589$) and Concept LR ($\rho = +0.510$). The linkage operates through the policy side: where policy heavily covers an SDG, framing divergence is wider, consistent with Haas's observation that epistemic communities occupy institutional niches \parencite[31]{Haas1992} and with the Mode~1/Mode~2 distinction \parencite{Gibbons1994}.

\subsection{The register decomposition: what the raw gap actually measures}

For 16 of 17 SDGs, the register component is positive (\RegCompSdgTen{} to \RegCompSdgThirteen{}; Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}), meaning register uniformly inflates the apparent divergence. The implication is stark: any SDG-mapping study that uses raw embedding distances without register adjustment will overestimate semantic divergence. The raw gap is a composite of topic, register, and convention; only the adjusted gap isolates topic.

The exception is SDG~17, where the register component is negative (\RegCompSdgSeventeen{}): register makes the two communities appear \emph{more similar} than their topics warrant. Under the raw gap SDG~17 ranks near the bottom (0.177); after register removal it jumps to the top (0.326). Policy partnership discourse uses institutional language (\textit{countries}, \textit{nations}, \textit{global}) that superficially resembles research vocabulary, but the substance differs: research concerns technical terms (\textit{model}, \textit{method}), while policy concerns institutional coordination.

SDG~17 is where framing power operates most invisibly: partnership language \emph{sounds} like either community, but the coordination logic belongs exclusively to policy. LeBlanc \parencite{LeBlanc2015} and ICSU \parencite{ICSU2017} describe SDG~17 as ``implementation infrastructure rather than substantive domains''; the register inversion confirms this empirically.

The register decomposition also reveals what the embedding instrument actually measures. Embedding distance is a composite signal of topic and register \parencite{Harris1954}. The INLP procedure with SDG stratification \parencite{Ravfogel2020INLP} exploits the structural fact that the 17 SDGs supply a near-orthogonal topic set that cross-cuts both registers, allowing linear separation. The method's applicability is bounded by the ontology: in domains where topic and register are tightly confounded (e.g., clinical notes versus biomedical literature), the projection would leak topic into the register direction.

\subsection{Implications: topical mention is not alignment}

Coverage, the Level~1 metric existing studies report, captures only a weak form of salience; it does not address credibility or legitimacy \parencite{Cash2003}. The adjusted gap is a Level~2 metric that begins to measure framing compatibility.

The high-gap SDGs (17, 3, 16, 9, 13) are candidates for boundary-organising practices \parencite{Guston2001}: policy synthesis briefs, joint workshops, or funder-required summaries. Cash et al.\ \parencite{Cash2003} caution that translation alone cannot resolve the tradeoffs among salience, credibility, and legitimacy.

Existing SDG-mapping studies report only coverage \parencite{Armitage2020}; this paper shows coverage and framing are separable dimensions.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Semantic proximity, not substantive alignment.} The study is positioned at Level~2 of a three-level construct (\S\ref{sec:alignment-framework}). Level~3 requires qualitative, institutional, and deliberative methods and is left to future work.

\textbf{Distance metric discards distributional shape.} Centroids are mean-direction measures. Under raw embeddings, distribution-aware metrics (sliced Wasserstein, Chamfer) correlated strongly with the semantic gap ($\rho = 0.44$--$0.91$), but this agreement was largely a register artifact: the distribution-aware metrics track the register component of the gap, not the adjusted semantic gap (Appendix~\ref{sec:distributional-robustness}). After register removal, the adjusted semantic gap is a mean-direction measure, and distribution-aware metrics correlate only moderately with it (e.g.\ Gaussian-2-Wasserstein $\rho=\DistGapSpearmanFrechet$); they remain an informative full-distribution complement, not a re-ranking. The Wasserstein shape term accounts for $\approx\DistGapShapeShareMean\%$ of the distance. Sampling stability is verified with a replicate seed (maximum gap change $\DistGapMaxSeedDelta$).

\textbf{Corpus scope and asymmetric construction.} The research corpus is AI~$\cap$~SDG (tight intersection); the policy corpus is broader institutional SDG discourse. The comparison is inherently asymmetric because ``AI and the SDGs'' is not yet a mainstream organising category in policy discourse. The curated AI/SDG policy family (94 documents) is underpowered for the symmetric check. The asymmetry was addressed by re-testing the central interaction on the curated AI/SDG family (Section~\ref{sec:interaction}, Appendix~\ref{app:policy-source-data}); broadening the research side to all-SDG research would complete the symmetry and is left to future work.

\textbf{Register adjustment is partial.} INLP removes a linear subspace that captures the dominant corpus-level register difference, but register is multi-layered \parencite{biber1988variation}. The register interpretation rests on a structural argument (SDG stratification ensures topic cannot leak into the removed direction; \S\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp}) plus partial empirical validation against independent linguistic markers (Appendix~\ref{app:register-validation}). The validation finds substantial reduction of the corpus-level signal but not its complete elimination.

\textbf{Per-SDG assignment is approximate.} The hard argmax treats SDG discourse as cleanly separable, but the PCA landscape (Figure~\ref{fig:pca-register-before-after}) shows real-world SDG discourse is fuzzy. SDG~4 is artefact-affected by the machine-learning/education ``learning'' overlap; its rank is read conditional on the lexical audit (Appendix~\ref{app:sdg4-lexical-audit}). Leave-one-out of SDG~4 preserves the adjusted association ($\rho = 0.597$, $p = 0.016$).

\textbf{Cross-sectional design.} The study observes one period (2018--2025) against a similarly timed policy corpus; it cannot assess whether alignment is improving or deteriorating, or whether specific events (e.g., the EU AI Act 2024, IPCC AR6 2022) \parencite{EUAI2024, IPCC2022} shifted research framing.

\subsection{The framework as the main contribution}

The primary contribution of this paper is the measurement framework, not the specific AI--SDG findings. The framework advances AI--SDG mapping from Level~1 (lexical correspondence) to Level~2 (observed semantic proximity), with explicit marking of Level~3 boundaries (\S\ref{sec:alignment-framework}). The cross-sensitivity transparency (Table~\ref{tab:cross-sensitivity-robustness}) is a reusable template: instead of reporting a single gap ranking, it makes the stability of every position transparent across the measurement choices analysts control. The register decomposition via SDG-stratified INLP is a methodological innovation applicable to any cross-corpus comparison where topic and register are confounded. The protocol is portable to any research-policy interface that shares a category system and embeds both sides in a common semantic space.

The AI--SDG findings are a first application to be checked against a larger, more diverse policy corpus, not settled results. The findings reported here, the cancellation, the SDG~17 inversion, and the positive adjusted association, are properties of this specific pair of corpora at this moment in time. The framework that produced them is designed to endure.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The Introduction asked whether shared SDG labels between academic AI research and institutional policy discourse indicate genuine alignment. The answer is no, not without examining framing. This paper demonstrated that coverage divergence (which SDGs each community prioritises) and within-SDG semantic framing divergence (how differently they discuss the same goal) are separable dimensions that can diverge independently: SDG~9 has the second-largest coverage gap but a moderately ranked semantic gap, while SDG~17 has moderate coverage but the widest adjusted gap. The raw correlation between the two dimensions is near-zero (Spearman $\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$), but this masks a register-topic cancellation: after INLP register removal, the adjusted semantic gap is positively associated with coverage divergence ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic$, $p = \RhoCovTopicP$), confirming that the two communities' different topical priorities are accompanied by different framings once register is accounted for.

The practical implication is that topical mention is not alignment. SDG tags, badges, and publication counts capture only whether a text is \emph{about} a goal, not how it frames it. The register decomposition reveals that embedding distance, like keyword matching, is a composite signal: for 16 of 17 SDGs, register uniformly inflates the apparent divergence between research and policy, making the two communities seem more different than their topics warrant. The single exception, SDG~17, is the most instructive case: policy partnership discourse uses institutional language that superficially resembles research vocabulary, masking the deepest topic divergence in the framework. Reporting both dimensions, and acknowledging the register component, would at minimum make the gap visible.

The framework is the paper's primary contribution. It advances AI--SDG mapping from Level~1 (lexical correspondence) to Level~2 (observed semantic proximity), with explicit marking of Level~3 boundaries (\S\ref{sec:alignment-framework}). The cross-sensitivity grid, the register decomposition, and the coverage--semantic separability test are portable to any research-policy interface that shares a category system and embeds both sides in a common semantic space. Level~3, whether the divergences documented here reflect productive exploration, institutional failure, or simply different knowledge cultures pursuing different mandates, requires qualitative, institutional, and deliberative methods that lie beyond the scope of embedding analysis. The coverage patterns it captures are externally valid, reproducing the SDG ranking that prior keyword- and citation-based inventories established. The framework does not solve the alignment problem. It makes the problem visible.

\subsection{Retrieval and Query Chain}
\label{app:supp-retrieval}

The research corpus was retrieved from the OpenAlex API \parencite{Priem2022} using 68 queries formed by the cross-product of OpenAlex's native SDG work filter and four AI search terms (\texttt{artificial intelligence}, \texttt{machine learning}, \texttt{deep learning}, \texttt{neural network}), restricted to \texttt{publication\_year > 2017}. The 2018 start date reflects the post-AlexNet \parencite{NIPS2012_c399862d} emergence of deep learning as the dominant paradigm and a research-uptake lag after the SDGs' adoption in 2015. OpenAlex SDG metadata defines the candidate universe; the SDG assignments used in the analysis are produced by the independently validated supervised classifier (Section~\ref{sec:supervised-classifier}). Complete query construction and credential handling are implemented in \path{1_code/0_fetch/fetch_openalex.py}.

The retrieval boundary is a deliberate scope condition. Elsevier's data-driven characterisation of AI research draws on over 700 field-specific keywords across some 600,000 documents \parencite{Elsevier2019}, favouring recall. The OECD.AI Observatory methodology instead defines a paper as AI when it carries the Artificial Intelligence or Machine Learning field of study in the Microsoft Academic Graph (MAG), whose Fields of Study taxonomy OpenAlex later inherited and extended by mapping it to Wikidata identifiers \parencite{OECDAiMethod, Priem2022, Wang2020}. The present design prioritises precision: a four-term query keeps non-AI noise out of the embedding space at the cost of recall in specialised AI subfields. \textcite{CSET2023} reach the same conclusion for AI-research identification, namely that results are sensitive to the retrieval method and that supervised classification is preferred.

A direct test of this scope condition re-ran retrieval using field-of-study membership for the same two AI fields, mirroring the OECD.AI definition \parencite{OECDAiMethod}, and re-scored the resulting corpus through the identical pipeline. Concept retrieval used OpenAlex concept tags for Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning, capped at \texttt{MAX\_PAPERS\_CONCEPT\_CORPUS} = 100{,}000 papers, yielding the roughly 100{,}000-paper concept corpus. This scale sits on the empirical variance plateau established by the sample-stability ladder (Appendix~\ref{app:sample-stability}): at 50,000 papers the coverage-profile standard deviation is \SampleMacroVarianceFiftyK{} and the mean within-SDG semantic gap is \SampleMeanSemanticGapFiftyK{}, within ~0.002 of the full-corpus estimate of \SampleMeanSemanticGapTwoM{} (full corpus N = \SampleStabilityFullCorpusN), while policy-text calibration bias has also converged (\SamplePolicyBiasFiftyK{}). The scale also exceeds every corpus size used in prior SDG classification and mapping benchmarks, namely the OSDG community dataset (30,534 texts) \textcite{Pukelis2022}, the SDG benchmark encoder evaluation corpus (35,001 papers) \textcite{Gjorgjevikj2025}, the SDGi corpus (20,267 segments) \textcite{SDGi2024}, the Aurora expert-validated set (4,971 texts) \textcite{Vanderfeesten2020Aurora}, and the 6,000-paper benchmark by \textcite{Kashnitsky2024}, while remaining far smaller than the full \NResearchSegments{}-segment research corpus and thus a computationally tractable substrate for OpenAlex concept tagging.

The adjusted semantic gap is a mean-direction estimate, and the distribution-aware distances are a complementary full-distribution view. Future work should report both and, where the two disagree, investigate whether the divergence is shape-driven (as here) or a residual artefact.

\section{Model Selection: Grid Search Protocol and Rationale}
\label{app:model-selection}

\subsection{Models Tested}

Three model families were evaluated under 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation (document-level grouping, not random splits) on the pooled training data (n=\NTrainPool{}). The test set (n=\NTestPool{}) was never touched during any grid search.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Logistic Regression (LR)}: Interpretable per-dimension coefficients per SDG; fast; standard multiclass baseline.
  \item \textbf{MLP (PyTorch)}: Can capture non-linear interactions in the embedding space.
  \item \textbf{RF / XGB}: Considered but excluded because the embedding space is already a learned representation  --  bagged trees add ensemble complexity without a clear mechanism for improvement over LR/MLP, and would not produce interpretable coefficients per dimension.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{LR Grid Search}

The LR grid searched C $\in \{0.1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 30, 100\}$ with L2 penalty and class\_weight $\in \{\text{None}, \text{balanced}\}$ (16 L2 configurations). The best L2 configuration was C=3.0, penalty=l2, class\_weight=None (CV macro-$F_1$ = \LrCvMacroFone{} $\pm$ \LrCvMacroFoneStd{}). C=1.0, C=2.0 and C=5.0 lie within $\sim$0.0004 of the optimum, while C=10.0 is $\sim$0.0026 below; C=30 and C=100 degrade clearly (0.804 and 0.797), confirming the optimum is a moderately regularised C$\approx$3 and that weaker regularisation overfits. C=3.0 was selected as the empirical optimum. A separate L1 (lasso) spot-check (C=10) reached CV macro-$F_1$ = 0.8066 $\pm$ 0.0069, below the L2 champion, confirming that L1 penalisation is not competitive for this embedding geometry (prior spot-check documented in 5\_notes/MODEL\_SELECTION.md).

\subsection{MLP Grid Search}

The MLP swept n\_layers $\in \{2, 3, 4, 6\}$ $\times$ hidden\_size $\in \{256, 384\}$ $\times$ lr $\in \{3\mathrm{e}{-4}, 1\mathrm{e}{-3}\}$ (16 configurations) with weight\_decay=0 and dropout=0.3, trained with AdamW and early stopping. The best MLP (3 layers, 256 hidden, lr=3e-4) achieved CV macro-$F_1$ = \MlpCvMacroFone{} $\pm$ \MlpCvMacroFoneStd{}. Depth 3 was optimal; depth 6 did not improve over depth 3, and depth 2/4 were within $\sim$0.001, so architecture is not the binding constraint. Learning rate had negligible impact: across the two learning rates the CV macro-$F_1$ range at the best architecture was \MlpCvRangeLrSweep{}.

\noindent\textbf{Champion: LR (C=3.0, L2).} The gap between LR and MLP is \LrMlpGap{} macro-$F_1$, a modest difference insufficient to justify the complexity trade-off. MLP requires 3 hidden layers, AdamW, learning rate tuning, early stopping, and dropout selection (16 configurations swept). LR requires one free parameter (C), no hidden layers, no dropout, no random seed sensitivity. For this study's purpose  --  building an interpretable supervised reference to compare corpora, not a classifier built to label single documents  --  LR is the right choice. LR provides 768 signed coefficients per SDG class, each directly attributable to a specific semantic dimension; MLP's hidden layers diffuse this information across non-linear transformations. The macro-$F_1$ numbers are reported honestly: LR \LrCvMacroFone{} $\pm$ \LrCvMacroFoneStd{}, MLP \MlpCvMacroFone{} $\pm$ \MlpCvMacroFoneStd{}, gap \LrMlpGap{} macro-$F_1$. LR is chosen for transparency, not superior accuracy.

\subsection{Held-Out Test Evaluation of the MLP Robustness Check}

The MLP test macro-$F_1$ (\MlpMacroFOne{}) cited in Section~\ref{sec:validation} is a separate evaluation from the CV grid-search results above. After the CV sweep, the champion MLP architecture (3 layers, 256 hidden, lr=3e-4) was retrained on the full training pool (n=\NTrainPool{}) and evaluated once against the held-out test set (n=\NTestPool{}), the same sanctioned single-use test evaluation applied to the LR champion. The resulting test macro-$F_1$ of \MlpMacroFOne{} is not directly comparable to the CV mean of \MlpCvMacroFone{}~$\pm$~\MlpCvMacroFoneStd{} from the grid search: the CV number is an average across five document-grouped folds on the training pool alone, while the test number is a single evaluation on a disjoint held-out sample after retraining on the full pool. Both numbers confirm that the LR--MLP gap is modest and that architecture choice is not the binding constraint on classification quality.

\paragraph{Convergence results.}\label{app:register-inlp}
Held-out accuracy dropped from \RegisterFirstAcc{} (iteration~1) to \RegisterFinalAcc{} by iteration~\RegisterIterFinalK{}, at which point no further linear register separation was detectable. The Spearman rank correlation between the iteration~1 and iteration~\RegisterIterFinalK{} gap vectors was \RegisterIterSpearmanRho{}: the additional removed directions shifted the relative SDG ordering substantially. The mean gap compresses from \MeanRawGap{} to \MeanAdjustedGap{}, a reduction of \RegisterIterReductionPct{}\%, before plateauing. The Spearman $\rho$ decays rapidly in the first 15 iterations (1.000 to \RegisterIterRhoAtFifteen{}) and then stabilises around \RegisterIterPlateauRho{}, indicating that the first 15 orthogonal directions capture the bulk of the register signal.

The decomposition reveals a striking pattern for SDG~17 (Partnerships for the Goals). Under the raw semantic gap, SDG~17 shows the smallest gap (\RawGapSdgSeventeen{}, rank 17 of 17), suggesting near-identical framing. After register removal, the adjusted gap is \AdjGapSdgSeventeen{}, the largest adjusted gap in the corpus (rank 1). The register component is \RegCompSdgSeventeen{}, the only negative value, indicating that register actually \emph{increases} the semantic similarity for this policy-dominant SDG: the policy discourse on partnerships uses register conventions that make it appear closer to research than the underlying topic content warrants. For all other SDGs, the register component is positive (range: \RegCompSdgFifteen{} to \RegCompSdgThirteen{}), confirming that register uniformly inflates the raw gap. The headline correlation pattern is consistent with the apparent cancellation: the raw coverage-vs-gap correlation is near-zero ($\rho = \SpearmanCovRaw$, $p = \SpearmanCovRawP$) because the register component ($\rho = \RhoCovRegister$, $p = \RhoCovRegisterP$) appears to cancel the topic signal ($\rho = \RhoCovTopic$, $p = \RhoCovTopicP$; Table~\ref{tab:register-decomposition}).

The same iterative procedure was run on the MiniLM and SciBERT encoder tracks (the latter two on the S1 subset, by design); all three reach the $\leq 0.5$ stopping threshold. \subsection{Construct Validation: Purpose and Validation Question}\label{app:register-validation}
\label{sec:regval-purpose}

The register adjustment of Section~\ref{sec:register-adjustment-inlp} removes, through Iterative Nullspace Projection, the subspace of the embedding space that is linearly decodable as corpus identity. The structural argument for reading that subspace as register is that every INLP iteration is trained on SDG-balanced research and policy embeddings: no linear direction can simultaneously encode SDG topic and corpus identity, so what remains linearly decodable is the corpus-level register difference. That argument guarantees \emph{what} is removed, a corpus-separable linear component, but it does not by itself establish what that component is. This appendix evaluates the register interpretation empirically, using an independent surface-linguistic register score computed from the segment texts, following Biber-style register features \parencite{biber1988variation}.

The validation can establish three things: whether the adjustment removes a large corpus-separable component; whether the removal is accompanied by evidence that SDG (topic) structure is being destroyed; and whether apparent residual register signals survive controlled samples. It cannot establish that the removed subspace is identical to the measured linguistic register features. The register score used here is a small set of surface indicators, and the validation is single-encoder and sample-limited. The conclusion is correspondingly bounded: the evidence is consistent with a register reading and provides partial, not complete, empirical support for it.

\subsection{Construct Validation: Register Operationalisation and Sample Construction}
\label{sec:regval-operationalisation}

\textbf{Sample construction.} All results use the canonical MPNet track, \RegValSampleN{} segments per sample (12 per SDG per corpus, seed 42), and the same frozen projection matrix $G$ as the main analysis. A small number of long flagship reports (the SDSN Sustainable Development Reports 2024 and 2025, the UNDP Human Development Report 2021/22, the WHO report on ethics and governance of AI for health, the EU AI Act, and two UN SDG progress reports) each contribute many near-duplicate segments: \RegValMegaDocs{} source documents, global flagship reports (the SDSN Sustainable Development Reports 2024 and 2025, the UNDP Human Development Report 2021/22, the WHO report on ethics and governance of AI for health, the EU AI Act, and two UN SDG progress reports), contribute \RegValMultiParentUnits{} segments across \RegValMegaSdgs{} of the 17 SDGs. These segments are register outliers (mean sentence length \RegValMegaMeanSentLen{} words vs.\ \RegValNonMegaMeanSentLen{} for the remaining units; passive rate \RegValMegaPassive{} vs.\ \RegValNonMegaPassive{}; first-person rate \RegValMegaFirstPerson{} vs.\ \RegValNonMegaFirstPerson{}; nominalisation rate \RegValMegaNominal{} vs.\ \RegValNonMegaNominal{}), and because segments of the same document sit close together in embedding space they can create spurious pooled correlations.

The primary validation therefore uses a one-per-parent design (global deduplication by source document; \RegValOppDistinctParents{} distinct parents, no multi-parent units), which caps each flagship document at a single segment and replaces excluded flagship segments with other policy segments from the same SDG, drawn predominantly from shorter national and thematic monitoring reports. The two designs differ partly in composition, which the Limitations return to.

\textbf{Register score.} For every sampled segment, six register features are computed per 1{,}000 words: hedge-word rate, deontic-modality rate, passive-voice frequency (a be-form immediately preceding a past participle, allowing one intervening adverb), mean sentence length, first-person-pronoun rate, and nominalisation rate; and the first principal component of the standardised features is taken as the register score. The orientation (positive loading on sentence length) is a sign convention only; the score's interpretation does not rest on any single feature, and sentence length in particular is composition-sensitive, as shown below.

\textbf{Corpus feature contrasts.} With the sample structure established, the feature differences can be read correctly. Policy segments are markedly more deontic (\RegValCorpusDeonticPol{} vs.\ \RegValCorpusDeonticRes{} per 1{,}000 words) and research segments hedge more (\RegValCorpusHedgeRes{} vs.\ \RegValCorpusHedgePol{}) and passivise more (\RegValCorpusPassiveRes{} vs.\ \RegValCorpusPassivePol{}); these contrasts are stable across samples and point in the directions commonly associated with institutional-modality and academic writing register, respectively. First-person and nominalisation rates are roughly balanced at the corpus level (\RegValCorpusFirstPersonPol{} vs.\ \RegValCorpusFirstPersonRes{}; \RegValCorpusNominalPol{} vs.\ \RegValCorpusNominalRes{}), the flagship segments being the outliers on both. Mean sentence length is the one feature whose corpus direction is not robust: policy segments appear much longer on the original sample (\RegValCorpusSentLenPol{} vs.\ \RegValCorpusSentLenRes{} words), but this contrast is driven by the flagship-document segments, whose extraction-artefact-inflated mean (\RegValMegaMeanSentLen{} words) does not generalise, and it reverses direction once those segments are set aside. Sentence length was therefore treated as composition-sensitive and used to motivate the robustness checks below rather than as a load-bearing indicator of register.

\subsection{Construct Validation: Corpus Discrimination Before and After INLP}
\label{sec:regval-discrimination}

A 5-fold logistic-regression classifier distinguishes research from policy segments almost perfectly on raw embeddings (accuracy \RegValAccRawOrig{} on the original sample; \RegValAccRawOpp{} on the one-per-parent sample; Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-accuracy}). After register removal the accuracy falls to \RegValAccAdjOrig{} on the original sample (95\% CI \RegValAccAdjOrigCi{}, binomial $p=\RegValAccAdjOrigP{}$ against 0.5) and to \RegValAccAdjOpp{} on the one-per-parent sample (95\% CI \RegValAccAdjOppCi{}, $p=\RegValAccAdjOppP{}$). The adjustment therefore removes a substantial share of the corpus-linear signal. The removal is not complete: the primary one-per-parent value is significantly above chance.

A per-SDG-deduplicated variant of the sample (dominated by the \RegValMultiParentUnits{} mega-document policy segments above) yields an adjusted accuracy of \RegValAccAdjOrig{}, indistinguishable from chance; excluding those segments raises it to \RegValAccMegaExcl{} (difference \RegValAccRise{}, 95\% CI \RegValAccRiseCi{}), confirming the mega-document clustering drives the original-sample result. The canonical one-per-parent estimate (\RegValAccAdjOpp{}) is the primary result; residual above-chance separation after removal is not a method failure, since INLP's stopping rule converges on its own training distribution while the validation classifier is re-estimated from scratch, so the two evaluations are not directly comparable.

The register-only rows of Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-accuracy} bound the interpretation. A classifier trained on the six surface features alone reaches only \RegValAccRegOrig{} on the original sample and \RegValAccRegOpp{} on the one-per-parent sample, barely above chance in the latter case. The linguistic markers therefore capture only a part of the corpus distinction. This is consistent with the register reading (the corpora do differ in register-like surface ways), but it also delimits it: the removed embedding component is far broader than these six surface indicators, so the validation cannot claim that the removed subspace is exhausted by, or identical to, the measured features.

\subsection{Construct Validation: Topic Preservation}
\label{sec:regval-topic}

If the adjustment removed topic, 17-way SDG selectivity, how well the SDG of a segment can be predicted from its embedding, would fall. It does not (Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-selectivity}): on the original per-SDG-dedup sample (draw~1), LR cross-validated accuracy is \RegValSelRawLr{} on raw and \RegValSelAdjLr{} on adjusted embeddings, and kNN \RegValSelRawKnn{} and \RegValSelAdjKnn{} (chance \RegValSelChance{}). The change is small and of mixed sign, and is best read as sampling noise around an unchanged quantity rather than as evidence of preservation in the strong sense. The correct statement is that there is no evidence that topic structure is systematically removed; the analysis does not claim that topic is preserved perfectly, and it was run on the original sample only, not re-estimated on the one-per-parent rebuild. Topic remains linearly decodable after register removal, as the identification argument anticipates.

\subsection{Construct Validation: Residual Register Diagnostics and Robustness Investigation}
\label{sec:regval-residual}

Two checks examine whether residual register survives adjustment. First, the register score correlates positively with the amount of embedding removed on the per-SDG-deduplicated sample (\RegValTwoBOrigPooled{}, $p = \RegValTwoBOrigPooledP{}$; within-research $\rho = \RegValTwoBOrigWithinRes{}$, within-policy $\rho = \RegValTwoBOrigWithinPol{}$). Second, distance to the SDG centroid in adjusted space correlates more strongly with the register score than in raw space (raw $\rho = \RegValTwoCOrigRaw{}$ rising to adjusted $\rho = \RegValTwoCOrigAdj{}$). Both signals are absent on the controlled one-per-parent sample: the removed-norm correlation becomes $\rho = \RegValTwoBOppPooled{}$ (n.s.; within-corpus \RegValTwoBOppWithinRes{} and \RegValTwoBOppWithinPol{}, both n.s.), and the centroid-distance correlation becomes negative (raw $\rho = \RegValTwoCOppRaw{}$; adjusted $\rho = \RegValTwoCOppAdj{}$). The original patterns are attributable to flagship-document clustering, register-extreme, mutually similar segments of a few documents dominating the pooled correlation, not to residual register, so the direct surface-linguistic corroboration of the removed subspace is partial: the register reading rests on the corpus-discrimination result and the absence of counter-evidence rather than a measured correspondence between the removed component and the six features.

A within-SDG decomposition (register score vs.\ distance to the \emph{other-corpus} SDG centroid, adjusted space) yields one nominally surviving trace among policy segments on the initial draw ($\rho = \RegValOtherDistRho{}$, $p = \RegValOtherDistP{}$; this value reproduced exactly on a re-run of the same sample and survived mega-document exclusion on that draw, $\rho = \RegValMegaExclDrawThree{}$, $p = \RegValMegaExclDrawThreeP{}$). A draw-stability check re-samples the one-per-parent construction with fresh random streams (seeds 43--45): the statistic is draw-unstable: $\rho = \RegValDrawFortyThree{}$ ($p = \RegValDrawFortyThreeP{}$), $\rho = \RegValDrawFortyFour{}$ ($p = \RegValDrawFortyFourP{}$), $\rho = \RegValDrawFortyFive{}$ ($p = \RegValDrawFortyFiveP{}$), with the sign flipping across draws and the mean near zero; and mega-document exclusion is likewise draw-specific ($\rho = \RegValMegaExclDrawOne{}$ n.s., $\rho = \RegValMegaExclDrawTwo{}$ n.s., $\rho = \RegValMegaExclDrawThree{}$ $p = \RegValMegaExclDrawThreeP{}$ on draws 1--3). Per-SDG, only \RegValPerSdgSig{} of 17 reach $p < 0.05$ (\RegValPerSdgNeg{} negative, \RegValPerSdgPos{} positive). The trace is therefore draw-level noise, not residual register structure.

\subsection{Construct Validation: Validation Conclusion}
\label{sec:regval-conclusion}

The validation supports the following claims. (i) The adjustment removes a substantial corpus-level component: corpus accuracy falls from \RegValAccRawOrig{} to \RegValAccAdjOrig{} on the original sample and from \RegValAccRawOpp{} to \RegValAccAdjOpp{} on the one-per-parent sample. (ii) The removed component is not strongly attributable to SDG topic loss: 17-way SDG selectivity is essentially unchanged. (iii) Apparent residual register signals were explained by sampling and document-clustering effects rather than by stable residual structure: the initial removed-magnitude and centroid-distance associations vanished or reversed on the controlled sample, and the one surviving within-SDG trace was draw-unstable. (iv) The remaining evidence provides partial empirical support for interpreting the removed component as register-related: the corpora differ in register-like surface features in stable directions, and the adjustment removes corpus-separable signal while leaving topic structure intact, but the direct correspondence between the removed component and the measured linguistic markers was not established, and residual corpus-linear signal remains after removal.

The register interpretation is therefore consistent with the validation evidence rather than confirmed by it. The conclusion should be read alongside the main text, which describes the register interpretation as resting on partial, not complete, empirical support.

\subsection{Construct Validation: Limitations}
\label{sec:regval-limitations}

The validation is bounded in ways that should be read alongside the main text. First, the register score is operationalised as the first principal component of six hand-specified features, and the choice of operationalisation is not resolved here: an alternative a-priori ``institutional'' z-score combination gave null or reversed results on the initial screen, and the score is oriented by a sign convention rather than by a substantive prior. Second, each sample contains only \RegValSampleN{} segments (12 per SDG per corpus), and the per-SDG tests within the draw-stability stage have $n = 12$ policy units each, so the \RegValPerSdgSig{}-of-17 significant per-SDG result is not evidence of absence at the per-SDG level. Third, the draw-stability check was applied to the within-SDG trace only; the corpus-level accuracies in Table~\ref{tab:register-validation-accuracy} were not re-estimated on fresh draws. Fourth, the one-per-parent sample is a rebuild, not a subset of the original sample, and the excluded flagship documents are replaced by shorter national and thematic monitoring documents, so the two-sample comparison is partly compositional; the mega-vs-non-mega feature contrast documents that shift, and the selectivity check was run on the original sample only. Fifth, the validation is MPNet-canonical only. Sixth, the residual corpus-linear signal that survives adjustment is not characterised further here; its exact linguistic content remains open, which is part of why the empirical support is partial rather than complete. Finally, two distinct statistics in this appendix round to $-$0.197 at three decimals: the one-per-parent pooled centroid-distance correlation (\RegValTwoCOppAdj{}) and the draw-stability-stage policy other-corpus distance correlation (\RegValOtherDistRho{}); they are different quantities and should not be conflated.

either metric -- itself informative: coverage divergence tracks the gap and policy share,
not dominance or raw research share). The only reversals are the two SciBERT configs; the
zero-shot config, already scoped as an uncertainty bound, is stable on the adjusted gap.
SciBERT is designated a lower-trust robustness encoder (its scientific pretraining
compresses gap magnitudes, $\SciBERTMeanGap{}$ against $\MPNetMeanGap{}$,
Section~\ref{sec:encoder-sensitivity}).

\emph{Possible causes of the SciBERT divergence.} (A) At $n=17$ the Pearson estimates are noisy and the permutation null is only rejected for large magnitudes: both SciBERT adjusted-gap values are small in absolute terms and do not reach significance, so the rank/magnitude sign flip is within estimation error. (B) Pearson weights absolute distances, Spearman weights ranks;
SciBERT's $\sim$8$\times$ smaller gap magnitudes make Pearson disproportionately
sensitive to its few larger gaps. (C) SciBERT has the lowest assignment fidelity of the
three encoders, giving noisier per-SDG gaps. (D) The flip is in the \emph{adjusted} gap,
and INLP removal is already documented as encoder-dependent. (E) Pearson is leveraged by
individual outliers (e.g.\ SDG~3). None of A--E is established; A and E are the most
directly testable. All are consistent with the divergence being uninformative for the
conclusion, which rests on the supervised tracks where the signal is large ($+0.70$) and
stable across metrics. The raw-value specification therefore confirms, rather than
threatens, the rank-based result.

\subsection{Detailed Policy Profiles by Source Family}
\label{app:policy-source-data}

The full policy corpus combines a curated AI/SDG sub-corpus, SDGi VNR/VLR reports, and UN General Debate speeches. These are all policy-facing texts, but they are not interchangeable genres. The family split reported in Table~\ref{tab:policy-source-family} therefore asks a narrow question: whether the full-corpus policy profile is broadly stable across source families or whether it is strongly shaped by corpus composition.

The answer is mixed. The broad international-institutional profile is clearly source-family sensitive. The curated AI/SDG family is much more innovation-oriented, with SDG 9 leading its document-weighted profile, while the SDGi and UNGDC families are more strongly concentrated on climate, partnerships, and governance, consistent with the UNGD topic distributions reported by \textcite{JankinEtAl2024}, and, in the SDGi case, SDG 11-style institutional review language. At the same time, the broader full-corpus pattern is not driven by one family alone: both the UNGDC speeches and the full corpus are dominated by the climate--partnerships--governance block, and SDG 17 remains the largest semantic-gap case across all three families.

The implication is interpretive rather than corrective. The full policy corpus should be read as international institutional SDG discourse, not as a universal profile of all AI sustainability policy. The scope of what the policy-side distribution represents is accordingly narrower, but the main diagnostic purpose remains valid.

\section{Pooled Regression: Coverage Predictors and the Semantic Gap}
\label{app:regression}

This appendix pools all 24 encoder--retrieval--method--cap configurations into a single panel ($N = 408$) and regresses the within-SDG semantic gap on coverage predictors using OLS with cluster-robust standard errors by SDG (17 clusters). The dependent variable is the rank of the semantic gap within each configuration (1--17 per config, ties averaged); predictors are expressed in percentage points (0--100). Table~\ref{tab:k1-specification-grid} reports the full 23-specification grid across core regressions (Panel~A), robustness checks (Panel~B), interaction terms (Panel~C), and alternative functional forms (Panel~D).

The adjusted semantic gap is positively associated with the coverage gap ($b = +0.18$, $p = 0.069$; Panel~A, column~1); the coefficient is marginally significant at conventional thresholds but is confirmed by the supervised-only subsample ($p = 0.040$), the MPNet-only subsample ($p = 0.003$), and the raw-DV specification in cosine units ($p = 0.007$). The raw gap and the register component show no detectable association with any coverage predictor, consistent with the cancellation pattern reported in Section~\ref{sec:interaction}. Policy coverage (the share of policy documents assigned to a given SDG) is a stronger predictor of the adjusted gap ($b = +0.41$, $p = 0.003$), capturing how concentrated policy attention is on each goal. The coverage-gap-by-encoder interaction (Panel~C) reveals that the effect is strongest for MPNet and reverses for SciBERT, suggesting that domain-specialised pretraining compresses the adjusted semantic gap regardless of coverage structure.
