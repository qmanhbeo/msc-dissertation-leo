\begin{abstract}
The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) provide the dominant international framework for sustainability governance, yet it remains unclear whether academic artificial intelligence (AI) research engages with policy priorities at the SDG level. Existing bibliometric studies map AI research to SDGs using keyword methods, but none has systematically compared the \emph{semantic content} of research and policy discourse or tested whether the two forms of topical gap are related. This study advances from lexical to topical alignment measurement using Sentence-BERT embeddings and a nearest-centroid semantic instrument validated against expert-labelled SDG benchmarks (macro-F1 = \MacroFOne). Applying this instrument to \NResearchPapers{} research abstracts and \NPolicyChunks{} policy document chunks from \NPolicyDocs{} sources, we find: (i) a pronounced coverage gap in which policy discourse concentrates on SDG 13 (Climate, \PolicyPctSdgThirteen\% of documents), SDG 17 (Partnerships, \PolicyPctSdgSeventeen\%), and SDG 16 (Governance, \PolicyPctSdgSixteen\%), while research concentrates on SDG 3 (Health, \ResearchPctSdgThree\%), SDG 4 (Education/Learning, \ResearchPctSdgFour\%), and SDG 9 (Innovation, \ResearchPctSdgNine\%); (ii) the largest within-SDG semantic gaps occur in SDG 17 (\SemanticGapSdgSeventeen), SDG 13 (\SemanticGapSdgThirteen), and SDG 10 (\SemanticGapSdgTen); (iii) research attention alone does not predict semantic divergence ($r = \HPrimaryPearsonR$, $p = \HPrimaryPearsonP$; $n = \HPrimaryN$), but SDGs with larger cross-corpus coverage imbalance tend to show larger semantic gaps; and (iv) policy chunks score higher against research-derived centroids (\HAsymPolicyScore) than research papers score against OSDG-derived centroids (\HAsymResearchScore), although this directional asymmetry remains inconclusive because the A15 calibration bias (\AffFifteenDiff) is larger than the observed H26 gap (\HAsymGap). These findings challenge the assumption that topical engagement implies substantive alignment and suggest that research-policy dialogue requires attention to semantic register, not only subject coverage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Artificial intelligence is now routinely presented as part of the solution to sustainable development. In academic research, papers increasingly claim that a model, application, or dataset contributes to one or more Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), with SDG attribution often functioning as a form of relevance-signalling beyond technical novelty. In policy discourse, international organisations, intergovernmental bodies, and national AI strategies likewise frame AI as an enabler or accelerator of SDG progress \parencite{UN2015, OECD2019, UNESCO2021, UKAI2021, UNAI2024}. Across these domains, a shared narrative has taken hold: AI is expected to help deliver the global development agenda.

That narrative matters because it shapes research framing, funding priorities, and policy expectations. Yet the apparent alignment it implies is usually inferred rather than demonstrated. Researchers and policymakers may converge on the same SDG labels while emphasising different problems, actors, timescales, and interventions. A paper and a policy document can both invoke SDG 3 (Good Health) or SDG 13 (Climate Action) without addressing the same substantive terrain. High-level thematic agreement can therefore mask deeper divergence. If so, current claims about ``AI for SDGs'' may overstate how far research effort is aligned with policy priorities, not because the narrative is necessarily false, but because the methods used to validate it remain too coarse.

The underlying problem is not new. Research-policy mismatch has been a central concern in policy studies and knowledge-utilisation scholarship for decades. What is new is the combination of this longstanding problem with the rise of AI-for-SDG framing and the availability of embedding-based semantic tools that make large-scale measurement possible. These tools allow alignment to be assessed as a property of discourse, rather than inferred only from anecdote, keyword coincidence, or aggregate counts. In that sense, the aim of this paper is deliberately specific: to test whether academic AI-for-sustainability research and SDG policy discourse actually occupy similar regions in semantic space.

A substantial body of bibliometric work documents \emph{what} AI research engages with: studies consistently find concentration on technically tractable SDGs such as SDG 3, SDG 7, SDG 9, and SDG 13, with relative neglect of SDGs 5, 10, 16, and 17 \parencite{Vinuesa2020, Singh2023, Cowls2021}. However, most of this literature maps research to the SDGs through keywords, topic counts, expert judgements, or bibliometric assignment. These approaches are valuable for measuring coverage, but they implicitly assume that shared SDG labels imply alignment. They can tell us which goals are mentioned or assigned; they cannot tell us whether research and policy mean similar things when they invoke the same goal. Nor do they test whether distributional overlap and semantic overlap move together or come apart. Shared labels, in short, do not by themselves establish shared meaning.

This study addresses that gap. Its central claim is straightforward: alignment cannot be inferred from topical overlap alone. We make three contributions. First, we advance AI--SDG mapping from Level 1 (lexical or bibliometric correspondence) to Level 2 (topical proximity in semantic space), using Sentence-BERT \parencite{Reimers2019} embeddings and SDG centroids derived from the OSDG Community Dataset \parencite{Pukelis2022}. Second, we distinguish between a \emph{coverage gap} (which SDGs each corpus emphasises) and a \emph{semantic gap} (how differently the two corpora discuss the same SDG), and measure both across all 17 SDGs. Third, we evaluate whether these two dimensions are related, or whether apparent overlap in SDG coverage can coexist with substantial semantic divergence.

Our central research question is therefore: \emph{To what extent do academic AI-for-sustainability research and SDG policy discourse align in coverage and semantic content across the 17 SDGs, and how are these two dimensions related?}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews the relevant literature on AI-SDG mapping, research-policy alignment, and semantic similarity methods. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes our corpora, measurement instrument, and gap analysis procedures. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical findings. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the findings, introduces a productive/problematic misalignment typology, and discusses limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with implications and directions for future research.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{AI and the SDGs: What Bibliometrics Tells Us}

The relationship between AI research and the SDGs has been studied primarily through bibliometric and expert survey methods. \textcite{Vinuesa2020} conducted the most widely cited study, in which an expert panel assessed how AI could enable or inhibit each of the 169 SDG targets; their finding that AI could facilitate 134 targets while potentially inhibiting 59 has shaped discourse around AI governance. However, as the authors acknowledge, the study reflects expert perception rather than empirical measurement of the research literature.

Subsequent empirical work has mapped the AI publication record to the SDGs. \textcite{Singh2023} analysed 20 years of AI, machine learning, and deep learning publications and found consistent concentration on SDGs 3 and 7. \textcite{Cowls2021} examined 108 deployed AI for Social Good projects and found SDG 3 dominant while SDGs 5, 16, and 17 were largely unaddressed. \textcite{Nedungadi2024} applied topic modelling to a broader corpus and confirmed these patterns. The methodological concern raised by \textcite{Armitage2020} is important: different SDG classification methods yield different distributions, and results are not fully comparable across studies. Our embedding-based approach provides a semantically grounded alternative that is less sensitive to vocabulary choices than keyword dictionaries.

Despite convergent findings on which SDGs attract the most research, these bibliometric studies share two limitations relevant to this paper. They measure the presence of SDG-relevant keywords rather than the semantic content of engagement, and they make no comparison to the SDG emphasis of the policy documents that the SDG framework is meant to translate into action.

\subsection{Research-Policy Alignment in AI and Sustainability}

A growing literature documents structural tensions between what AI research produces and what sustainability policy requires. \textcite{Strauss2025} show that corporate AI research concentrates on pre-deployment technical problems while policy emphasises deployment-stage societal impacts, identifying a temporal and stage-based gap. \textcite{SioumalasChristodoulou2025} find that AI research metrics focus on technical performance while policy discourse emphasises ethical and social dimensions aligned with SDG-related concerns. \textcite{Toney2024} provide the most methodologically direct precedent, demonstrating in the FAccT context that apparent high-level topical agreement between research and policy can mask substantial operational divergence --- precisely the phenomenon our within-SDG semantic gap analysis is designed to detect.

These findings are consistent with theoretical accounts of research-policy misalignment. The knowledge utilisation framework \parencite{Rose2017} identifies relevance, credibility, and legitimacy as the three conditions for research uptake; topical overlap is a necessary but insufficient condition for relevance. The epistemic communities framework \parencite{Haas1992} frames policy influence in terms of shared causal beliefs --- our semantic similarity measurement operationalises the ``shared causal beliefs'' dimension, identifying where researcher and policymaker communities do and do not occupy overlapping semantic territory. \textcite{Gibbons1994} attribute structural misalignment to the dominance of Mode 1 (discipline-driven, supply-side) knowledge production: research agendas set by researcher interests and funding incentives will naturally diverge from policy demand, not by accident but by design.

\subsection{Semantic Similarity Methods for Cross-Corpus Comparison}

The use of Sentence-BERT \parencite{Reimers2019} and related transformer-based encoders for cross-corpus semantic comparison has been validated across several domains relevant to this study. \textcite{Bergman2023} demonstrated that BERT-based methods can effectively compare regulatory documents across jurisdictions, providing methodological precedent for cross-institutional semantic comparison. \textcite{Gjorgjevikj2025} benchmarked sentence encoders specifically for SDG association tasks, finding that general-purpose models such as those in the sentence-transformers family provide competitive performance against fine-tuned domain-specific variants for SDG classification.

The OSDG Community Dataset \parencite{Pukelis2022} provides the labelled corpus from which our SDG centroids are derived. The dataset contains over 40,000 texts validated for SDG relevance by a global community of annotators, covering SDGs 1--16 (SDG 17 is absent from the dataset). OSDG labels are not simply machine-generated labels: volunteers are shown a text and a suggested SDG label, then accept or reject that label, with agreement recorded across multiple annotators. This makes OSDG suitable as a broad human-validated reference corpus, while also introducing a limitation: because annotators validate a suggested label rather than freely choosing among all 17 SDGs, the labels partly inherit the framing of the source documents and the original suggested labels. \textcite{Ingram2025} note that high-quality graded relevance sets for SDG classification do not exist, making community-validated datasets such as OSDG the current best available resource. The SDG Classification Benchmark \parencite{SDGBenchmark} provides an expert-labelled complement covering all 17 SDGs; its labels were annotated and verified by human experts, making it appropriate for independent instrument validation.

The nearest-centroid approach --- computing the mean embedding of labelled texts for each SDG and using cosine distance to the nearest centroid as the classification criterion --- is conceptually simple, computationally efficient, and avoids the risk of overfitting that characterises supervised classifiers trained on small labelled sets. This simplicity is a deliberate methodological choice rather than a fallback: the study requires a transparent measurement instrument for corpus-level comparison, not an opaque operational classifier. A fine-tuned classifier might improve individual-document accuracy, but it would also introduce additional training choices, hyperparameters, and overfitting risks that are unnecessary for the present purpose. The centroid represents the ``direction'' in semantic space most characteristic of each SDG, making the resulting score vectors directly interpretable.

\subsection{Theoretical Framing: Productive and Problematic Misalignment}

Not all research-policy misalignment is necessarily harmful. \textcite{Heilinger2023} warn against ``greenwashing'' misalignment where research adopts SDG framing superficially; but the converse risk is also real: demanding that research track policy discourse too closely would suppress basic research and frontier exploration that creates future policy options. We therefore distinguish between \emph{productive misalignment} --- where research addresses SDG-relevant dimensions that policy has not yet engaged --- and \emph{problematic misalignment} --- where research is absent from or superficially engaged with dimensions that policy identifies as urgent.

This distinction, introduced theoretically in \textcite{Toney2024} and operationalised here at SDG level, provides the interpretive framework for our Discussion. It shifts the normative question from ``how do we eliminate misalignment?'' to ``which forms of misalignment are beneficial, and which require redress?''

\subsection{Research Gap}

The literature reviewed above has not, to our knowledge, produced: (i) a systematic within-SDG semantic gap analysis comparing what research and policy say \emph{about the same SDG}; (ii) a bidirectional alignment analysis measuring both whether research engages policy framing and whether policy engages research framing; or (iii) an examination of whether coverage gaps and semantic gaps are correlated, independent, or inversely related. These are the contributions of the present study.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Conceptual Framing: Topical Overlap as a Measurable Proxy}

This study measures \emph{topical overlap} between research and policy --- whether the two corpora discuss the same subject areas, as proxied by semantic similarity in embedding space. We explicitly do not claim to measure substantive alignment (whether research and policy propose compatible solutions to the same problems), causal influence (whether research shapes policy or vice versa), or operational alignment (whether the same methods and interventions are implied). Topical overlap is the necessary but not sufficient condition for research-policy dialogue: shared vocabulary does not guarantee shared meaning or direction, but its absence makes dialogue structurally impossible.

This framing positions our contribution at Level 2 of a three-level hierarchy: Level 1 measures lexical co-occurrence (keyword dictionaries; most prior work), Level 2 measures topical similarity (embedding-based; this study), and Level 3 measures operational alignment (structured action comparison; approximated by \textcite{Toney2024}). Advancing to Level 2 allows us to detect semantic divergence that keyword methods miss, while Level 3 remains beyond the scope of unsupervised text analysis on this corpus scale.

A further conceptual note is necessary on the linguistic register difference between corpora. Policy documents use performative modal language (``must'', ``should'', ``will'') and articulate goals at high abstraction. Research papers use hedged indicative language (``we demonstrate'', ``results suggest'') and report empirical findings at high specificity. High cosine similarity between a research abstract and a policy chunk therefore indicates that they discuss the same \emph{subject}, not that they are ``saying the same thing.'' All findings in this paper are framed accordingly.

\subsection{Research Corpus}

The research corpus consists of \NResearchPapers{} English-language research abstracts retrieved from the OpenAlex API \parencite{Priem2022}, covering publications between 2018 and 2025. Abstracts were retrieved using a structured query combining OpenAlex's native SDG work filter with four AI-related search terms (``artificial intelligence'', ``machine learning'', ``deep learning'', ``neural network''), yielding 68 queries: 17 SDGs times four AI terms.\footnote{Complete query parameters are documented in \path{code/0_fetch/fetch_openalex.py}. Each query filters to one OpenAlex native SDG identifier, publication year after 2017, and records with abstracts, then applies the AI search term.} This makes OpenAlex the retrieval mechanism rather than the final labelling authority. OpenAlex SDG metadata defines the candidate universe of AI-for-sustainability papers; the SDG assignments used in the analysis are produced later by the independently validated centroid instrument described below. After deduplication by OpenAlex work ID and removal of records lacking usable abstracts, \NResearchPapers{} unique papers were retained in the canonical corpus. No balancing by SDG, citation count, or OpenAlex relevance rank is applied, because such trimming would distort the coverage profile the study aims to measure. Each paper's title and abstract were concatenated to form the text unit for embedding.

The 2018 start date reflects the post-AlexNet emergence of deep learning as the dominant AI paradigm and the adoption of the SDG framework in 2015 with a research uptake lag. Earlier papers are excluded to ensure comparability in methodological framing.

\subsection{Policy Corpus}

The policy corpus contains \NPolicyChunks{} text chunks drawn from three source collections, representing \NPolicyDocs{} unique source documents:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Curated AI and SDG policy documents} (8,592 chunks from 31 documents): Including the UN 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development \parencite{UN2015}, the Paris Agreement \parencite{Paris2015}, seven UN SDG Progress Reports (2017--2024), IPCC Sixth Assessment Report Working Group II and III Summaries for Policymakers \parencite{IPCC2022}, the OECD AI Principles \parencite{OECD2019}, the EU AI Act \parencite{EUAI2024}, the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI \parencite{UNESCO2021}, the UK National AI Strategy \parencite{UKAI2021}, the UN AI Advisory Body Report \parencite{UNAI2024}, the African Union Continental AI Strategy \parencite{AUAI2024}, SDSN Sustainable Development Reports 2024 and 2025 \parencite{Sachs2024,Sachs2025}, and additional national AI strategies and international frameworks.

  \item \textbf{SDGi Voluntary National Review and Voluntary Local Review corpus} \parencite{SDGi2024} (31,941 chunks from 2,279 documents): National government reports submitted to the UN High-Level Political Forum, covering 40+ countries. These represent state-level sustainability policy discourse.

  \item \textbf{UN General Debate Corpus} \parencite{UNGDC2024} (6,472 chunks from 82 documents): SDG-relevant passages filtered from UN General Debate speeches, sessions 70--80 (2015--2024), extracted by keyword filtering on SDG-related terms.
\end{enumerate}

Each source document was chunked into segments of approximately 150--300 words using sentence-boundary-aware dynamic chunking. Short fragments (fewer than 20 words) were discarded. Chunks were deduplicated by exact text match after normalisation, yielding the final \NPolicyChunks{} unique chunks. Chunk identifiers encode source document identity, enabling document-level weighting in subsequent analyses.

\textbf{Source heterogeneity note.} The three source collections represent structurally different document types. The curated AI/SDG documents are AI governance and sustainability strategy texts; VNR/VLR reports are national government self-assessments of SDG progress structured around the 17 goals; UNGDC speeches are annual head-of-state addresses that follow UN discourse conventions. SDG profiles in Section~\ref{sec:results} should be read with this in mind: the policy corpus SDG distribution (which concentrates on SDGs 13, 16, and 17) likely reflects VNR report institutional structure as much as it reflects the priorities of AI governance specifically. Where an AI-governance-specific interpretation is needed, the curated AI/SDG sub-corpus (31 documents) is the more relevant reference.

\subsection{Embedding Model and Normalisation}

All texts were encoded using the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} sentence-transformers model \parencite{Reimers2019}, producing 384-dimensional dense vector representations. This model was selected for three reasons: it achieves competitive performance on semantic textual similarity benchmarks, it is computationally efficient on CPU hardware (relevant for WSL deployment), and it avoids the need for domain-specific fine-tuning that would introduce additional analytical assumptions. All embeddings were L2-normalised to unit vectors prior to storage, such that cosine similarity between any two vectors equals their dot product. This normalisation is applied consistently across all corpora and is required for the centroid computation to yield cosine similarities.

\subsection{SDG Measurement Instrument: Centroids and Validation}

The core measurement instrument is a set of 17 per-SDG centroid vectors in the embedding space. For SDGs 1--16, centroids are computed as the mean embedding of all texts in the OSDG Community Dataset labelled for that SDG (after filtering at annotator agreement $\geq 0.5$ and minimum 20 words), drawn from a pool of 30,534 texts. For SDG 17, OSDG contains no labelled texts; the centroid is derived from 31 expert-labelled texts in the SDG Classification Benchmark \parencite{SDGBenchmark}. Each raw centroid vector (the mean of unit vectors) is L2-normalised to a unit vector before use, so that downstream dot products equal cosine similarities. The raw centroid norm (prior to normalisation) is retained as a cohesion diagnostic: it equals the mean cosine similarity of member texts to the unit centroid.

This is not a supervised machine-learning classifier in the conventional sense. No model weights are fitted, no gradient descent is used, and no train/test split is required for parameter estimation. The only learned representation is the pre-trained sentence encoder; the SDG instrument itself is a transparent geometric summary of human-labelled examples. Its purpose is not industry-grade operational labelling, but a reproducible semantic proxy for comparing broad corpus-level patterns.

\textbf{Validation.} The instrument is validated using the full SDG Classification Benchmark (616 texts, all 17 SDGs) as an independent evaluation set. For each benchmark text, the predicted SDG is the centroid with the highest cosine similarity. The primary evaluation metric is macro-F1 computed over SDGs 1--16 (n = 585), excluding SDG 17 because its centroid was built from a subset of the same benchmark texts (contamination). The instrument achieves macro-F1 = \MacroFOne{} on the uncontaminated evaluation, comfortably above the pre-registered pass threshold of 0.50 and far above the random 17-class baseline of approximately \RandomBaselineSeventeenClass. This level of performance would not justify deployment as a high-stakes operational SDG labeller, but it is sufficiently discriminative for the study's purpose: exploratory corpus-level comparison of broad SDG coverage and semantic framing. Per-SDG F1 scores vary: SDG 4 (Quality Education) achieves the highest F1 (\FiSdgFour) and SDG 16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions) the second highest (\FiSdgSixteen), reflecting their lexically distinctive vocabularies. Notably, the very high SDG 4 F1 means the instrument is reliably assigning texts to that SDG --- which strengthens rather than weakens the SDG 4 artefact concern (Section~\ref{sec:sdg4artefact}): ML papers are being confidently and consistently placed near the Education centroid. SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities, F1 = \FiSdgEleven) and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth, F1 = \FiSdgEight) are the lowest-performing, reflecting semantic overlap with SDG 9 and the SDG 1/8/10 cluster respectively. SDG 15 (Life on Land, F1 = \FiSdgFifteen) is also below the mean and warrants corresponding caution. Findings for these SDGs are reported with appropriate caveats.

The validation task should also be interpreted correctly. It tests whether positive benchmark examples are assigned to the nearest appropriate SDG; it does not evaluate a rejection option for texts unrelated to any SDG. This is acceptable for the present design because the research corpus is first restricted to an OpenAlex AI-and-SDG retrieval universe. The centroid instrument then answers a relative question: among the 17 SDGs, which one is this eligible text closest to?

\subsection{Coverage Gap Analysis}

For each item in the research and policy corpora, the predicted SDG is the centroid with the highest cosine similarity score (hard assignment). The coverage profile for a corpus is the proportion of items assigned to each SDG.

A critical methodological issue arises for the policy corpus: the three source collections differ dramatically in size (SDGi: 31,941 chunks; curated docs: 8,592 chunks; UNGDC: 6,472 chunks) and the SDGi corpus includes 2,279 individual national reports. A simple chunk-weighted profile would be dominated by the volume of a small number of large documents rather than reflecting the range of policy voices. We therefore compute a \emph{document-weighted} policy coverage profile, in which each of the 2,392 unique source documents contributes equally regardless of chunk count. Each document's SDG assignment is the argmax of its mean chunk score vector. Document-weighted profiles are used as the canonical representation throughout; chunk-weighted profiles are retained as a diagnostic comparison.

The coverage gap for SDG $j$ is defined as:
\[
  \text{CoverageGap}_j = \left|\text{ResearchProportion}_j - \text{PolicyProportion}^{\text{docwt}}_j\right|
\]

\subsection{Semantic Gap Analysis}

The semantic gap measures within-SDG semantic divergence: given that both corpora have texts assigned to SDG $j$, how differently do they discuss it? For each SDG $j$:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Collect all research papers assigned to SDG $j$ (the \emph{research cluster} for $j$).
  \item Collect all policy chunks assigned to SDG $j$, subject to a per-document chunk cap of 50 chunks per source document (the \emph{policy cluster} for $j$, capped). The cap prevents SDSN (up to 3,179 chunks in a single document) from dominating the policy sub-centroid.
  \item Compute the L2-normalised mean embedding of each cluster: $\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j$ and $\mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j$.
  \item Define: $\text{SemanticSimilarity}_j = \mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j \cdot \mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j$ (dot product of unit vectors = cosine similarity).
  \item Define: $\text{SemanticGap}_j = 1 - \text{SemanticSimilarity}_j$.
\end{enumerate}

A semantic gap of 0 would indicate perfectly overlapping semantic directions; a gap approaching 1 would indicate orthogonal framing. Robustness is assessed by replicating the analysis with chunk caps of 20 and 100 per document; rankings were stable across all three caps.

\subsection{Coverage--Semantic Interaction and Bidirectional Asymmetry}

The central structural hypothesis (H25) is that coverage gaps and semantic gaps are correlated across SDGs. We compute Pearson $r$ and Spearman $\rho$ between research coverage proportions and semantic gap scores across all 17 SDGs, and report sensitivity analyses excluding SDG 4 (see Section~\ref{sec:sdg4artefact}).

The directional asymmetry hypothesis (H26) tests whether research engages policy framing less than policy engages research framing. To operationalise this, we build research-side SDG centroids by hard-assigning each paper to its top OSDG centroid and computing the mean embedding of papers assigned to each SDG. Policy chunks are then scored against these research centroids. The mean top-centroid similarity for research papers against OSDG centroids (\HAsymResearchScore) is compared to the mean top-centroid similarity for policy chunks against research centroids (\HAsymPolicyScore).

\subsection{Key Assumptions and Mitigations}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\textbf{A15 --- OSDG circularity.} OSDG is human-validated, but many source texts and suggested labels come from UN-adjacent SDG document collections. Since our policy corpus also contains UN and institutional SDG documents, OSDG-derived centroids may be calibrated to policy vocabulary, systematically inflating policy cosine similarity scores relative to research scores. We test for this post hoc by comparing mean top-centroid scores: policy chunks score \AffFifteenDiff{} higher than research papers (\AffFifteenPolicyScore{} vs \AffFifteenPaperScore), exceeding our pre-registered flag threshold of 0.10. This difference is acknowledged throughout the Results and Discussion as a potential calibration bias. Coverage gap and semantic gap findings remain valid as relative comparisons, but absolute score comparisons between corpora should be interpreted cautiously.

\textbf{A19 --- Unit-of-analysis asymmetry.} Research papers are single, focused abstract-length texts (mean $\sim$200 words); policy corpus items are 150-word chunks from documents of highly variable length. Document-weighting (Section 3.6) and per-document chunk caps (Section 3.7) are the primary mitigations.

\textbf{SDG 4 artefact.}
\label{sec:sdg4artefact}
The research coverage profile assigns \ResearchPctSdgFour\% of papers to SDG 4 (Quality Education). This is unexpectedly high and likely reflects a measurement artefact: machine learning papers frequently use ``learning,'' ``training,'' and ``model'' as core vocabulary, which overlaps with the OSDG education corpus. SDG 4 centroid findings are reported throughout with this caveat; sensitivity analyses excluding SDG 4 are provided for all correlation results.

\textbf{A26 --- SDG 1/8/10 cluster.} SDG centroids for SDGs 1 (No Poverty), 8 (Decent Work), and 10 (Reduced Inequalities) show very high pairwise cosine similarities (\CentroidSimOneEightTenMin--\CentroidSimOneEightTenMax), producing systematic classifier leakage. Individual findings for these three SDGs are reported as a macro-cluster in the discussion.

\subsection{Directional Asymmetry: H26}

The bidirectional asymmetry analysis finds that policy chunks score \HAsymPolicyScore{} against research-derived SDG centroids, while research papers score \HAsymResearchScore{} against OSDG-derived SDG centroids, a gap of \HAsymGap. This direction is consistent with H26 (that research ignores policy framing more than policy ignores research framing).

However, H26 is \textbf{inconclusive} with the current instrument. The A15 calibration diagnostic found that policy chunks score \AffFifteenDiff{} higher than research papers against OSDG centroids --- this baseline asymmetry is \emph{larger} than the measured H26 gap of \HAsymGap. The entire observed asymmetry therefore falls within the range that the calibration bias alone could produce, and the result cannot be distinguished from instrument artefact. Reporting H26 as ``supported'' in this context would overstate the evidence. Replication with a domain-neutral embedding model (e.g., a model fine-tuned independently of UN documents) is required before any directional claim can be made with confidence.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Two Independent Dimensions of Misalignment}

The headline result of this study is simple: alignment cannot be inferred from topical overlap. The rebuilt canonical analysis shows that research attention alone is a poor predictor of semantic convergence. SDGs that attract substantial research attention can still be either relatively close to policy framing (for example, SDG 9) or substantially divergent from it (for example, SDGs 3 and 7). At the same time, the strongest policy-dominant SDGs, especially SDGs 13 and 17, are also among the most semantically distant. The sample-stability ladder strengthens this reading: once the research subsamples reach roughly 200{,}000 papers, the headline downstream metrics already sit very close to the full-corpus values. The practical implication is that research-policy mismatch is not a single scalar problem. Attention allocation and framing divergence must be measured separately.

This interpretation is consistent with the epistemic communities framework \parencite{Haas1992}: research and policy communities can share concern for the same SDG while operating with different timescales, vocabularies, and decision contexts. The Mode 1/2 distinction \parencite{Gibbons1994} pushes the argument further. Supply-driven research agendas reward tractable technical problems, while policy discourse rewards implementable coordination, institutional legitimacy, and collective action. The policy-dominant concentration on SDGs 13, 17, and 16 therefore looks less like random imbalance and more like a structural difference in what each knowledge system is organised to do.

\subsection{Where Misalignment Concentrates}

Two concentration patterns matter. First, the policy corpus is overwhelmingly dominated by the climate--partnerships--governance block, with SDGs 13, 17, and 16 alone accounting for \PolicyTopThreePct\% of source documents. Second, the largest semantic gaps are also concentrated in or near that policy-facing region, especially SDGs 17 and 13. This suggests that the most institutionally prominent policy goals are precisely where research and policy are least likely to be speaking in the same register.

By contrast, the strongest apparent alignment case is SDG 9. It combines high research coverage (\ResearchPctSdgNine\%) with one of the lowest semantic gaps (\SemanticGapSdgNine). That does not prove substantive alignment, but it does show that the embedding-based instrument can separate a comparatively close case from more visibly divergent ones. The contrast between SDG 9 and high-gap cases such as SDG 3 or SDG 13 is analytically useful: it demonstrates that semantic divergence is not simply a mechanical consequence of all high-salience SDGs.

\subsection{Implications}

Three implications follow from the combined findings, and each pushes against the common habit of treating topical mention as evidence of alignment.

\textbf{Funding allocation.} The results point to two priorities: low-coverage high-gap goals such as SDG 10, and policy-dominant high-gap goals such as SDGs 13 and 17. Redirecting even modest research funding toward these areas, with explicit attention to policy framing rather than topic mention alone, would address both dimensions of misalignment simultaneously.

\textbf{SDG-aware research communication.} The finding that research attention alone does not predict semantic alignment suggests that simply increasing research output on neglected SDGs is insufficient; research communication norms need to change as well. Publication incentives that reward explicit SDG engagement (as evidenced by better alignment among explicitly SDG-framed publications; cf.\ \textcite{Nedungadi2024}) may be more effective than topic-level funding mandates.

\textbf{Bidirectional framing for AI governance.} H26 is inconclusive under the current instrument (the measured asymmetry of \HAsymGap{} is smaller than the A15 calibration bias of \AffFifteenDiff). However, the \emph{question} H26 poses --- whether AI governance policy has been more responsive to research than research has been to governance priorities --- remains substantively important and empirically tractable with a better-calibrated encoder. Future work replicating this analysis with a domain-neutral model should treat this as a primary hypothesis.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound the conclusions.

\textbf{Topical overlap is not substantive alignment.} The study measures semantic co-location in embedding space, not whether research and policy are substantively addressing the same problems in compatible ways. High cosine similarity reflects shared vocabulary, not shared agenda. All findings must be read with this constraint.

\textbf{A15 calibration bias.} The OSDG circularity (A15) means policy scores against OSDG centroids are likely inflated. Coverage proportions are less affected than absolute scores, but the finding warrants replication with a calibration-neutral instrument.

\textbf{SDG 4 artefact.} The \ResearchPctSdgFour\% research assignment to SDG 4 likely overstates genuine education research. All findings involving the research-side SDG 4 profile should be treated with corresponding uncertainty.

\textbf{Corpus scope.} The policy corpus, while broad, represents international institutional discourse (UN, OECD, national governments). Domestic implementation plans, sub-national policies, and non-English-language policy documents are not represented. The research corpus is also English-language. Both biases likely understate the perspectives of the Global South.

\textbf{Cross-sectional design.} The study provides a snapshot of the 2018--2025 research period against a policy corpus assembled over a similar timeframe. It cannot address whether alignment is improving or deteriorating, or whether specific policy events (e.g., the EU AI Act 2024, IPCC AR6 2022) have shifted research framing.

\textbf{Single embedding model.} Using a single general-purpose encoder means the findings are model-specific. A domain-adapted model (e.g., fine-tuned on sustainability text) might produce different cluster structures and coverage profiles.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper revisits an old problem with a new instrument. Policy scholars have long argued that research and policy often fail to meet one another on equal terms. What recent semantic tools add is not the discovery that mismatch exists, but the ability to measure one important dimension of it directly, across thousands of texts, instead of describing it impressionistically. The main result is clear: alignment cannot be inferred from topical overlap.

Current AI-for-sustainability research is structurally misaligned with SDG policy discourse along two related but non-identical dimensions. The first is coverage: policy discourse is overwhelmingly oriented toward climate governance (SDG 13, \PolicyPctSdgThirteen\%), international partnerships (SDG 17, \PolicyPctSdgSeventeen\%), and institutional frameworks (SDG 16, \PolicyPctSdgSixteen\%), while research is more concentrated on health (SDG 3, \ResearchPctSdgThree\%), learning-related material (SDG 4, \ResearchPctSdgFour\%), and innovation (SDG 9, \ResearchPctSdgNine\%). The second is semantic divergence: even when research and policy occupy the same SDG, they often do not frame that SDG in commensurate terms. The largest semantic gaps occur in SDGs 17, 13, and 10, showing that some of the most policy-salient goals are also those where shared SDG labels conceal the most divergent discourse.

The central structural finding is therefore more precise than a simple ``independence'' claim. Research coverage proportion itself shows no significant association with semantic gap ($r = \HPrimaryPearsonR$, $p = \HPrimaryPearsonP$; $n = \HPrimaryN$), so sheer research attention is not a reliable indicator of alignment. But SDGs with larger cross-corpus imbalance do tend to show larger semantic gaps. That matters because it challenges a common inference in AI-for-SDG mapping: research cannot be treated as aligned with policy simply because it appears to cover the same goals. Coverage is necessary, but it is not enough.

The resulting diagnostic is still useful beyond the immediate case. It separates goals where research is present but framed differently from goals where the two corpora are both unevenly distributed and semantically far apart. SDG 9 remains the clearest apparent alignment case in the current corpus, while SDGs 13 and 17 exemplify policy-dominant high-divergence misalignment. That contrast is exactly why topical overlap alone is too weak a test.

Several findings call for direct follow-up. The SDG 4 artefact should be resolved by qualitative inspection or a fine-tuned classifier. The A15 calibration bias should be addressed by replicating with a domain-neutral encoder. The temporal dimension --- whether the gaps are narrowing as the SDG framework matures --- can be addressed with the year metadata available in the OpenAlex corpus. Most importantly, the 2$\times$2 typology sketched here requires qualitative operationalisation of the productive/problematic distinction \parencite{Toney2024}, which this study has measured but not fully interpreted. Extending the analysis to the 169 individual SDG targets (using the AURORA dataset), and disaggregating the policy corpus by country income level (H34), would provide finer-grained evidence about where the misalignment problem is most acute and for whom.

The headline message for research governance is therefore straightforward. Knowing which SDGs research covers is necessary but not sufficient. Measuring whether research and policy mean compatible things when they invoke those SDGs is a distinct diagnostic, and an equally important one. If topical overlap is mistaken for alignment, misalignment will be systematically underestimated.
