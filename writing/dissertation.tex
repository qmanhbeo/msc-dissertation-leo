\begin{abstract}
The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) provide the dominant international framework for sustainability governance, yet it remains unclear whether academic artificial intelligence (AI) research engages with policy priorities at the SDG level. Existing bibliometric studies map AI research to SDGs using keyword methods, but none has systematically compared the \emph{semantic content} of research and policy discourse or tested whether the two forms of topical gap are related. This study advances from lexical to topical alignment measurement using Sentence-BERT embeddings and a nearest-centroid classifier validated against expert-labelled SDG benchmarks (macro-F1 = 0.733). Applying this instrument to a preliminary working snapshot of 6,172 research abstracts and 47,005 policy document chunks from 2,392 sources, we find: (i) a pronounced coverage gap in which policy discourse concentrates on SDG 13 (Climate, 36\% of documents), SDG 17 (Partnerships, 35\%), and SDG 16 (Governance, 20\%), while research concentrates on SDG 4 (Education/Learning, 22\%) and SDG 9 (Innovation, 17\%); (ii) a semantic gap in which SDG 8 (Decent Work), SDG 3 (Health), and SDG 16 (Governance) show the largest within-SDG semantic divergence between the two corpora; (iii) critically, coverage gaps and semantic gaps are \emph{statistically independent} ($r = 0.145$, $p = 0.578$), indicating that they represent two structurally distinct dimensions of misalignment requiring separate interventions; and (iv) evidence of directional asymmetry in which policy engages research framing more than research engages policy framing. These findings challenge the assumption that topical engagement implies substantive alignment and suggest that research-policy dialogue requires attention to semantic register, not only subject coverage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Artificial intelligence has become a prominent feature of sustainability discourse. Governments, international organisations, and research funders increasingly position AI as a tool for accelerating progress toward the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), a framework of 17 goals and 169 targets adopted in 2015 to guide global development through 2030 \parencite{UN2015}. But one assumption often goes untested: if research and policy appear to address the same SDGs, they are treated as aligned. That assumption is too weak. Shared topical labels do not guarantee shared meaning, and shared vocabulary does not guarantee that research is engaging the problems policy treats as urgent.

The underlying problem is not new. Research-policy mismatch has been a central concern in policy studies and knowledge-utilisation scholarship for decades. What is new is methodological. Embedding-based semantic tools now make it possible to measure one dimension of that mismatch directly, at corpus scale, rather than infer it from anecdote or keyword frequency. In that sense, the idea of this paper is deliberately basic: it applies a new measurement technology to an old problem, namely whether research and policy are actually speaking to one another.

A substantial body of bibliometric work documents \emph{what} AI research engages with: studies consistently find concentration on technically tractable SDGs such as SDG 3 (Good Health), SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), and SDG 13 (Climate Action), with relative neglect of SDGs 5, 10, 16, and 17 \parencite{Vinuesa2020, Singh2023, Cowls2021}. However, these studies implicitly treat topical mention as a proxy for alignment. They can tell us whether research points at an SDG, but not whether it addresses the same substantive terrain as policy discourse. None has asked whether nominal overlap conceals semantic divergence, nor whether distributional and semantic forms of mismatch move together or come apart.

This study addresses that gap. Its central claim is straightforward: alignment cannot be inferred from topical overlap. We make three contributions. First, we advance research-policy alignment measurement from Level 1 (lexical: same keywords) to Level 2 (topical: same subject matter in semantic space), using Sentence-BERT \parencite{Reimers2019} embeddings and SDG centroids derived from the OSDG Community Dataset \parencite{Pukelis2022}. Second, we provide the first systematic comparison of both a coverage gap (which SDGs each corpus emphasises) and a semantic gap (how differently the two corpora discuss the same SDG) across all 17 SDGs simultaneously. Third, we test the structural relationship between these two gap types --- a question with direct implications for how research-policy misalignment should be diagnosed and addressed.

Our central research question is: \emph{To what extent does academic AI-for-sustainability research show topical overlap with SDG policy priorities, and where do the gaps lie?}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews the relevant literature on AI-SDG mapping, research-policy alignment, and semantic similarity methods. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes our corpora, measurement instrument, and gap analysis procedures. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical findings. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the findings, introduces a productive/problematic misalignment typology, and discusses limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with implications and directions for future research.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{AI and the SDGs: What Bibliometrics Tells Us}

The relationship between AI research and the SDGs has been studied primarily through bibliometric and expert survey methods. \textcite{Vinuesa2020} conducted the most widely cited study, in which an expert panel assessed how AI could enable or inhibit each of the 169 SDG targets; their finding that AI could facilitate 134 targets while potentially inhibiting 59 has shaped discourse around AI governance. However, as the authors acknowledge, the study reflects expert perception rather than empirical measurement of the research literature.

Subsequent empirical work has mapped the AI publication record to the SDGs. \textcite{Singh2023} analysed 20 years of AI, machine learning, and deep learning publications and found consistent concentration on SDGs 3 and 7. \textcite{Cowls2021} examined 108 deployed AI for Social Good projects and found SDG 3 dominant while SDGs 5, 16, and 17 were largely unaddressed. \textcite{Nedungadi2024} applied topic modelling to a broader corpus and confirmed these patterns. The methodological concern raised by \textcite{Armitage2020} is important: different SDG classification methods yield different distributions, and results are not fully comparable across studies. Our embedding-based approach provides a semantically grounded alternative that is less sensitive to vocabulary choices than keyword dictionaries.

Despite convergent findings on which SDGs attract the most research, these bibliometric studies share two limitations relevant to this paper. They measure the presence of SDG-relevant keywords rather than the semantic content of engagement, and they make no comparison to the SDG emphasis of the policy documents that the SDG framework is meant to translate into action.

\subsection{Research-Policy Alignment in AI and Sustainability}

A growing literature documents structural tensions between what AI research produces and what sustainability policy requires. \textcite{Strauss2025} show that corporate AI research concentrates on pre-deployment technical problems while policy emphasises deployment-stage societal impacts, identifying a temporal and stage-based gap. \textcite{SioumalasChristodoulou2025} find that AI research metrics focus on technical performance while policy discourse emphasises ethical and social dimensions aligned with SDG-related concerns. \textcite{Toney2024} provide the most methodologically direct precedent, demonstrating in the FAccT context that apparent high-level topical agreement between research and policy can mask substantial operational divergence --- precisely the phenomenon our within-SDG semantic gap analysis is designed to detect.

These findings are consistent with theoretical accounts of research-policy misalignment. The knowledge utilisation framework \parencite{Rose2017} identifies relevance, credibility, and legitimacy as the three conditions for research uptake; topical overlap is a necessary but insufficient condition for relevance. The epistemic communities framework \parencite{Haas1992} frames policy influence in terms of shared causal beliefs --- our semantic similarity measurement operationalises the ``shared causal beliefs'' dimension, identifying where researcher and policymaker communities do and do not occupy overlapping semantic territory. \textcite{Gibbons1994} attribute structural misalignment to the dominance of Mode 1 (discipline-driven, supply-side) knowledge production: research agendas set by researcher interests and funding incentives will naturally diverge from policy demand, not by accident but by design.

\subsection{Semantic Similarity Methods for Cross-Corpus Comparison}

The use of Sentence-BERT \parencite{Reimers2019} and related transformer-based encoders for cross-corpus semantic comparison has been validated across several domains relevant to this study. \textcite{Bergman2023} demonstrated that BERT-based methods can effectively compare regulatory documents across jurisdictions, providing methodological precedent for cross-institutional semantic comparison. \textcite{Gjorgjevikj2025} benchmarked sentence encoders specifically for SDG association tasks, finding that general-purpose models such as those in the sentence-transformers family provide competitive performance against fine-tuned domain-specific variants for SDG classification.

The OSDG Community Dataset \parencite{Pukelis2022} provides the labelled corpus from which our SDG centroids are derived. The dataset contains over 40,000 texts crowd-labelled for SDG relevance by a global community of annotators, covering SDGs 1--16 (SDG 17 is absent from the dataset). \textcite{Ingram2025} note that high-quality graded relevance sets for SDG classification do not exist, making community-validated datasets such as OSDG the current best available resource. The SDG Classification Benchmark \parencite{SDGBenchmark} provides an expert-labelled complement covering all 17 SDGs and is used here for independent instrument validation.

The nearest-centroid approach --- computing the mean embedding of labelled texts for each SDG and using cosine distance to the nearest centroid as the classification criterion --- is conceptually simple, computationally efficient, and avoids the risk of overfitting that characterises supervised classifiers trained on small labelled sets. It has been used in related work on document classification and is directly interpretable: the centroid represents the ``direction'' in semantic space most characteristic of each SDG.

\subsection{Theoretical Framing: Productive and Problematic Misalignment}

Not all research-policy misalignment is necessarily harmful. \textcite{Heilinger2023} warn against ``greenwashing'' misalignment where research adopts SDG framing superficially; but the converse risk is also real: demanding that research track policy discourse too closely would suppress basic research and frontier exploration that creates future policy options. We therefore distinguish between \emph{productive misalignment} --- where research addresses SDG-relevant dimensions that policy has not yet engaged --- and \emph{problematic misalignment} --- where research is absent from or superficially engaged with dimensions that policy identifies as urgent.

This distinction, introduced theoretically in \textcite{Toney2024} and operationalised here at SDG level, provides the interpretive framework for our Discussion. It shifts the normative question from ``how do we eliminate misalignment?'' to ``which forms of misalignment are beneficial, and which require redress?''

\subsection{Research Gap}

The literature reviewed above has not, to our knowledge, produced: (i) a systematic within-SDG semantic gap analysis comparing what research and policy say \emph{about the same SDG}; (ii) a bidirectional alignment analysis measuring both whether research engages policy framing and whether policy engages research framing; or (iii) an examination of whether coverage gaps and semantic gaps are correlated, independent, or inversely related. These are the contributions of the present study.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Conceptual Framing: Topical Overlap as a Measurable Proxy}

This study measures \emph{topical overlap} between research and policy --- whether the two corpora discuss the same subject areas, as proxied by semantic similarity in embedding space. We explicitly do not claim to measure substantive alignment (whether research and policy propose compatible solutions to the same problems), causal influence (whether research shapes policy or vice versa), or operational alignment (whether the same methods and interventions are implied). Topical overlap is the necessary but not sufficient condition for research-policy dialogue: shared vocabulary does not guarantee shared meaning or direction, but its absence makes dialogue structurally impossible.

This framing positions our contribution at Level 2 of a three-level hierarchy: Level 1 measures lexical co-occurrence (keyword dictionaries; most prior work), Level 2 measures topical similarity (embedding-based; this study), and Level 3 measures operational alignment (structured action comparison; approximated by \textcite{Toney2024}). Advancing to Level 2 allows us to detect semantic divergence that keyword methods miss, while Level 3 remains beyond the scope of unsupervised text analysis on this corpus scale.

A further conceptual note is necessary on the linguistic register difference between corpora. Policy documents use performative modal language (``must'', ``should'', ``will'') and articulate goals at high abstraction. Research papers use hedged indicative language (``we demonstrate'', ``results suggest'') and report empirical findings at high specificity. High cosine similarity between a research abstract and a policy chunk therefore indicates that they discuss the same \emph{subject}, not that they are ``saying the same thing.'' All findings in this paper are framed accordingly.

\subsection{Research Corpus}

The research corpus reported in this draft is an interim working snapshot rather than a final locked harvest. As of \AnalysisSnapshotDate, the OpenAlex collection process was still underway and only approximately half of the planned query set had completed. The counts and downstream results reported here should therefore be read as provisional.

The research corpus consists of 6,172 English-language research abstracts retrieved from the OpenAlex API \parencite{Priem2022}, covering publications between 2018 and 2025. Abstracts were retrieved using a structured query combining four AI-related terms (``artificial intelligence'', ``machine learning'', ``deep learning'', ``neural network'') with each of the 17 SDGs as topic keywords, yielding 68 queries. Abstracts were required to include both an AI term and an SDG-relevant term. After deduplication by OpenAlex work ID and removal of records lacking abstracts, 6,172 unique papers were retained in the current snapshot. The corpus spans applications across all SDG areas and includes conference papers, journal articles, and preprints. Each paper's title and abstract were concatenated to form the text unit for embedding.

The 2018 start date reflects the post-AlexNet emergence of deep learning as the dominant AI paradigm and the adoption of the SDG framework in 2015 with a research uptake lag. Earlier papers are excluded to ensure comparability in methodological framing.

\subsection{Policy Corpus}

The policy corpus contains 47,005 text chunks drawn from three source collections, representing 2,392 unique source documents:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Curated AI and SDG policy documents} (8,592 chunks from 31 documents): Including the UN 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development \parencite{UN2015}, the Paris Agreement \parencite{Paris2015}, seven UN SDG Progress Reports (2017--2024), IPCC Sixth Assessment Report Working Group II and III Summaries for Policymakers \parencite{IPCC2022}, the OECD AI Principles \parencite{OECD2019}, the EU AI Act \parencite{EUAI2024}, the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI \parencite{UNESCO2021}, the UK National AI Strategy \parencite{UKAI2021}, the UN AI Advisory Body Report \parencite{UNAI2024}, the African Union Continental AI Strategy \parencite{AUAI2024}, SDSN Sustainable Development Reports 2024 and 2025 \parencite{Sachs2024,Sachs2025}, and additional national AI strategies and international frameworks.

  \item \textbf{SDGi Voluntary National Review and Voluntary Local Review corpus} \parencite{SDGi2024} (31,941 chunks from 2,279 documents): National government reports submitted to the UN High-Level Political Forum, covering 40+ countries. These represent state-level sustainability policy discourse.

  \item \textbf{UN General Debate Corpus} \parencite{UNGDC2024} (6,472 chunks from 82 documents): SDG-relevant passages filtered from UN General Debate speeches, sessions 70--80 (2015--2024), extracted by keyword filtering on SDG-related terms.
\end{enumerate}

Each source document was chunked into segments of approximately 150--300 words using sentence-boundary-aware dynamic chunking. Short fragments (fewer than 20 words) were discarded. Chunks were deduplicated by exact text match after normalisation, yielding the final 47,005 unique chunks. Chunk identifiers encode source document identity, enabling document-level weighting in subsequent analyses.

\subsection{Embedding Model and Normalisation}

All texts were encoded using the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} sentence-transformers model \parencite{Reimers2019}, producing 384-dimensional dense vector representations. This model was selected for three reasons: it achieves competitive performance on semantic textual similarity benchmarks, it is computationally efficient on CPU hardware (relevant for WSL deployment), and it avoids the need for domain-specific fine-tuning that would introduce additional analytical assumptions. All embeddings were L2-normalised to unit vectors prior to storage, such that cosine similarity between any two vectors equals their dot product. This normalisation is applied consistently across all corpora and is required for the centroid computation to yield cosine similarities.

\subsection{SDG Measurement Instrument: Centroids and Validation}

The core measurement instrument is a set of 17 per-SDG centroid vectors in the embedding space. For SDGs 1--16, centroids are computed as the mean embedding of all texts in the OSDG Community Dataset labelled for that SDG (after filtering at annotator agreement $\geq 0.5$ and minimum 20 words), drawn from a pool of 30,534 texts. For SDG 17, OSDG contains no labelled texts; the centroid is derived from 31 expert-labelled texts in the SDG Classification Benchmark \parencite{SDGBenchmark}. Each raw centroid vector (the mean of unit vectors) is L2-normalised to a unit vector before use, so that downstream dot products equal cosine similarities. The raw centroid norm (prior to normalisation) is retained as a cohesion diagnostic: it equals the mean cosine similarity of member texts to the unit centroid.

\textbf{Validation.} The instrument is validated using the full SDG Classification Benchmark (616 texts, all 17 SDGs) as an independent evaluation set. For each benchmark text, the predicted SDG is the centroid with the highest cosine similarity. The primary evaluation metric is macro-F1 computed over SDGs 1--16 (n = 585), excluding SDG 17 because its centroid was built from a subset of the same benchmark texts (contamination). The instrument achieves macro-F1 = 0.733 on the uncontaminated evaluation, comfortably above the pre-registered pass threshold of 0.50. Per-SDG F1 scores vary: SDG 16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions) achieves the highest F1 (0.857), reflecting its lexically distinctive vocabulary. SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities, F1 = 0.519) and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth, F1 = 0.531) are the lowest-performing, reflecting semantic overlap with SDG 9 and the SDG 1/8/10 cluster respectively. Findings for these SDGs are reported with appropriate caveats. No classifier is trained; the instrument uses no learned parameters and cannot overfit.

\subsection{Coverage Gap Analysis}

For each item in the research and policy corpora, the predicted SDG is the centroid with the highest cosine similarity score (hard assignment). The coverage profile for a corpus is the proportion of items assigned to each SDG.

A critical methodological issue arises for the policy corpus: the three source collections differ dramatically in size (SDGi: 31,941 chunks; curated docs: 8,592 chunks; UNGDC: 6,472 chunks) and the SDGi corpus includes 2,279 individual national reports. A simple chunk-weighted profile would be dominated by the volume of a small number of large documents rather than reflecting the range of policy voices. We therefore compute a \emph{document-weighted} policy coverage profile, in which each of the 2,392 unique source documents contributes equally regardless of chunk count. Each document's SDG assignment is the argmax of its mean chunk score vector. Document-weighted profiles are used as the canonical representation throughout; chunk-weighted profiles are retained as a diagnostic comparison.

The coverage gap for SDG $j$ is defined as:
\[
  \text{CoverageGap}_j = \left|\text{ResearchProportion}_j - \text{PolicyProportion}^{\text{docwt}}_j\right|
\]

\subsection{Semantic Gap Analysis}

The semantic gap measures within-SDG semantic divergence: given that both corpora have texts assigned to SDG $j$, how differently do they discuss it? For each SDG $j$:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Collect all research papers assigned to SDG $j$ (the \emph{research cluster} for $j$).
  \item Collect all policy chunks assigned to SDG $j$, subject to a per-document chunk cap of 50 chunks per source document (the \emph{policy cluster} for $j$, capped). The cap prevents SDSN (up to 3,179 chunks in a single document) from dominating the policy sub-centroid.
  \item Compute the L2-normalised mean embedding of each cluster: $\mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j$ and $\mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j$.
  \item Define: $\text{SemanticSimilarity}_j = \mathbf{c}^{\text{res}}_j \cdot \mathbf{c}^{\text{pol}}_j$ (dot product of unit vectors = cosine similarity).
  \item Define: $\text{SemanticGap}_j = 1 - \text{SemanticSimilarity}_j$.
\end{enumerate}

A semantic gap of 0 would indicate perfectly overlapping semantic directions; a gap approaching 1 would indicate orthogonal framing. Robustness is assessed by replicating the analysis with chunk caps of 20 and 100 per document; rankings were stable across all three caps.

\subsection{Coverage--Semantic Interaction and Bidirectional Asymmetry}

The central structural hypothesis (H25) is that coverage gaps and semantic gaps are correlated across SDGs. We compute Pearson $r$ and Spearman $\rho$ between research coverage proportions and semantic gap scores across all 17 SDGs, and report sensitivity analyses excluding SDG 4 (see Section~\ref{sec:sdg4artefact}).

The directional asymmetry hypothesis (H26) tests whether research engages policy framing less than policy engages research framing. To operationalise this, we build research-side SDG centroids by hard-assigning each paper to its top OSDG centroid and computing the mean embedding of papers assigned to each SDG. Policy chunks are then scored against these research centroids. The mean top-centroid similarity for research papers against OSDG centroids (0.353) is compared to the mean top-centroid similarity for policy chunks against research centroids (0.472).

\subsection{SDG Index Contextualisation}

Gap scores are joined with the Kaggle SDG Index 2022 global mean scores \parencite{Sachs2024} to contextualise findings. We compute Pearson and Spearman correlations between research coverage proportions and SDG Index scores (H21), and between semantic gap scores and SDG Index scores (H22). SDG 17 is excluded from index-based analyses as it is not scored in the SDG Index. This analysis tests whether research neglect and semantic divergence are concentrated on the SDGs where real-world progress is weakest.

\subsection{Key Assumptions and Mitigations}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\textbf{A15 --- OSDG circularity.} The OSDG Community Dataset was annotated using a tool trained on UN-adjacent documents. Since our policy corpus also contains UN documents, OSDG-derived centroids may be calibrated to policy vocabulary, systematically inflating policy cosine similarity scores relative to research scores. We test for this post hoc by comparing mean top-centroid scores: policy chunks score 0.191 higher than research papers (0.544 vs 0.353), exceeding our pre-registered flag threshold of 0.10. This difference is acknowledged throughout the Results and Discussion as a potential calibration bias. Coverage gap and semantic gap findings remain valid as relative comparisons, but absolute score comparisons between corpora should be interpreted cautiously.

\textbf{A19 --- Unit-of-analysis asymmetry.} Research papers are single, focused abstract-length texts (mean $\sim$200 words); policy corpus items are 150-word chunks from documents of highly variable length. Document-weighting (Section 3.6) and per-document chunk caps (Section 3.7) are the primary mitigations.

\textbf{SDG 4 artefact.}
\label{sec:sdg4artefact}
The research coverage profile assigns 22.1\% of papers to SDG 4 (Quality Education). This is unexpectedly high and likely reflects a measurement artefact: machine learning papers frequently use ``learning,'' ``training,'' and ``model'' as core vocabulary, which overlaps with the OSDG education corpus. SDG 4 centroid findings are reported throughout with this caveat; sensitivity analyses excluding SDG 4 are provided for all correlation results.

\textbf{A26 --- SDG 1/8/10 cluster.} SDG centroids for SDGs 1 (No Poverty), 8 (Decent Work), and 10 (Reduced Inequalities) show very high pairwise cosine similarities (0.780--0.887), producing systematic classifier leakage. Individual findings for these three SDGs are reported as a macro-cluster in the discussion.

Two SDGs require specific caveats. SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities, F1 = 0.519) is most frequently confused with SDG 9 (Innovation), whose centroid is its nearest neighbour (cosine similarity 0.716). This null result is itself a substantive finding. It means that the two dimensions of misalignment are orthogonal: high research attention to an SDG does not predict whether research and policy will discuss that SDG similarly or differently. This is visible in the data: SDG 9 (high research coverage, 17.2\%; low semantic gap, 0.201) and SDG 3 (moderate research coverage, 8.2\%; high semantic gap, 0.285) occupy opposite corners of the coverage--semantic gap space. A 2$\times$2 typology of misalignment follows naturally:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{High coverage, low semantic gap} (SDG 9, SDG 11): Research engages the same SDG framing as policy. May reflect genuine responsiveness or shared paradigm. Interpretively complex.
  \item \textbf{High coverage, high semantic gap} (SDG 3, SDG 4, SDG 16): Research addresses the SDG but in a different register. The most structurally problematic configuration.
  \item \textbf{Low coverage, low semantic gap} (SDG 17, SDG 5, SDG 1): Research rarely addresses the SDG, but when it does, it stays close to the policy framing. Limited research effort that is nonetheless aligned.
  \item \textbf{Low coverage, high semantic gap} (SDG 8, SDG 10, SDG 6): Double neglect --- research rarely engages the SDG, and when it does, it diverges from policy framing. The highest-risk configuration for research-policy disconnection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Directional Asymmetry: H26}

The bidirectional asymmetry analysis finds that policy chunks score 0.472 against research-derived SDG centroids, while research papers score 0.353 against OSDG-derived SDG centroids. The gap of 0.119 indicates that policy engages research framing more than research engages policy framing, consistent with H26.

This finding must be interpreted against the A15 calibration flag. The OSDG circularity diagnostic found that policy chunks score 0.191 higher than research papers against OSDG centroids, which is larger than the H26 asymmetry gap of 0.119. This means the asymmetry could be entirely explained by calibration bias: if OSDG centroids are calibrated to policy-adjacent vocabulary, policy chunks will always score higher regardless of genuine directional asymmetry. H26 is therefore reported as providing \emph{partial support} at best --- the direction is consistent with the hypothesis, but the magnitude is within the range attributable to calibration bias. Replication with a domain-neutral embedding space (e.g., a model fine-tuned independently of UN documents) would be needed to confirm the finding.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Two Independent Dimensions of Misalignment}

The headline result of this study is simple: alignment cannot be inferred from topical overlap. Current AI-for-sustainability research is structurally misaligned with SDG policy discourse along two independent dimensions: what it covers, and how it frames what it covers. Coverage gaps and semantic gaps are statistically independent ($r = 0.145$, $p = 0.578$). That null result is not an absence of finding; it is the finding. It means that research-policy mismatch is not one problem with one remedy, but two separable problems that require different diagnostics and different interventions.

The structural implication is immediate. Increasing research attention to a neglected SDG will not automatically produce semantic convergence with policy discourse on that SDG. Conversely, reducing semantic distance within an SDG does not require equalising the distribution of research attention across SDGs. What looks like alignment in a keyword or topic-count sense can therefore be false reassurance: two communities may appear to occupy the same policy space while still talking past one another.

This interpretation is consistent with the epistemic communities framework \parencite{Haas1992}: research and policy communities can share causal beliefs about a domain (captured in topical overlap) while operating at different levels of abstraction, on different time horizons, and with different institutional mandates. The Mode 1/2 knowledge production distinction \parencite{Gibbons1994} reinforces this: supply-driven research agendas generate SDG-coverage patterns driven by funding, tractability, and disciplinary norms, while the semantic register of research discourse is governed by academic communication conventions that differ structurally from policy speech acts. Both gaps are therefore endogenous to the organisation of knowledge production, not accidental noise around an otherwise aligned system.

\subsection{The SDG 9 Paradox: Shared Vocabulary or Shared Blind Spot?}

SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure) presents the most interpretively complex finding. It receives the second-highest research coverage (17.2\% of papers) and one of the smallest semantic gaps (0.201), suggesting both corpora discuss innovation and infrastructure in similar terms. On a surface reading, this is the closest approximation to research-policy alignment in the dataset.

However, this result is consistent with two interpretations of opposite valence. Under the \emph{genuine responsiveness} interpretation, AI research has successfully engaged with the innovation and infrastructure SDG because both communities share a ``techno-innovation'' vocabulary that generates natural semantic convergence. Under the \emph{shared blind spot} interpretation \parencite[H29]{Toney2024}, both corpora are embedded in an institutional culture that valorises technological progress, and what appears as alignment is actually convergent fixation on the same paradigm rather than responsive engagement. The SDG Index score for SDG 9 (51.77, the lowest of all 16 scored SDGs in 2022) is notable in this context: despite high research attention and apparent semantic alignment, SDG 9 real-world progress is worse than most other SDGs. This is consistent with the shared blind spot interpretation: both research and policy address the innovation discourse of SDG 9 while the actual infrastructure access goals --- particularly in the Global South --- remain substantially unmet. The two communities may be aligned on the language of technology but divergent on the urgency of equitable access.

\subsection{Productive and Problematic Misalignment}

The 2$\times$2 typology introduced in Section~\ref{sec:results} matters because it converts the independence result into a practical diagnostic. Once coverage and semantic distance are treated as separate axes, not a single continuum, different forms of misalignment become analytically and institutionally distinguishable.

\textbf{Problematic misalignment} is most acute in the high-coverage, high-semantic-gap quadrant (SDGs 3, 4, 16). For SDG 3, research concentrates on AI-assisted diagnostics, drug discovery, and genomics --- technically impressive but addressing primarily the clinical problems of well-resourced healthcare systems. Policy discourse frames SDG 3 around universal health coverage, maternal mortality, infectious disease control, and access in low-income settings \parencite{SioumalasChristodoulou2025}. The semantic gap (0.285) quantifies this divergence: research and policy on global health are not merely at different levels of abstraction but in different conversations. This is consistent with the Mode 1/2 framework --- supply-driven health AI research addresses the technically tractable frontier, not the access frontier that policy identifies as the priority.

For SDG 16, research papers concentrate on governance AI, algorithmic accountability, and bias in legal systems, while policy discourse addresses democratic institution-building, rule of law, and conflict resolution --- a more fundamental divide. The high F1 score for SDG 16 centroid (0.857) confirms that this gap is not a classifier artefact.

\textbf{Potentially productive misalignment} may characterise some research-dominant low-semantic-gap cases, where research is pursuing SDG-relevant problems at greater technical depth than policy has yet articulated. However, we are unable to confirm this without qualitative inspection of the specific research-policy pairs within each SDG --- an important direction for future work.

\textbf{Double neglect} (low coverage, high semantic gap: SDG 8, SDG 10) is the highest-risk configuration. Research rarely engages Decent Work and Economic Growth or Reduced Inequalities, and when it does, the framing is semantically distant from policy. The 1/8/10 cluster (SDGs 1, 8, 10 have near-collinear centroids, similarities 0.780--0.887) means these findings should be read as a group: the full complex of poverty, labour, and inequality is simultaneously under-researched and semantically divergent from policy when it does appear.

\subsection{The SDG 13 Anomaly}

SDG 13 (Climate Action) produces a finding that cuts against simple narratives. It has the largest policy-dominated coverage gap (policy 36.1\% vs research 5.0\%), indicating that policy discourse is overwhelmingly oriented toward climate while research is not. Yet SDG 13 also shows a moderate semantic gap (0.219, slightly below the mean), and the SDG Index score of 82.98 is the highest of all scored SDGs, driven by climate commitment signalling.

This pattern is internally consistent: the SDG 13 centroid is close to the SDG 17 centroid (cosine similarity 0.860, the highest pair in the centroid similarity matrix), reflecting that climate discourse and partnership discourse are semantically intertwined in the OSDG labelling corpus. Policy documents that discuss climate action and international climate partnerships jointly may be labelled under SDG 13 or SDG 17, inflating both estimates. The coverage gap for SDG 13 and SDG 17 combined (36.1\% + 34.9\% = 71.0\% of policy documents) is more informative than either figure alone.

For research, the 5.0\% assignment to SDG 13 is lower than bibliometric studies suggest (prior work consistently identifies climate action as a major AI research theme). This discrepancy likely reflects differences in classification method: keyword-based studies classify any paper mentioning ``climate'' as SDG 13, while our embedding-based approach assigns papers to the nearest centroid, so papers combining climate methods with innovation framing may be absorbed into the SDG 9 cluster. This illustrates the methodological sensitivity documented by \textcite{Armitage2020}: embedding-based classification produces systematically different distributions than keyword methods, and neither is straightforwardly superior.

\subsection{Implications}

Three implications follow from the combined findings, and each pushes against the common habit of treating topical mention as evidence of alignment.

\textbf{Funding allocation.} The double-neglect quadrant (SDG 8, SDG 10) represents both the most under-researched SDGs and the most semantically divergent. Redirecting even modest research funding toward AI approaches to decent work and reduced inequalities, with explicit attention to policy framing, would address both dimensions of misalignment simultaneously.

\textbf{SDG-aware research communication.} The finding that coverage and semantic gaps are independent suggests that simply increasing research output on neglected SDGs is insufficient; research communication norms need to change as well. Publication incentives that reward explicit SDG engagement (as evidenced by better alignment among explicitly SDG-framed publications; cf.\ \textcite{Nedungadi2024}) may be more effective than topic-level funding mandates.

\textbf{Bidirectional framing for AI governance.} H26 (with caveats) and the semantic gap analysis suggest that policy documents engage research framing somewhat more than research engages policy framing. This asymmetry, if replicated with better-calibrated instruments, would suggest that AI governance frameworks have been more responsive to research outputs than research has been to governance priorities --- an important finding for AI governance scholarship.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound the conclusions.

\textbf{Topical overlap is not substantive alignment.} The study measures semantic co-location in embedding space, not whether research and policy are substantively addressing the same problems in compatible ways. High cosine similarity reflects shared vocabulary, not shared agenda. All findings must be read with this constraint.

\textbf{Preliminary draft status.} This manuscript was compiled on \DraftCompiledAt, but the empirical snapshot discussed throughout is pegged to \AnalysisSnapshotDate, approximately five months before the submission deadline of 1 September 2026. As of that snapshot date, the OpenAlex retrieval process was still in progress and only approximately half of the planned query set had completed. All research-side counts, coverage estimates, and downstream inferential results are therefore provisional and subject to change.

\textbf{A15 calibration bias.} The OSDG circularity (A15) means policy scores against OSDG centroids are likely inflated. Coverage proportions are less affected than absolute scores, but the finding warrants replication with a calibration-neutral instrument.

\textbf{SDG 4 artefact.} The 22.1\% research assignment to SDG 4 likely overstates genuine education research. All findings involving the research-side SDG 4 profile should be treated with corresponding uncertainty.

\textbf{Corpus scope.} The policy corpus, while broad, represents international institutional discourse (UN, OECD, national governments). Domestic implementation plans, sub-national policies, and non-English-language policy documents are not represented. The research corpus is also English-language. Both biases likely understate the perspectives of the Global South.

\textbf{Cross-sectional design.} The study provides a snapshot of the 2018--2025 research period against a policy corpus assembled over a similar timeframe. It cannot address whether alignment is improving or deteriorating, or whether specific policy events (e.g., the EU AI Act 2024, IPCC AR6 2022) have shifted research framing.

\textbf{Single embedding model.} Using a single general-purpose encoder means the findings are model-specific. A domain-adapted model (e.g., fine-tuned on sustainability text) might produce different cluster structures and coverage profiles.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper revisits an old problem with a new instrument. Policy scholars have long argued that research and policy often fail to meet one another on equal terms. What recent semantic tools add is not the discovery that mismatch exists, but the ability to measure one important dimension of it directly, across thousands of texts, instead of describing it impressionistically. The main result is clear: alignment cannot be inferred from topical overlap.

Current AI-for-sustainability research is structurally misaligned with SDG policy discourse along two independent dimensions. The first is coverage: policy discourse is oriented toward climate governance (SDG 13, 36\%), international partnerships (SDG 17, 35\%), and institutional frameworks (SDG 16, 20\%), while research is oriented toward technical innovation (SDG 9, 17\%) and, subject to the artefact caveat, educational or learning methodology (SDG 4, 22\%). The second is semantic divergence: even when research and policy occupy the same SDG, they often do not frame that SDG in commensurate terms. SDG 8 (Decent Work), SDG 3 (Good Health), and SDG 16 (Governance) show the largest within-SDG divergence, indicating not mere imbalance of attention but parallel monologues within shared labels.

The decisive structural finding is that these two dimensions are independent ($r = 0.145$, $p = 0.578$). That matters because it overturns a common inference in AI-for-SDG mapping: research cannot be treated as aligned with policy simply because it appears to cover the same goals. Coverage is necessary, but it is not enough. What looks like alignment under keyword or topic-count approaches may in fact be shallow alignment, or false alignment altogether.

The 2$\times$2 typology that follows from this result provides a more useful diagnostic than the simple research-neglect narrative. The highest-risk configuration is double neglect (low coverage + high semantic gap: SDGs 8 and 10); the most interpretively complex is the high-coverage, low-semantic-gap case of SDG 9, where shared vocabulary may conceal a shared blind spot about access and equity. This is why the independence result scales beyond the immediate case: it offers a general way to think about mismatch between knowledge systems, not just between AI research and SDG policy.

Several findings call for direct follow-up. The SDG 4 artefact should be resolved by qualitative inspection or a fine-tuned classifier. The A15 calibration bias should be addressed by replicating with a domain-neutral encoder. The temporal dimension --- whether the gaps are narrowing as the SDG framework matures --- can be addressed with the year metadata available in the OpenAlex corpus. Most importantly, the 2$\times$2 typology sketched here requires qualitative operationalisation of the productive/problematic distinction \parencite{Toney2024}, which this study has measured but not fully interpreted. Extending the analysis to the 169 individual SDG targets (using the AURORA dataset), and disaggregating the policy corpus by country income level (H34), would provide finer-grained evidence about where the misalignment problem is most acute and for whom.

The headline message for research governance is therefore straightforward. Knowing which SDGs research covers is necessary but not sufficient. Measuring whether research and policy mean compatible things when they invoke those SDGs is a distinct diagnostic, and an equally important one. If topical overlap is mistaken for alignment, misalignment will be systematically underestimated. For this reason, the present manuscript should be read as a preliminary analytical draft rather than a final settled account: as of \AnalysisSnapshotDate, the research corpus was still being assembled, and the reported findings remain subject to revision.
